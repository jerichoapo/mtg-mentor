# Who's the Beatdown?

**Mike Flores — The Dojo, 1999 (reprinted by StarCityGames)**

> Why it's in the list: the most cited strategy article ever written. In every matchup one deck is
> the aggressor and one is the control, determined by the decks and not by your preferences.
> Misassigning your role is a self-inflicted loss.

---

## Editor's note on provenance

The StarCityGames reprint carries a preface worth keeping. The Dojo was the first Magic website, and
it published many of the game's most fundamental principles. Almost all strategic theory traces back
to its writers. The Dojo went out of business in 2000 because of financial trouble, and in a
last-ditch effort to save the four years of accumulated writing, the editor asked the community to
archive the articles. StarCityGames reprints the best of them, adding links to clarify older cards
that newer players won't recognize.

## The thesis

The most common mistake Flores sees in tournament Magic is subtle and disastrous: **misassigning who
is the beatdown deck and who is the control deck** in a similar-deck-versus-similar-deck matchup. The
player who misassigns himself is inevitably the loser.

The structural claim underneath it: in similar-deck-versus-similar-deck matchups, unless the decks
are genuinely symmetrical (a true mirror), **one deck has to play the role of beatdown and the other
has to play the role of control.** This becomes a serious dilemma when, for instance, both players
are on aggressive decks. Someone still has to be the control deck.

## Example 1: the aggro deck that had to be control

At a 1.x PTQ in Washington, D.C., Flores's teammate Al Tran was playing for a Top 8 slot against
Sligh. Al was on Lan D. Ho's White Weenie/Jank deck, normally an aggressive deck, but not against
Sligh.

The match was 1-1 and the third game would decide Top 8.

- Al's opponent went first and played a **Jackal Pup**. Al's hand held two **Cursed Scroll**, two
  **Swords to Plowshares**, an **Honorable Passage**, and some land. Al chose not to Plow the Pup,
  taking 2 on the first attack.
- The opponent played a second Pup. Al again declined to Plow, waiting on Scroll mana or a Lightning
  Bolt.
- On his own second turn Al played a land and a Cursed Scroll, leaving only one land up.
- On the opponent's third turn: another Mountain, then **Ball Lightning**. Al was forced to Plow the
  Ball. He took control over the next few turns but died to Bolts.

**The diagnosis.** Al was piloting a beatdown deck and wanted to deal damage with his own creatures.
But in this matchup he had to be the control deck. Sligh is much faster than Jank, so Jank's path to
victory is stifling Sligh's early speed with removal and then locking down the midgame with Cursed
Scroll. Because Sligh also has Cursed Scroll, and more Bolts than Jank, **the only way Jank wins is
to have a decent life total when it starts deploying its own threats.**

The counting: it ostensibly hurts the race to give the Sligh player four extra life by killing the
Jackal Pups. But Al ended up giving him six more life from the Ball Lightning anyway, *and* took at
least eight from the Pups before he controlled them. It would have profited him far more to Plow the
Pups, Passage the Ball, and enter the midgame at twenty life as he began threatening with his own
Paladins and Priests.

**This is the load-bearing example of the article.** The removal was in hand the whole time. Hoarding
it was the loss.

## Example 2: two control decks, and the one who guessed wrong

At the same PTQ, Flores was playing High Tide against CounterSliver, normally a dangerous matchup for
him. The opponent ran the usual Slivers, Worship, and permission, plus Cursed Scroll. **He made the
mistake of thinking he was the control deck.**

He played a turn-2 Crystalline Sliver, then two turns later a Worship, and Flores Stroked him out.
(Flores had killed him in game one with Palinchron, and because he had mostly shown Disrupts, Force
Spikes, and card drawing, the opponent may have thought he was more creature-heavy.)

**The diagnosis.** It doesn't matter what he thought. Flores was clearly the control deck:

- comparable or greater permission
- where the opponent had Slivers, Flores had card drawing and deck manipulation
- where the opponent had dual lands, Flores had **Thawing Glaciers**, which guaranteed he'd never
  miss a land drop
- Flores had already countered a couple of Brainstorms with Disrupt

Which means **Flores wins the long game every single time.**

The opponent's job was therefore to kill Flores before Flores killed him. The normal formula is to
play decent-sized Slivers, two power or more, attack every turn, and leave mana open to counter
whatever the opposing blue deck does that might be threatening, whether a Wrath of God, an Engineered
Plague, or here the High Tide finishing combo. His two errors: he should have threatened more
aggressively, since a single Crystalline Sliver gives Flores many turns of Thawing and drawing; and
**tapping out is the death knell** — Flores didn't even have to spend a Turnabout on him.

## The three diagnostic questions

In similar-deck-versus-similar-deck matchups, Flores gives three things to look at to determine role:

1. **Who has more damage?** Usually he has to be the beatdown deck.
2. **Who has more removal?** Usually he has to be the control deck.
3. **Who has more permission and card drawing?** Almost always he has to be the control deck.

And the resulting job description:

- **If you are the beatdown deck, you have to kill your opponent faster than he can kill you.**
- **If you are the control deck, you have to weather the early beatdown and get into a position where
  you can gain card advantage.**

## Example 3: doing it right — Price vs. Pacifico, 1998 U.S. Nationals Top 8

A Sligh mirror where the roles were correctly assigned. On the surface the two decks look very
similar, but there are major design differences.

**Dave Price's deck** ran more Cursed Scrolls than Pacifico's, plus **Hammer of Bogardan** and
**Fireslinger**. His only real beatdown was Jackal Pup and Ball Lightning; the rest of the deck was
control and utility oriented.

**Pacifico's deck** was much more damage-oriented, built around attacking and celerity creatures
rather than dedicated removal. Beyond Jackal Pup and Ball Lightning he had **Goblin Vandal**, **Mogg
Flunkies**, **Suq'Ata Lancer**, and **Viashino Sandstalker**. He had no Fireslinger and no Hammer,
and ran only three Cursed Scrolls.

Price's deck could get a quick start, but in this matchup **it was the control deck**, set up for the
long game. In one duel Price simply played lands and Scrolls and did very little else. He removed
Pacifico's creatures with blocks or Bolts, then Scroll-locked him, gained a little card advantage,
and finished the game.

Had Price tried to race, he might not have won. **When two players blindly throw creatures at each
other, the one with more damage-oriented cards wins the race.** Flores adds the aside that we expect
good Sligh play from the King of Red.

## Example 4: the hard case — Suicide Black vs. Sligh

Two very fast beatdown decks. Sligh invariably wins. Work the questions:

**Which deck has more damage?** Suicide Black. It runs many high power-to-cost creatures such as
**Carnophage**, **Sarcomancy**, and sometimes **Flesh Reaver**, plus occasionally **Hatred**. It
damages even itself.

**Which deck has more removal?** Sligh. If Suicide Black runs Cursed Scrolls, Sligh can match them,
and Sligh has not just weenies but Bolts.

Sligh goldfishes around turn 4; Suicide Black can goldfish turn 2 or 3 depending on version and
Ritual draw. So clearly **Suicide Black has to be the beatdown and Sligh has to be the control deck.**

**But here is the twist that makes the article more than a formula.** Suicide Black *can't afford* to
be the beatdown deck. It can't deploy many of its clocks, especially Sarcomancy or Flesh Reaver,
because Sligh has so many Bolts. It can almost never cast Hatred for fear of an auto-loss to
Incinerate. So if it can't really beat down, **it has to try to be the control deck** — and anyone
who has watched this matchup with a decent Sligh draw knows how well control-oriented Suicide Black
turns out.

**The sideboard resolution.** After sideboarding, Suicide Black has traditionally done much better.
By cutting a lot of its self-damaging cards for creature removal and lifegain, it can play the
control role more adequately and has a much improved, if not great, chance of winning.

## The line

> **Misassignment of Role = Game Loss.**

## Notes for use

**The thing most summaries get wrong.** The article is usually reduced to "figure out who's the
beatdown." The harder and more interesting content is in Example 4: the role assignment is determined
by the decks, but a deck can be *assigned* the beatdown role and still be unable to *execute* it,
which forces it into a role it's badly built for. That's the case where sideboarding, not play
skill, is the fix.

**The failure mode is a hand you already hold.** In Example 1 the removal was in hand from turn one.
The loss came from saving it. Aggressive players hoard removal for "real" threats; the role
assignment tells you the Jackal Pups *were* the real threat.

**Tapping out.** Example 2 gives the cleanest tell in the article: a deck trying to be the control
deck that taps out on its own turn has misassigned itself, or is about to lose anyway.

**Where this goes next.** Flores rewrote this himself thirteen years later into eight principles with
modern cards. Read that immediately after. Zvi's "Who's the Beatdown II: Multitasking" adds
**Inevitability**, the question of which deck wins a genuinely long game, which is the missing
variable in the three questions above.
