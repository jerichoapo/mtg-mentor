# Clear the Land and the Fundamental Turn

**Zvi Mowshowitz — The Dojo (reprinted by StarCityGames, stamped December 31, 2000)**

> Why it's in the list: assign every deck a number — the turn it actually wins. Then compare it to the
> room. If your deck's fundamental turn is six and the format's is four, you don't have a deck, you have
> a goldfish. The fastest way to evaluate a brew before you build it.

## The setup: a reader-request column about a bad card

The column began as a response to reader suggestions about which card to cover next. **Two suggestions
dominated: Trade Routes and Clear the Land.** Zvi was even sent several decklists abusing Clear the Land.

> **The problem is, Clear the Land isn't a very good card.**

**And he generalizes immediately.** Like cards that only damage your opponent, Clear the Land belongs to
**another group of cards that generally aren't very good: cards that require mana to cast and generate
more mana in the long term.**

## The arithmetic on Clear the Land

**What do you actually get?** At best, **five tapped lands.**

**The deckbuilding constraint:** realistically the maximum land count is around **thirty-six**. After
that you need **four slots for Exploration and four for Clear the Land, leaving only sixteen for
everything else.**

**The yield:**

- **With 36 lands, unstacked: an average of three lands per Clear the Land.**
- **Stacked with Brainstorm** — the most realistic way to set up a third-turn Clear the Land —
  **3.8 lands.**

**What your opponent gets:** they average around **24 lands** and put **two** of theirs into play.

**Three things that blunt the advantage:**

1. **All these lands come into play tapped.**
2. **Type II has no lands you really want to put into play**, because Wizards had moved away from putting
   costs on playing lands.
3. **He gets to use his mana first.**

**So the net:** you get **three to four mana on each future turn** from Clear the Land, **your opponent
gets two.**

### Why it looks fine on paper

> **This doesn't immediately seem all that bad.** You get a card more than he does on average, two if you
> do something as simple as Brainstorm. **You can have better lands than he does** — Rishadan Ports, Dust
> Bowls, man lands — **while he just gets two extra basics. You can use a lot of mana because you
> designed your deck that way, while many opponents won't be able to.**

**So you build one of these two decks:**

4 Treetop Village · 4 Faerie Conclave · 4 Rishadan Port · 4 Dust Bowl · 4 Remote Isle ·
4 Blasted Landscape · 8 Forest · 4 Island · 4 Brainstorm · 4 Natural Affinity · 4 Exploration ·
4 Clear the Land · 4 Greener Pastures · 4 Trade Routes

14 Forest · 13 Island · 4 Rishadan Port · 4 Vernal Bloom · 4 Exploration · 4 Clear the Land ·

</details>

> **But either way, the decks don't work out. Why?**

## Reason one: the all-land problem

> **The decks are going to have an all-land problem, of course. Too often they'll have nothing to do.**

Drawing all land isn't always that bad for the first deck **if it also has an Exploration or Trade
Routes** — but **if it doesn't, or the relevant card is countered or removed, you're in real trouble.**

> **This is the first problem with decks that use mana to get mana: they end up having to play too much
> mana. That's not very surprising.**

**The standard he holds them to:**

> **Ideally your deck ends up like Kai's from Worlds: every other spell in your deck can win you the
> game, so your deck becomes somewhat inconsistent and has to mulligan more than you'd like, but makes up
> for it by being amazing with the right draw.**

> **These decks are probably not amazing enough with that right draw.**

---

## Reason two: the Fundamental Turn

**The concept the article is remembered for, and Zvi's stated reason for writing it down:**

> **This one allows me to know instantly, without taking even a test draw, that the decks are
> unworkable.**

### The definition

> **Whenever I make a deck, I assign it a Fundamental Turn.**

| Deck type | Its Fundamental Turn |
|---|---|
| **Beatdown or combo** | **The turn you kill your opponent.** An easy concept, one number. |
| **Control** | Each aspect can be said to have one, but the important one is **the turn in which the deck's strategy begins to work and you make up for any early disadvantage.** |

**His examples:**

- **An old-style U/W control deck: FT 4** — when it could cast **Wrath of God** and stabilize the board.
- **A monoblue deck from UBC: FT 5** — when it could cast **Treachery** and **Morphling**. **The game may
  not be over then, but that's when you win it.**

**The general rule:** **any turn in which you do something that ensures you will win the game becomes the
FT.**

**And the fractional case, which shows the concept's flexibility:** a Bargain deck that lands **Defense
Grid on turn 1 half the time and turn 2 the other half has an anti-control FT of 1.5 after sideboarding**
— assuming the control deck can't remove the Grid without giving Bargain enough time to win.

## The FT in practice: Rec/Sur vs. High Tide

**Zvi's worked example, and the best part of the article — it shows the concept used as a design tool
rather than a description.**

**The problem:** testing for the NY4 qualifiers, he needed Rec/Sur to beat High Tide. This was before
Frantic Search or Avalanche Riders.

**The key fact:**

> **High Tide had a very stable Fundamental Turn: turn 4.** Most of the time it would win on turn 4 unless
> disrupted. **Most of the time it would be unable to win on turn 3, and wouldn't try anyway unless it
> knew it needed to.**

**Two ways to buy turns:**

- **A Duress generally bought you a turn**, and about half the time it did **even if it was countered**,
  because the opponent couldn't use the same mana to Impulse or Intuition. **Force of Will was
  different.**
- **Each counterspell the Tide deck wanted to work around generally cost it another turn.**

### How he used the information

> **I knew my deck would have to hit the Tide deck on turn three and then again on turn four whenever I
> went second, and often also on turn one or two in order to get rid of Force of Will.**

> **But I also knew I couldn't keep up that pressure forever**, so I'd need something to cripple him while
> Rec/Sur's slow clock worked, **if I didn't draw both Recur and Survival.**

**The solution, built backwards from the FT:**

> **Five Red Elemental Blast, four Boil, two Thrull Surgeon, four Duress.**

**How the pieces interlocked:**

> **The Surgeons allowed me to hit the Tide player's hand for four mana, sometimes spread over two turns
> while I saved mana for a blast. Once they'd been Duressed and then Surgeoned twice, a Boil took them out
> of the game, and Ashen Ghouls or the combo killed them.**

> **If I didn't get Survival, I forced them to work around enough counters and disruption to buy myself
> enough time to use my alternate plans. My deck was suddenly hugely disruptive at exactly the right
> time.**

**His honest result:** **game one was really bad, so the deck was about 55–60% for the match.**

### When the FT moves

**This is the payoff of the whole example:**

> **Then consider what happens when the FT changes. Tide got Frantic Search, and its fundamental turn
> became 3.**

> **A strategy that gave the Tide deck three turns and then demolished it wasn't good enough anymore: you
> couldn't tap out before the Tide deck's third turn.** That meant less disruption later.

> **The plan no longer worked, and Rec/Sur no longer had a practical way to defeat Tide.**

**One card moved the number by one, and an entire matchup plan became unworkable.**

## Back to Clear the Land

**Now the verdict is instant.**

**Best case for the land deck:** turn 1 Exploration, turn 2 Clear the Land, turn 3 attack for 6, turn 4
Natural Affinity and attack for ten, **turn 5 win.**

**The combo version** can win turn 3 on a great draw, **but realistically turn 4.**

> **Turn four would normally be okay in today's Type II. But on turn 2 you give your opponent two extra
> lands. What does that do to your opponent's Fundamental Turn?**

**The damage, quantified:**

| Opponent type | Effect of the two free lands |
|---|---|
| **General** | **Speeds up their FT by one turn** |
| **Beatdown already summoning part of the kill** | **About half a turn** |
| **Some control decks** | **Actually speeds it up by two turns** |

> **Type II in general has a fundamental turn of 4 right now, which makes a deck with an FT of 4 that
> speeds up its opponent by a turn unacceptable.**

**Zvi's own assessment of the explanation:**

> **That was a very long-winded explanation of something very intuitive, obvious and simple. But I use it
> a lot, so I felt I needed to pin it down.**

## The closing tease

> **Type II as a whole is Fundamental Turn 4. There is a deck — or possibly two or more variants with the
> same key card — that has Fundamental Turn 3. That's the deck to play. As for what it is, I'll give you
> one guess.**

He signs off as **"President, The Alliance to Ban Yawgmoth's Bargain (now in its second Pro Tour
season!)"**

## Notes for use

- **The concept in one line:** assign every deck the turn it actually wins, then compare that number to
  the format's. It converts a vague question ("is my deck fast enough?") into arithmetic you can do
  before building.
- **The definition is subtler than the popular version.** For control decks the FT is **not** the turn you
  win, it's **the turn your strategy starts working and you erase your early deficit.** That's why a U/W
  deck's FT is 4 (Wrath) even though the game runs much longer.
- **Fractional and matchup-specific FTs are allowed** — the Defense Grid example gives 1.5, and it's an
  *anti-control* FT specifically. The number is a property of a deck **in a matchup**, not a fixed
  attribute.
- **The killer application is the second-order one:** it isn't enough that your FT beats the format's, you
  also have to ask **what your deck does to your opponent's FT.** Clear the Land fails not because turn 4
  is slow but because it hands the opponent two lands and accelerates *them*. Any card that ramps
  symmetrically, or that costs you tempo, has this problem.
- **The Frantic Search moment is the most instructive detail in the article.** A single printing moved
  High Tide from FT 4 to FT 3 and invalidated an entire disruption plan. When evaluating a new set, ask
  which decks' FTs moved.
- **"Cards that require mana to cast and generate more mana in the long term"** is a durable warning about
  a whole card class, and it's the same argument Reid Duke makes about **investment** twenty years later
  (`block-1-the-spine/22-investment-2015-03-02.md`): a delayed payoff is discounted by how fast the
  format is.
- **Cross-reference:** Flores lists this at **#7** in his countdown and describes it exactly this way —
  "this kills on turn six? Then you'd better be able to win by turn four in this room." See
  `block-0-orientation/04-shoulders-of-giants-2013-08-06.md`. The fundamental turn is also the number
  underneath Duke's treatment of **linear strategies** (installment 07) and **inevitability**
  (installment 16), and it's a close cousin of Flores's rule that when two decks share an end game, the
  faster one is favored.
