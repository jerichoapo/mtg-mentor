# Eight Core Principles of "Who's the Beatdown?"

**Mike Flores — StarCityGames, March 16, 2012**

> Why it's in the list: Flores rewriting his own article thirteen years later at a reader's
> challenge, distilling it into eight principles and re-illustrating them with cards you can actually
> picture.

---

## How it came about

The article opens on a joke about reader reaction to the original: "WTF is a Cursed Scroll?"

SCG editor Lauren Lee had laid out fourteen topics for future exploration. The second was a challenge
of, in Flores's words, heretofore untold degree of difficulty: rewrite "Who's the Beatdown?" using
modern Standard examples.

Flores riffs on what the most iconic Magic article of all time would be if you distilled it to one
pick (Timmy, Johnny, and Spike), and what the best of the *strategy* articles would be (Magic: The
Intangibles, Information Cascades, or How to Win a PTQ). Kidding aside, he concedes the
most-referenced seminal strategy article is "Who's the Beatdown?" from 1999, and calls it a very
good and dense article.

Rather than recreate its narratives in a contrived 2012 voice, he distills it into eight points.

He also flags the article's known limitation: he has often joked that "Who's the Beatdown?" wouldn't
have met SCG's minimum word count. It touches on and implies a number of profound truths but isn't
exhaustive about them. Zvi Mowshowitz's "Who's the Beatdown II: Multitasking" coined **inevitability**
as an extrapolation of principle #4.

---

## The eight principles

1. There Are Two Kinds of Decks and Their Positions Are Fluid
2. Greed Must Be Balanced Against Speed
3. Thou Shalt Play Differently Going First and Going Second
4. Inevitability + A General Rule
5. All War Is Based on Deception
6. Identify Position; Take Action
7. One Definition of a Bad Matchup
8. Sideboarding Can Be Used to Eliminate Dead Weight

---

## 1. There are two kinds of decks and their positions are fluid

The core principle: rather than handle decks by their macro-identities (beatdown, control, combo, and
their eight or so sub-genres), the more useful model reduces them to **two**: beatdown and control.

This was more controversial in 1999 than it reads now. What do you mean there's no such thing as a
combo deck? PV says aggro-control is the best archetype, how can there be no such thing?

The argument is that macro distinctions are useless *in context*. The real puzzle is contextual
identity — Flores frames it as the Vorlon and Shadow questions, "Who are you?" and "What do you
want?" Are you:

1. the guy trying to kill the other guy, or
2. the guy trying to get card advantage

The answer doesn't dictate your position everywhere and for all time, or your identity as a player.
It dictates how you should conduct yourself in the game at hand.

### The Joey Pasco example

Joey loves Islands and self-identifies as a Blue Mage. Building Constructed, he loves Mana Leak and
Snapcaster Mage, but might also play reanimation spells, planeswalkers, and combo kills.
Superficially, or if he'd never read the article, he might consistently position himself as "the
control" because there are Islands in front of him.

Now put Joey on **Sam Black's Esper Spirits** against **G/R Wolf Run Ramp**.

Traditionally, and certainly in 1999, Mountains and Forests together would be default-considered the
beatdown against anything-Islands as the control. But here, **Spirits is less likely to succeed
playing the card-advantage control game than playing the aggressor.**

Why Spirits is the beatdown:

- Delver can drop three power on turn 1.
- It has permission, but that permission is better for holding tempo, protecting a clock, or denying
  the opponent's massive mana expenditure than as a control spell buying time for card advantage.
- It has some board control, and even specialized cards like Revoke Existence and Divine Offering,
  but the modal card **Vapor Snag is good precisely because it pokes the opponent for one** — another
  way to aggressively gobble up time as a resource.

Why Ramp is the control: it plays **Whipflare**, a cheap red Wrath. It searches its library like a
blue deck and grabs two and three cards at a time with **Green Sun's Zenith** or **Primeval Titan**
for bulk card advantage.

Flores then paints the failure: imagine Delver holding back Mana Leak mana from turn 2, Draw-Go
style, trying to grind out Wolf Run with Lingering Souls and Moorland Haunt, pretending Vapor Snag,
Gut Shot, and Dismember are Swords to Plowshares, and paying full retail for Gitaxian Probe because
control must preserve its life total. Does that look like a game Joey wins?

### The fluidity, demonstrated

The same decks change position depending on the opponent.

**Spirits vs. Wolf Run.** The successful Spirits deck exploits mana inefficiencies but picks its
spot. It must stick a threat and ride it, typically while adding more power to the board, to win
before the opponent reaches successful Titan range. The early battle is mana exchanges, Delvers
against Galvanic Blasts. The middle turns pose the question: does the impending Titan take control,
or does Spirits explode with an Overrun-like Drogskol Captain turn? And if a Titan does stick, does a
topdecked Dungeon Geists steal it back (a control-like effect that also adds an offensive, evasive
body), or does the status quo of card power, card advantage, and inevitability take it?

**Wolf Run vs. B/U Control.** Wolf Run is no longer the control. The opponent is less blue in a
literal sense, but Wolf Run loses the control position contextually. **Galvanic Blast goes from
awesome to awful.** Titans go from inevitable, which forced Spirits to cut them off, invalidate them,
or race them, to being *test spells*: how many of these in a row can you answer, because it's the
last fatty that kills you.

**Spirits vs. a real Delver deck.** Spirits shifts from aggressor to control. Lingering Souls becomes
a source of grinding card advantage, and its job is to trade with Delvers with value or buy time.
Drogskol Captain is still good, but the size it produces is less about swinging a big turn and more
about that 1/1 Spirit becoming a 2/2 to make combat unproductive for the opponent.

**The conclusion:** there are only two kinds of decks, but because positions are fluid, **you may not
know which one you are until the game starts.**

## 2. Greed must be balanced against speed

This is the Al Tran story from the original. Flores names the crime precisely: **he chose greed over
speed.** Rather than use mana immediately to deal with threats, he waited to maximize his cards'
effects in terms of life swing, misassigning himself as beatdown even though his opponent, on the
play, was laying two-power creatures for one mana.

The 2012 equivalent: holding a Day of Judgment to defend against a turn-1 Stromkirk Noble into
turn-2 Stormblood Berserker when you have a perfectly good **Galvanic Blast** and **Incinerate** in
hand.

You might badly want a two-for-one out of the Day of Judgment, and want to hold your burn to deal a
greedy seven to the opponent's face. In the process you take between six and twelve damage depending
on play or draw. And if you miss your fourth land drop? Nineteen.

Flores's line on it: Mike Long once asked what any of us wanted out of Magic other than a two-for-one.
His answer — how about a fifth-turn untap with more than ten life.

The balancing caveat: the control role does typically instruct you to play in favor of card
advantage. It isn't that you must always act *now*, but that you balance decisions with attention to
time and long-term strategy rather than overbearing deference to card advantage.

**The general guideline from the original:**

> "When two decks have the same end game, generally, the faster one will be favored."

This is related to but not identical to inevitability. If you know both players plan to close the
damage gap and then finish with a big Devil's Play, you want one of two things:

1. A significant life advantage at the point Devil's Play becomes a relevant anti-opponent card.
2. A way to get Devil's Play online faster, or with more available mana, especially if you're the
   victim of early beatdown.

## 3. Thou shalt play differently going first and going second

The original doesn't go deep here and there's no general rule, but intuitively most players know it.

The example is a Mono Red mirror. **On the play** you have more incentive to be the aggressor, so you
can exploit a serendipitous curve or an opponent's stumble. **On the draw** you have natural card
advantage, since going second got you a free card, so you have the liberty to use your mana to
prevent damage instead.

## 4. Inevitability, plus a general rule

Inevitability doesn't get its name until Zvi's "Who's the Beatdown II: Multitasking," but its
existence is implied in the original.

**Zvi's definition, quoted:**

> A player is said to have inevitability if and only if from the current position they will win a
> long game. A player is said to have inevitability in a matchup if and only if they have
> inevitability on turn 1.

Beyond the practical truism that the faster of two decks with the same end game is favored, Flores
says the 1999 article tells us something else:

**Decisions in the very early game, say turn 1, can affect which player has inevitability in the game
at hand.**

Back to the Galvanic Blast / Incinerate / Day of Judgment case: you can take roughly twelve damage
essentially needlessly by playing for a greedy two-for-one instead of meeting the opponent with
speed, which might put you in a bad if not insurmountable position. How does that change if you just
Blast the Stromkirk Noble? Will the opponent even try to play a non-buffed Stormblood Berserker?

The corollary he adds about the beatdown deck: as the control you often find yourself on your heels
against aggressive starts, but the beatdown is very much **restricted by what it draws** and loses a
lot of general liberty because of it. It's entirely possible the opponent does nothing on turn 2 and
you get a free pass.

## 5. All war is based on deception

Borrowed from Master Sun. The most famous line of *The Art of War* had a moment in the original too.

The general point most players don't espouse: **one way to gain an advantage is to hide who you
are.** In the original, Flores described pretending to be a blue control deck. His opponent took
actions consistent with his own baseline gameplan, and then, while his mana was tapped, Flores killed
him with the combo.

His 2012 note: that's basically how he played Exarch Twin, what Gerry Thompson called the "control"
Twin strategy, the previous year. Twelve years and no new tricks.

The limit: this isn't a superpower available 100% of the time, since in Top 8 situations opponents
often have each other's decklists. But it's a tool you can use some of the time, and **the advantage
comes from the opponent taking actions inconsistent with their actual position.**

## 6. Identify position; take action

The first step to playing a strategically correct game is to actually identify which role you're
supposed to play.

**The original checklist, revisited:**

1. Who has more damage? Usually that has to be the beatdown deck.
2. Who has more removal? Usually that has to be the control deck.
3. Who has more permission and card drawing? Almost always that has to be the control deck.

**Flores now disputes his own #3.** A deck like Delver might have more card drawing than the deck
meant to play control in a matchup (Gitaxian Probe, Ponder) and might use its permission to defend
tempo rather than for control concerns. He takes exception with his own "almost always."

He notes most players, even in a much more sophisticated age of defined archetypes, don't run this
process enough.

**The second half matters more.** A natural like Jon Finkel might take correct actions without first
evaluating what he's supposed to do, but mere mortals need to do both in order to examine and choose
from the true palette of options.

The advanced question: **is there a way to jockey into the position the opponent wants to be in?** If
you can, you usurp all his actions, because he'll take the wrong role's actions.

**Aside:** Zvi and Flores at one point defined "the best deck" as the one that could play both
beatdown and control in the most matchups.

**How to close the loop**, borrowed from process theory, since the original only generalizes:

1. What is our goal? State the goal.
2. Produce a strategy to accomplish it; take actions consistent with it.
3. Get feedback. Is the strategy working?
4. Adjust if necessary.

In especially tight games, Legacy games that last two turns, you can't necessarily check and adjust.
In longer games, and in most processes in life, you can. The important thing is that your actions are
consistent with your strategy and your strategy furthers your actual goal.

## 7. One definition of a bad matchup

Can a deck be both the beatdown and the control? When Flores wrote the original in 1999 he didn't
realize the answer is yes.

**One definition of a terrible matchup is for a role to be completely undermined.**

The example: a no-reach creature deck against **Caleb Durward's U/B Control**, 6th place at the SCG
Standard Open in Nashville, October 8, 2011.

You're supposed to be the beatdown. You play little guys and attack with them. The problem is that
Caleb's deck was nothing but removal and Snapcaster Mages — Flores watched games where **Caleb did
more damage to himself with Dismembers than his opponent did to him.**

So you aren't doing a good job of being the beatdown. Are you going to suddenly play control? You
have no capacity to do so whatsoever.

**Nature abhors a vacuum.** If you aren't going to be the proactive attack deck, Caleb will do it for
you. He makes a proactive play like turn-3 Liliana of the Veil, and because you can't keep a creature
in play, Liliana isn't going anywhere.

Who's defining the terms of the game? If the U/B deck with all the card advantage is *also*
determining the field of battle, you're not in a good position. No position at all. Eventually he
just has a threat and beats you down.

## 8. Sideboarding can be used to eliminate dead weight

Most readers understand "addition by subtraction," that removing a negative can gain value. The
original argued a variation: **a deck with little to no relevance in a matchup can acquire the
opponent's non-preferred, or even preferred, role through clever sideboarding.** Can't accomplish
what you want as the beatdown? Is there a way to morph into the control?

The example is Patrick Chapin's Grixis Desperate Ravings deck, 2nd at Grand Prix Orlando,
January 14, 2012.

The deck was a series of trade-offs. As a base-control deck, Chapin constantly had to choose to get
worse against X in order to bolster his ability to contend with a more common Y, rather than
reinforcing a particular brand of forward movement.

**The problem.** Chapin asked Flores to brainstorm an anti-Humans sideboard strategy for
Pro Tour Dark Ascension. Humans was very fast on offense, its tokens gave natural card advantage
against point removal, and creatures like **Doomed Traveler** made proactive efforts such as Liliana
of the Veil less effective. Humans was card-advantageous enough to undermine Chapin's identity in a
practical sense even though the roles were unambiguous. Other aggro cards like **Strangleroot Geist**
had similar impact, putting him on his heels and challenging him to find the right answer on curve.

**The proposal.** Flores suggested transforming into a **Reanimator** deck, exploiting the fact that
Chapin was already playing Faithless Looting, Desperate Ravings, Liliana of the Veil, and his
trademark ambitious mana base. He could already play all the cards.

The reasoning: **so why not be the beatdown? Why not hit them where they live?** If the problem is
that a turn-4 sweeper is too slow, why not pose a turn-4 insurmountable threat? Be the actor rather
than the defender. Flores notes that a certain Grand Cenobite's persistent ability would make both
Doomed Traveler and Strangleroot Geist decidedly less persistent.

Chapin didn't adopt the idea. Flores's guess is that it's solely because he didn't frame it in a
"Who's the Beatdown?" context at the time, as he does here.

## Notes for use

**What changed in thirteen years.** Flores explicitly retracts part of his own checklist. Question 3
("who has more permission and card drawing?") no longer reliably identifies the control deck, because
modern tempo decks run cheap card selection and use permission to protect a clock. Treat questions 1
and 2 as durable and question 3 as a prompt rather than an answer.

**The two genuinely new ideas here**, neither in the 1999 article:

- **Principle 7, the undermined role.** A matchup is bad not because you're behind on power but
  because your role has been made unplayable and you can't switch. That's a deckbuilding and
  sideboarding problem, not a play problem.
- **Principle 6's advanced move.** Don't only identify your role, ask whether you can seize the role
  the *opponent* wants, which makes every action they take the wrong one.

**The definition worth memorizing** is Zvi's inevitability, quoted in full in principle 4. It's the
variable the original article was missing.
