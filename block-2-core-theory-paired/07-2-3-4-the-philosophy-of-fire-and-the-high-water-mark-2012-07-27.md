# 2-3-4: The Philosophy of Fire and the High-Water Mark

**Mike Flores — StarCityGames, July 27, 2012**

---

## ⚠️ Correction to the reading-list entry

**The reading list describes the high-water mark as "the peak position you reached and whether you
converted it."** That is not the concept in this article.

**Flores's high-water mark is a property of a *format*, not of a game:** it is **the damage threshold that
the format's commonly played removal reaches**, which in turn determines **how much toughness a creature
needs in order to be durable.** It's a card-evaluation tool for picking creatures, not a
position-conversion metric.

The rest of the reading-list entry is accurate — the article does place the Philosophy of Fire alongside
card advantage and tempo as one of three resource types.

---

## The three-resource framework

**The article opens with a block quote from Patrick Chapin**, which is where the "one of three" framing
comes from:

> **"These are the three fundamental building blocks of Magic theory… The Philosophy of Fire. Tempo. Card
> Advantage.**
>
> **These three concepts deal with the basic manipulation of resources in Magic. The reason there are three
> is because there are three ways for a resource to be available to you in a game.**
>
> **The Philosophy of Fire deals with resources that you start with and get no more of, unless you pay for
> them in some way with the use of specific cards. The most well understood example is that of your life
> total, but this also includes the cards in your library, how many poison counters you can endure without
> dying, and so on. This is really just a way of describing the option to continue playing the game."**
>
> — **Patrick Chapin**

**That's the taxonomy worth keeping:** three resource types, distinguished by *how the resource becomes
available* — what you start with and never regain (Philosophy of Fire), what accrues over time (tempo),
and what you draw (card advantage).

## The Sand Golem anecdote

Flores opens with a story about a game he lost badly — his fast aggressive deck of 2/2s running into an
**Aether Flash** — and having no maindeck card that could win.

> **So dismayed and feeling quite a bit up the wrong creek, I looked through my fifteen extra cards. "I
> guess if we are going to do this we are going to do this," I said to myself… If we're going to win, I
> concluded, it's going to be on the backs of these four Sand Golems.**

**The point he draws from it, and his stated view of what theory articles are for:**

> **I see the best of the theory articles as things that you can file away and use when you need them. Like
> I did against that long ago Aether Flash.**

# The Philosophy of Fire, restated

## Attribution and history

> **The Philosophy of Fire was originally proposed by Adrian Sullivan in the summer of 1999 and probably
> stands to this point as Adrian's most important contribution to the canon of Magic theory. In this case,
> I was just the first to write about it.**

**The context Sullivan was working against:** there was already a longstanding relationship between life
total and card economy — **Necropotence**, **Sylvan Library**, and in 2012 **Griselbrand**.

> **Obviously one-life-for-one-card was too good a deal** (Necropotence is restricted or banned in every
> format, as is Yawgmoth's Bargain, and even the brand spanking Griselbrand is close), **and Sylvan
> Library's four-life-for-a-card was kind of an add-on.**

> **Both of these systems were active and ultimately voluntary relationships between life total and cards.**

**Sullivan's move, stated precisely:**

> **In contrast, Adrian proposed a relationship of two life for one card, but coming the other way — a
> relationship the opponent might not opt into all by his lonesome.**

**The original question:**

> **"What if we consider every card worth two life points? We start with seven cards…" Do we only have to
> draw three more cards to win?**

> **Maybe it's not always that simple; maybe not all the time, but quite a bit of theory and many successful
> decks came out of Adrian's simple question and groundbreaking model.**

## Shock as the standard, thirteen years on

> **Shock then became the official standard for red burn spells. Shock was exactly where the line was drawn
> — one card, one mana, two life points. Anything better than a Shock was and is playable to the point of
> an auto-include, and this has not changed in the intervening thirteen years.**

**His 2012 evidence:** Shock itself still played as a minority option after thirteen years of power creep;
**Galvanic Blast** killing Delver of Secrets all year even in decks that rarely achieve metalcraft; **Pillar
of Flame** doing the same to Strangleroot Geist.

> **Didn't Brian Kibler take out Jon Finkel with a flurry of improbable Galvanic Blasts — again, in a deck
> that wasn't "supposed" to be able to hit for four?**

## Building with the Philosophy of Fire

**Why pure burn decks are rare:**

> **It is rare that you will see competitive decks that are really just mono-burn spells. Even highly
> burn-oriented decks in 2012 tend to play some number of recurring sources of damage.**

**Two reasons:**

1. **As a hedge against mulligans** — **going down a card in a system where you have to lace together ten
   cards can be catastrophic.**
2. **Because the Philosophy of Fire allows you to exploit a different kind of card advantage.**

**The revaluation:**

> **When you consider one card two damage, your idea of card economy changes dramatically. A card that can
> produce three damage — an Incinerate — is worth more than a card. A creature that can hit for multiple
> packets of two damage can be considered the same.**

> **When you think of Goblin Guide as an engine for *you* — when it looks so much like an engine for *them*
> — you are empowered with an all-different superpower.**

### The Modern Bump deck

<summary>Andy Burden — R/B Burn, 1st, PTQ Louisville, Feb 18 2012 (Modern)</summary>

**Creature (12)** — 4 Dark Confidant · 4 Goblin Guide · 4 Grim Lavamancer

**Sorcery (12)** — 4 Bump in the Night · 4 Lava Spike · 4 Rift Bolt

**Instant (16)** — 3 Burst Lightning · 2 Flames of the Blood Hand · 4 Lightning Bolt · 4 Searing Blaze ·
3 Shard Volley

**Land (20)** — 4 Arid Mesa · 4 Blackcleave Cliffs · 2 Blood Crypt · 6 Mountain · 4 Scalding Tarn

**Sideboard** — 3 Ensnaring Bridge · 4 Inquisition of Kozilek · 4 Shattering Spree · 4 Torpor Orb
</details>

> **The Bump deck is basically The Philosophy of Fire in its purest form.**

**And the arithmetic that justifies the "bad" cards:**

> Cards like **Lava Spike**, or **Spark Elemental** in some versions, **are just bad Bump in the Nights. But
> in a deck that considers every card an opportunity to deal two damage, each and every one of them actually
> performs 50% above the curve at one mana.**

> **Normally you need ten spells to deal 20, but when some of your cards deal three damage, you only need
> seven.**

He adds that **Bump in the Night itself is a hedge against mana flood, in a sense worth three cards.**

### Burn is hard to play

> **Superficial examination of "burn" decks often equates them with a low level of play, but in reality I
> think they are among the hardest decks to pilot at a high level.**

**The turn-one dilemma he uses to prove it: Lava Spike or Goblin Guide?**

**For Lava Spike:** **it does more damage and does more damage now.**

**Against Goblin Guide:** **it gives the opponent the opportunity to draw an extra card — and in the case
that he does, that can exactly undo what you are trying to do. Remember, one card equals two damage.**

**His answer, with the reasoning:**

> **I think that much or most of the time, Goblin Guide is the better first-turn play.**
>
> - **If you can get in twice you deal four rather than "just" three**, and if more times, better still.
> - **There are actually a fair number of decks that have problems with a fast 2/2.**
> - **Goblin Guide has to get in through The Red Zone while Lava Spike doesn't** — that is, **the window for
>   Lava Spike may stay open after the likelihood of connecting with a Goblin Guide has plummeted.**

> **In either case, if you can hit for four — or for God's sake six — the number of cards you need goes down
> relative to dealing three one time.**

## Mixed threats and interaction

**Why the archetype works structurally:**

> **The Philosophy of Fire performs in large part because it allows seemingly low-power decks to perform
> non-interactively. It lets "burn" decks work like Storm combo decks.**

**The matchup asymmetry:**

> **If you pit your burn deck against a creature deck, you might be raced by the creature deck — whose
> creatures, in matchup, each perform like recurring burn spells in a race.**

> **On balance, the more burn you play, the better you tend to perform against control decks, who devote lots
> of slots to creature control.**

**And the par standard for creatures:** even when you can trade with a creature, **if it got its money just
once — say a solo activation from a Grim Lavamancer — it can act at par with the typical Shock.**

### It's a mindset, not only an archetype

> **The Philosophy of Fire is a mindset, and a tool like many others that you can tuck into ye olde quiver
> for use when it is useful.**

> **You can also play a regular game of Magic and say to yourself, "Hey, my opponent is at such-and-such a
> life total; maybe I should consider evaluating how I might swap my topdecks for a different way to win"**
> — as Kibler did in the Top 8 of Pro Tour Dark Ascension.

**The reframe applied to a creature:**

> **What if you consider Delver of Secrets a three-damage burn spell? A six-damage one? Nine? FOR ONE MANA?
> You can constrict your requirements on how many cards you need to win the game with every swing.**

**And the pattern he watched all day:**

> **I can't tell you how many times I saw Boozer get in with Goblin Guide twice, look out-gunned by big stuff
> like Knight of the Reliquary, and then end it big with a Chain Lightning and a Price of Progress with his
> back against the wall.**

## The archetype's weaknesses

> **What do you do if you have to deal with a creature? God forbid — what if that creature has, gasp, greater
> than two toughness?**

> **Dealing with a creature at all is going to cost you resources. The allure of your strategy is that you
> only need ten cards to win, and you start with seven. If you have to burn some little guy, that is going to
> put a tax on you. If the creature is extra big and you have to use two cards? Disaster and disastrous.**

**And the worst case:**

> **A single hit from a big life gain creature like Baneslayer Angel is like a Mind Twist for you, when it is
> usually just "bad" for most decks.**

**Which explains a card choice from the concept's inventor:**

> **Not surprisingly for the man who invented The Philosophy of Fire, Adrian's first PTQ win was borne on the
> back of Natural Spring** — played maindeck **as a hedge against multiple big burn spells in a format intense
> with packets of four or even six damage.**

# The high-water mark

**The actual definition, which the reading list gets wrong.**

**The puzzle it answers:**

> **Newer players may be puzzled at how much more longer-lived players value four-toughness creatures over
> three-toughness ones. What's the big deal? Is a 3/4 really that much better than a 3/3?**

> **While it might not be a big deal right this second, the historical answer is, "Yeah, pretty much."**

**The mechanism:**

> **Starting with Alpha, the default burn spell in Magic has been Lightning Bolt.** In formats where Lightning
> Bolt is legal, **it stands to reason people will play it.**

**And why burn specifically sets the threshold:**

> **Burn spells aren't popular just because they can go to the face, but because of their versatility. They
> can kill either creatures or opponents, and while they are often less good at killing creatures than
> dedicated black point removal, they are never "dead" against creature-poor or creatureless decks.**

**The use:**

> **Just as The Philosophy of Fire can help you select cards towards a strategy, the notion of the high-water
> mark can be a tool you use to pick your creatures.**

### When it matters and when it doesn't

> **Some decks have lots of creatures (Mono-Green Aggro) and some creatures have massive impact despite their
> size (Snapcaster Mage), so they don't have to take the format's high-water mark particularly into account.
> If you are strategically playing extra creatures in your sideboard, you might want to.**

**The Finkel example:**

> When he executed on the Surprise transformative sideboard that first and best time, **Jon Finkel splashed
> Wildfire Emissary in his U/W Prison deck. He didn't have many slots for creatures, so he needed each
> creature to be high impact.**

> **Wildfire Emissary had a natural resistance to the base removal spell of the day, Swords to Plowshares…
> Well, if they were going to take out a Wildfire Emissary, Jon was going to make them use two cards. The bet
> was that they wouldn't be able to, and would be raced.**

### The Olle Rade example — the best illustration

> **Hall of Famer Olle Rade made his reputation, and took down a Pro Tour, using primarily 2/3 Spiders for
> three mana as his offense — Woolly Spider and Giant Trap Door Spider.**

> **If these creatures don't seem very good to you… they're not. But Rade realized that the high-water mark of
> the format was based on Pyroclasm and Stormbind, so the ability to weather two damage gave a creature
> greater value in context than the casual observer might think.**

*(That is the concept in one example: the creatures were bad in the abstract and correct in the format,
because the number that mattered was 2.)*

### Flores checking his own instinct on Thragtusk

> **When I first saw Thragtusk, I didn't immediately appreciate its durability. As a longtime player, I balked
> at Thragtusk's three toughness… it was not that long ago that we were levying Lightning Bolts at each other
> in Standard.**

**And the resolution, which states the key property of the concept:**

> **Unlike The Philosophy of Fire, which uses a base-two model — ten proposed cards against twenty starting
> life — the high-water mark fluctuates from format to format based on what people play.**

> **It is the reason why Birds of Paradise sometimes consistently live through turn 1, or why a vanilla 3/3
> for three can break a format.**

**His 2012 reading:** **the high-water mark in Standard may have fallen from four to three.** Galvanic Blast
outstripped Incinerate in popularity; **Searing Spear is no Incinerate, whereas Pillar of Flame is
substantially better than Shock.**

## Notes for use

- **The two concepts differ in a way worth holding onto.** The Philosophy of Fire is **fixed** — a base-two
  model, 10 cards against 20 life, stable across formats. The high-water mark is **variable** — it moves with
  whatever removal people are actually playing. One is a constant, the other is a reading of the room.
- **The high-water mark is a creature-selection tool.** Ask what damage number the format's common removal
  reaches, then ask whether your creature survives it. Rade's 2/3 Spiders beat a format whose number was 2.
  Wildfire Emissary beat a format whose answer was Swords. This is why toughness thresholds feel arbitrary
  until you know the number.
- **Chapin's three-resource taxonomy is the cleanest statement of why these three concepts and no others.**
  Resources differ by how they become available: what you start with and can't regain, what accrues with
  time, and what you draw. That's the organizing idea behind the whole of Block 2.
- **The Lava Spike vs. Goblin Guide dilemma is the best single test of whether you've internalized the
  theory.** The creature is worse *now* and better *in expectation*, and the tiebreaker is that the burn
  spell's window stays open longer. That's a real decision-theoretic argument, not a heuristic.
- **"A card that deals three performs 50% above the curve"** is the practical version of the whole model:
  set your denominator at 2, and everything above it reduces the number of cards you need.
- **Note the honesty about weaknesses.** Any creature you must answer taxes the plan; a lifelinking fatty is
  effectively a Mind Twist. That's why "pure burn" decks always carry recurring damage sources.
