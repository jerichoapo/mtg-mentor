# The Danger of Cool Things...

**Chad Ellis — The Dojo, 1999. Reprinted by Star City Games.**

> Why it's in the list: it isn't a theory article, it's a **discipline** article, and it is the only thing in
> this collection about the specific way good players lose games they had already won. Every other piece here
> tells you how to find the right line. This one tells you what makes you abandon it once you've found it.

---

## The reprint note

The SCG version carries an editor's preface worth keeping, because it explains why half of Block 2 exists in
this form at all:

> **A long time ago, the first Magic website was The Dojo — a site that is still legendary for publishing
> some of the most fundamental principles of Magic. Almost all strategical theory can be traced back to the
> Dojo's loyal writers.**

The Dojo went out of business in 2000 on financial trouble. In a last-ditch effort to save four years of
accumulated writing, **the editor asked the community to archive the articles.** SCG's reprints add links
clarifying old cards for players who never saw them.

## The thesis

Ellis opens by conceding the appeal completely, which is what makes the argument land:

> **One of the most fun things in Magic is doing something cool.**

He offers two of his own, one from each side. The duel he **lost** when his dead-next-turn opponent took
back a Flickering Ward, recast it naming Black, cast Evincar's Justice, took it back again, recast it naming
Red, and attacked past his only surviving blocker for exactly lethal. And the duel he **won** when he, dead
next turn, sent a shadow creature into the air and Interdicted his opponent's Spike Feeder so it couldn't
gain life.

He suspects this is why combo players are so loyal to their approach:

> **How can casting creatures, countering spells, and disenchanting Disks compare to generating infinite
> mana, shooting someone with a Mogg Fanatic 237 times, attacking with a 1,000,000/1,000,000 Mishra's
> Factory or putting someone in a hard lock?**

Even bad combo decks are hard to let go of. But the bad combo decks are not his target:

> **I want to talk about the tendency Magic players have to lose games or make blunders because they are
> fixated by the prospect of doing something cool.**

## Four examples, and he starts with himself

### 1. Mana burn, and being too clever

PTQ quarter-finals, game one against Mo Gilmore's Sligh. **Ellis is at one life; Mo is at eighteen.** Ellis
draws Yawgmoth's Will and finds a line: **Dark Ritual, Will, Ritual, Hymn to Tourach, Duress.** He has an
active Cursed Scroll and two Racks. With an empty hand and no creatures, Mo has only a few turns to draw a
burn spell or a Mogg Fanatic before the Scroll and Racks finish him.

He starts the sequence thinking Mo has three cards. Checking, he sees two.

> **Suddenly I start to get clever.**

He decides not to waste the Duress, and constructs three real reasons for it: there's a small but non-zero
chance Mo draws a non-burn, non-creature spell like Goblin Grenade or Fork; he might draw a second Will, in
which case the Duress would be useful; and he might get to use Spinning Darkness and need the Duress in his
graveyard. **All three are genuinely correct considerations.** That is the point.

> **Quick quiz: What happens when you are at one life and end your turn with Dark Ritual, Hymn you, go?
> I burn to death.**

It didn't matter, because Mo's next draw was a Fanatic. But the diagnosis is exact:

> **I was so happy about how clever I was being that I completely forgot that when I originally planned the
> Will I knew I needed to cast both spells to avoid mana burn.**

**The failure was not the analysis. The failure was that improving the plan silently discarded a constraint
the original plan had been built to satisfy.**

### 2. Wakefield, and the second Verdant Force

Ellis quotes Jamie Wakefield's Dojo report "Super Lucky Guy," where Wakefield catches himself: he Natural
Orders for a second Verdant Force when he already has one, and immediately realises it:

> **"Not very smart because he might top deck a Wrath of God, and I realize this as soon as I do it. Wake up
> and play smart, Wakefield!!!"**

Ellis's summary: **one Verdant Force was plenty to win.** Holding the Natural Order meant he could answer a
Wrath by sacrificing a creature to Order for the second Force. Casting it early put **both Forces under one
Wrath of God.** Wakefield diagnosed it himself; Ellis's addition is *why* it happened — he **got caught up
with the fun he was having shooting out dinosaurs.**

### 3. The one Wakefield didn't catch

This is the best passage in the article, because Ellis finds a second, larger error in the same report that
the author missed entirely.

Wakefield, with no creatures, topdecks Natural Order, then a Wall of Blossoms, then a land. Next turn he
plans to untap, Natural Order, and win. His opponent — who has been **Scrolling him with a Demonic
Consultation in hand** — draws Diabolic Edict and kills the Wall. Wakefield's write-up treats this as the
opponent being a "Super Lucky Guy."

Ellis's question:

> **Can you see what Jamie is missing here? He is so focused on the Order/Force combo he thinks his opponent
> is lucky for topdecking a Diabolic Edict... The same opponent who is Scrolling him with a Demonic
> Consultation!**

Assume the opponent draws something else. Wakefield untaps, Natural Orders, and:

> **Loses. "During your discard phase, I'll cast Demonic Consultation, naming Diabolic Edict." It's as
> simple as that.**

There was no luck involved. **The opponent held a tutor.** And the timing doesn't save it either: even
tapped out, the opponent can untap, Consult during upkeep and Edict during upkeep, because **as active
player he gets to act before the Verdant Force can be activated.**

The strategic conclusion:

> **Jamie's whole plan involved throwing away his one real chance at winning** — drawing a second creature so
> his Force would have Edict protection.

**Cool-thing fixation didn't only cause the misplay. It hid the misplay afterwards**, by supplying "he got
lucky" as an explanation that closed the question.

### 4. The matchup analysis that isn't objective

Ellis then goes through Wakefield's Secret Force versus Pox analysis line by line, and the exercise is a
demonstration of what non-objectivity looks like in writing rather than in play.

| Wakefield's claim | Ellis's answer |
|---|---|
| **Verdant Force** — built-in Pox and Edict protection | Only reliably arrives via Natural Order, which **needs two creatures in play to reach Edict protection** |
| **Wall of Blossoms** — blocks Steel Golem without costing a card | Genuinely good, but **Scroll or Funeral Charm lets the Golem through** |
| **Acridians** — block Steel Golem | Same answer. The Golem's job is to stop you beating down **while I rip up your hand and Scroll you.** Spending two mana on turns 2 and 3 for a creature that can't beat the Golem is fine by him |
| **Natural Order** — fetch life, a monkey, or the fatty | **Order costs four. Duress costs one, Hymn two, Consult-plus-Hymn three.** Not reliable against Pox |
| **Elves** — mana for Pox's land destruction | **Charms, Scrolls, and half the sideboard kill them** |
| **Cursed Scroll** — good when it kills your hand | Conceded outright: **"Pox doesn't like people with Scrolls."** |
| **Creeping Mold** — self-evident | A four-mana one-for-one on artifacts, into Hymns and Pox and Elfkill. **"Creep my Rack? Ritual/Will/Ritual/Hymn/Rack... We're back where we started except that we both have fewer cards in hand."** |

He extends it to sideboards: are three Charms and two Lifeforces really better than the Dystopia, Edicts, and
Perish that Pox brings in? And the structural asymmetry — **Secret Force has to topdeck its hosers; Pox can
topdeck a hoser or a Consultation and fetch the hoser it most wants.**

The point isn't the matchup. It's the failure mode:

> **Sometimes we can get so wrapped up in the cool things our deck can do that we lose objectivity and forget
> what our opponent can do.**

### 5. Terry, and the Living Death for the epics

The last example is the most painful. Top 8 of a Boston PTQ. Ellis's friend Terry is on Rec/Sur against a
mono-green deck with **no Cursed Scroll and no Desert Twister** — no answer to a recurring Spike Weaver.

> **It should be a bye.**

Terry has a Weaver in play, a graveyard full of good creatures, and Living Death and Recurring Nightmare in
hand. He can Recur the Weaver for Nekrataal, or cast Living Death, or anything. **His opponent knows he's
losing and says he's just going to drag it out.**

> **Terry gets focused on casting an amazing Living Death... A Living Death for the epics.**

He gets out Krovikan Horror and sacrifices his creatures **including both Birds of Paradise, which he needs
to cast Living Death.** He survives the next turn anyway, still has three lands, a Wall of Roots, Survival on
the board, and Recur plus Weaver to buy time. **He is still very much in the game.**

> **However, still thinking about the lost opportunity to cast Living Death, he draws something that doesn't
> produce B and concedes.**

**He conceded a winning position because he was mourning a play he didn't get to make.** That is the article
in one sentence.

## The rules

Ellis closes with a checklist rather than a principle, and it is short enough to memorise:

> **Few players are immune to the "cool things" problem. We all love to come up with combos or brilliant
> plays, but (from a competitive standpoint, at least) we need to remember that what matters is winning the
> game.**

1. **If the simple line wins, take it.** "If Living Death is enough to win, cast it."
2. **When you improve a plan at the last minute, re-check the original.** "Think for a moment about whether
   there were any reasons (like mana burn, in my case) why the original plan might be better."
3. **When you're close to winning, look for the disruption.** "Think about whether your opponent might have a
   way to disrupt your win (like Consulting for Diabolic Edict) and if you find one whether you have a better
   chance of winning through a different path."
4. **The summary rule:** **"Take the time to check what you are doing, make sure you aren't giving
   unnecessary chances to your opponent and don't be fancy for its own sake."**

He signs off as "Chads of MephistophEllis," Team Diesel and Team Your Move Games.

## Notes for use

- **This is the counterweight to everything else in Block 2.** Hahn, Flores, Zvi and Taylor are all teaching
  you to find better lines. Ellis is pointing out that **most losses at the top of a PTQ bracket come from
  the player who found a better line and then found a "better" one.** Rule 2 — re-check the original plan
  when you improve it — is the single most useful habit in this collection.
- **It pairs naturally with Block 0.** The cool-things problem is a **Johnny** failure mode, and Ellis is
  describing what happens when Johnny's motivation survives into a Spike's tournament. Read alongside
  [Timmy, Johnny, and Spike Revisited](../block-0-orientation/03-timmy-johnny-and-spike-revisited-2006-03-20.md),
  where Rosewater's subgroups make the same distinction from the design side.
- **And with Duke.** [Level One 25: Going Through the Motions](../block-1-the-spine/25-going-through-the-motions-2015-05-11.md)
  is the procedural version of Ellis's rule 4 — announce your spells correctly, don't skip steps. Duke's
  advice on [playing from ahead](../block-1-the-spine/24-playing-from-ahead-playing-from-behind-2015-03-30.md)
  is the modern statement of Terry's mistake: **when you're winning, take the line with the fewest ways to
  lose, not the best one.**
- **The Wakefield section is a lesson in reading your own results.** Ellis finds the real error by asking
  what the opponent's *hand* could do rather than what he drew. **"He got lucky" is almost always a place
  where analysis stopped early**, and that habit is worth more than any single play here.
- **Deeply dated card pool, entirely undated psychology.** Urza-block Standard: Pox, Secret Force, Rec/Sur,
  Sligh, Cursed Scroll, mana burn. **Mana burn no longer exists as a rule**, so example 1's specific
  punishment cannot happen today — but the shape of it (a late change to a plan that quietly violates a
  constraint the plan depended on) happens constantly.
- **The date is approximate.** SCG stamps every article in this Dojo reprint series January 1 of its year.
  The card references — Urza's Saga and Urza's Legacy, a PTQ for Pro Tour New York — put it in early 1999.
