# The Philosophy of Fire

**Mike Flores — StarCityGames, April 23, 2004**

> Why it's in the list: life total as a finite resource, and every card measured in damage rather than in
> cards. The consequence: an unblocked threat that trades down is still fine if it moved the opponent a
> quarter of the way to zero.

---

## Attribution, up front

**Flores does not claim the idea.**

> What we will call The Philosophy of Fire is a way of looking at cards and measuring them that **Adrian
> "invented"** during Urza's Block Constructed PTQs.

**And he's candid that the original deck never proved anything:** Adrian built it after ConTroll at GP
Memphis and no longer needed to test or play the format. **"Basically, Adrian's Flame Rifts, Landslides,
and non-Echo paying Ghitu Slingers just beat up on MikeyP, Zvi, and Rob Hahn at the airport."**

> **However the deck concept was, in my opinion, very interesting and different from any conventional way
> of looking at cards.**

*(This matches what Flores says in `block-0-orientation/04-shoulders-of-giants-2013-08-06.md`, where he
credits Sullivan with "an inventive connection between card economy and life totals" and describes his own
article as co-opting it.)*

---

## The Necropotence warm-up

**Why the article opens on an unrelated-looking archetype:**

> **Necropotence gives the average player the most concrete understanding of the interaction between cards
> and life of any archetype or mechanism.**

> **The Philosophy of Fire will do the exact same thing, but instead of trading life for cards from your
> own deck, it speaks about the relationship of trading cards for your opponent's life. Specifically, the
> goal will be to translate a hand into a dead man.**

### The puzzle: 1999 Ohio Valley Regionals, Top 4

**The position.** You're on Necropotence, up a game and commanding. Your opponent had a beatdown draw with
**Skittering Skirge**, **Dauthi Horror**, and **Dauthi Slayer**; you came back with **Stronghold
Taskmaster** and a well-placed **Drain Life**. They're on **twelve**, you're on **twenty**. No cards in
hand. You tap three of four lands for **Necropotence**.

You say **"pay four."** Your opponent objects that you're at twenty with twice his clock and should
"at least play right." You say **"fine — Necro for seven,"** and realize your error as you adjust the die.
**Head judge Mike Donais won't let you take it back.** An onlooker in a reversed fitted cap says *"these
aren't the droids you are looking for."*

**What do you do now?**

### Flores's answer

> **I think there is only one defensible play here.** Sammons had already made a mistake, **but it didn't
> have to cost him the game.**

> **He has to Necropotence for eleven, putting him on two life.** That would give him **eighteen cards in
> total** to use during his end step.

**Why eighteen and why two, not one:**

> In those eighteen cards he would have the maximum, if not certain, chance of finding **Dark Ritual** and
> **Diabolic Edict**. He would have to use Dark Ritual to play Diabolic Edict, and then likely mana burn —
> **which is why it is correct to go to two life rather than one.**

> **In the vastly unlikely scenario that his remaining sixteen cards didn't give him a Drain or Corrupt,
> Sammons would probably still be able to win with just the Taskmaster.**

**What actually happened:** he passed, and got killed by the **Hatred** Flores was holding — **and then
beat Flores in the third, and then beat one-time US National Champion Dennis Bently in the finals to win
the Ohio Valley crown.**

**Flores's trivia note:** watching that match were **Mike Donais, Aaron Forsythe, Randy Buehler, Worth
Wollpert, and he believes Pat Chapin**, with the Hatred deck designed by **Brian Schneider** — *"a
foreshadow of the future face of R&D."*

# The theory

## Three kinds of exchange

**Flores builds the concept by contrast.**

**1. Cards for cards.** The conventional view. **"Consider the simple relationships of a one-for-one
Shatter or two-for-one Unburden."**

**2. Your own life for your own cards.** **Necropotence** is a one-to-one exchange; **Sylvan Library** is
4 life for 1 card. Dred Panda Roberts is an x-for-(x−y) exchange where a limited number of cards are
relevant.

**3. Cards for the opponent's life** — the Philosophy of Fire.

### The Natural Spring bridge

**The example that makes the third category visible.**

> **Natural Spring is a card advantage card in a relational exchange not unlike Hymn to Tourach.**

> **Line up all of a red beatdown deck's cards and all of Baron Harkonnen's cards. That one Natural Spring
> will trade for four Shocks, two Incinerates and a Shock, a Ball Lightning and a Shock, or two Fireblasts
> and four Mountains.**

> **That's a boatload of card advantage from a silly card like Natural Spring that most people would never
> consider playing.**

**And the subtler layer:** recurring Natural Spring with **Gaea's Blessing** lets you draw three cards a
turn with **Sylvan Library** — *"not unlike the interaction between Drain Life and Necropotence."*

**Why the comparison works at all:**

> **The relationship is only valid if there are two exchanges we can perceive. We contrast cards for life
> (Shock), and again cards for life in the other direction (Natural Spring), to see the card advantage
> generated by Natural Spring when compared to Shock.**

**The definition, finally:**

> **What the Philosophy of Fire does is focus on the first part of that exchange. Rather than looking at a
> cards-for-cards or life-for-cards relationship, it focuses on cards for life, and associates a value
> based on the default damage spell being Shock.**

## The arithmetic

**The conversion rates, stacked:**

| Card | Exchange rate |
|---|---|
| **Necropotence** | **x life = x−1 cards** |
| **Sylvan Library** | **4 life = 1 card** |
| **A deck of all Shocks** | **1 card = 2 life** |

**The consequence:**

> **You start with seven cards. With mana development being your only limiting factor, that means you
> should be able to draw a lethal hand with ten cards.**

**Flores immediately checks himself:**

> Of course some of those cards are going to be land — **and if they aren't, you won't be able to play your
> spells anyway** — so this counting method **does not say you win or even assemble twenty in the first
> three turns.**

> **If you structure damage in two-point increments, your kill is probably going to show up in about seven
> or eight turns. Clearly that isn't fast enough.**

**Which produces the real design questions:**

> **So what happens when you up the amount of damage your average card does to three life? How do you
> count the relationships now? How many of your opponent's cards matter? Ultimately, how fast can you
> kill?**

**And the target:**

> At some point **you can structure your deck so you will poke in for a few damage before your average
> opponent has blockers or other developmental resources, and then end him with a burn flurry before he
> can kill you.**

> **Most of the time you will want your cards to do more than two damage each in order to quicken the
> clock, but two is a good starting point for conceptual measuring purposes.**

# The worked case: Dave Price Red vs. Fires

**The deck (David Price, Top 8 of a Grudge Match Qualifier before U.S. Nationals 2001):**

<details>
<summary>Dave Price Red, 2001</summary>

4 Chimeric Idol · 3 Firebrand Ranger · 4 Flametongue Kavu · 3 Ghitu Fire · 4 Kris Mage ·
3 Rage Weaver · 4 Seal of Fire · 4 Shock · 4 Skizzik · 4 Urza's Rage · 19 Mountain · 4 Rishadan Port
</details>

**The problem matchup was Fires of Yavimaya:**

> Consider that a Fires deck is essentially a mirror match where **the opponent has more and faster mana,
> haste, and creatures that are two if not three times the size** of yours. **The initial games were awful
> and the deck gave up a string of packs into the greedy hands of eager children.**

### The tell in the decklist

**Why Rage Weaver and Firebrand Ranger over Goblin Raider**, which Randy Buehler had specifically printed
so Price would have a two-drop?

> **Dave said, quite surprisingly: Goblin Raider Can't Block.**

### The reframe

> **In re-approaching the matchup against Fires, I operated not as the beatdown deck, but utilizing the
> Philosophy of Fire.**

**Why the normal plans fail:**

> **A red beatdown deck's plan is to deploy, burn a path, and hit. Failing that, deploy, attempt to
> overwhelm, and finish. But these plans don't work against Fires.** Fires is faster, its creatures are too
> big to burn, and it's difficult or impossible to race on the ground.

**The alternative:**

> **But what happens when you look at the deck purely as a burn deck? What happens when you manage your
> cards against time rather than against their cards?**

**And the exploit that follows:**

> **The confident opponent would not generally block an un-kicked Skizzik on turn 4 with his fresh and
> precious Blastoderm. The cocky opponent doesn't realize that that decision will in many games cost him
> the duel.**

> **Because you don't care about board development — at least as long as you still have life points —
> because you are playing with essentially a different paradigm, the way you value cards may not be obvious
> to the opponent, which will force him to make passive errors that do not correctly address your proactive
> strategy.**

### The central sentence

> **Your un-kicked Skizzik is not a loss of a card, it's a quarter of his life total. It's two and a half
> units of burn, or one and two-thirds, depending on how you are evaluating your hand.**

> **Though it looks like you are falling behind when the Skizzik hits the bin, in fact that one card
> over-performed according to your units of measurement.**

**And the felt experience of playing it:**

> **It is difficult to tell you how painful it is to look across the board and desperately throw your second
> to last card — probably a very good Flametongue Kavu or Chimeric Idol — at the opponent's head as you
> chump block with Kris Mage. But when that is your only play, you've got to make it.**

> **It is impossible to tell you how painful it is for the opponent when you rip that last land, lay it, and
> direct the Ghitu Fire for exactly his life total the next turn.**

## Why it makes you a better player

**The most valuable section of the article, and the one that generalizes furthest.**

> **The really great thing about the Philosophy of Fire is that it forces you to play much tighter Magic
> than you may be accustomed to. In many cases your cards are "objectively" inferior to your opponent's.
> You are forced to make tough decisions and think each action through before making a play.**

**His example of the required precision:** setting up a burn kill against Fires often involves **allowing
the opponent to enter the attack, then adding some amount of mana to your pool, then activating Chimeric
Idol, then blocking, then pitching a card to Kris Mage and possibly playing Ghitu Fire or Urza's Rage still
during the attack.**

> **This is not a difficult play if you walk through it step by step, but a lazy attack/block step or
> careless pass of priority will cost you a close game each and every time.**

### The self-diagnosis

**Flores describes a shared failure mode with teammate Tim McKenna:**

> **In Sealed Deck, Tim and I are both willing to take the worst deck on the team, at least as long as it
> has a little removal and a couple of outs. We know that with our backs to the wall, we will think through
> our plays and try to formulate a strategy that will let us exploit those outs.**

> **At the same time, when we are ahead, we both tend to get sloppy. Give us an advantage, and we will find
> every way in the world to let it slip away.**

**And why the deck fixes it:**

> **When you play focused on the Philosophy of Fire, your deck won't let you fall into those patterns. You
> have limited resources and have to manage them precisely in the face of your opponent's qualitative and
> developmental advantages. You can't make lazy plays.**

> **Conversely, when you are ahead, you just tap all your mana and X-spell the face, denying your opponent
> the opportunity to out-play your careless ass.**

**Epilogue on the deck:** Price didn't play it on Day Two, worried it had become a "pet" deck, and made Top
8 with Fires instead. **A month later Brian Kibler and others ran with the ideas at Worlds**, and SCG's
"mighty Potato" made the 2001 Worlds Top 8 on essentially Price's list, **this time with the Goblin Raiders
in.**

# The 2004 cautionary tale

**The back half of the article is Flores building a Philosophy of Fire deck for Regionals 2004 and failing,
publicly and in detail.**

**The thesis:** Big Red decks had beaten the expected top decks in both constituent blocks, so **"the
Standard choice became obvious."**

**The engine:**

> **Against a beatdown deck, Pulse of the Forge and Rorix Bladewing is a lock. It's twenty points of damage
> rolled into two cards, a guaranteed kill on turn 7, and in many games a closer in five.**

> **Rorix is the biggest, baddest burn spell in your arsenal. He is worth twelve life due to his combination
> of haste and a second attack.**

**The math:** Rorix hits for six (opponent to fourteen). Second attack for six, **with damage on the stack
send Pulse of the Forge at their head. What kind of beatdown deck can't deal seven measly damages in five to
seven turns?** Buy back the Pulse. Rorix's hit resolves. Replay the Pulse. **6 + 4 + 6 + 4 = 20.**

**The iteration, version by version, with the tester feedback verbatim:**

| Version | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **v1** — Talisman of Impulse, Tree of Tales, Shrapnel Blast, splash green for Naturalize | *"Lost to Affinity. Can't win if your Tree of Tales gets killed. Splash damage on Oxidize."* |
| **v2** — dodge artifact splash damage; Solemn Simulacrum, Seething Song, Temple of the False God | *"People are asking why, if I'm playing with Seething Song, I'm in the serious room. Also Temple of the False God sucks. I never cast my spells. And I'm losing to Bidding."* |
| **v3** — hybrid, back to Shrapnel Blast, 22 lands, eight Talismans, Furnace Dragons main | Better, but **"it seemed to lose because it didn't have enough mana,"** and always lost to a lethal Patriarch's Bidding |
| **v4** — cut threats for maindeck **Molten Rain** | **"Wow (I thought)."** Bidding went from close-but-unfavorable to close-but-favorable; stopped losing to random decks |
| **v5** — cut green, add Electrostatic Bolt, "focus Focus FOCUS" | **"There I learned I had committed the cardinal sin."** |

**Why Molten Rain worked**, which is a nice Philosophy-of-Fire argument in itself:

> **Under the theory that you only really needed two cards to win, we figured that cutting threats wouldn't
> be that bad if we could stifle the opponent's development long enough to get Rorix and Pulse online.**

> **We only ever needed a turn to close, and Molten Rain bought that turn.**

## The pet deck

> **A pet deck is kind of like the hot girl that lives around the corner on your floor your freshman year of
> college.** You are sure she will get with you… **But you are kidding yourself, man. Wake up.**

**Flores recounts the tell:** teammates telling him the deck was cheating him, Seth Burn pointing out he
didn't have the mana for the plays he was making, **six or seven sideboarded games against Affinity with
four Detonate, four Electrostatic Bolt, and four Furnace Dragon in — and still no wins.**

**The diagnosis, which is a real strategic point rather than just self-deprecation:**

> **The problem with the Affinity matchup is that while Rorix and Starstorm are objectively better cards
> than what the block build plays, relationally, they aren't better against Affinity.**

> **Those maindeck Detonates and Damping Matrices really do line up better than the Starstorms and
> Bladewings that let you race the Goblins.**

**And the most quotable failure in the article:**

> **The tragic thing is, this deck always wins. Always. It just happens to win exactly one turn too late.**

**His parting instruction to anyone who wants it:**

> **All you need to do to make this deck a winner is speed it up by exactly one turn — or, as in the Bidding
> matchup, slow the opponent down by one.**

**And the concept survives the failure:**

> **This doesn't mean that The Philosophy of Fire makes any less sense — because I think this non-traditional
> way of looking at the relationships between cards really is one of Adrian's better ideas — just that for
> my own sake, I have to step away from this particular implementation.**

## Bonus section: two things he learned

**1. Don't Turn-1 land-destroy against Affinity.**

> **You are looking at that artifact land on turn 1 and you are looking at the Detonate or Oxidize in your
> opening hand. Don't do it, man.** Every single time I tell myself not to, but most of the time I do it
> anyway. **It's always wrong. They always play a Skullclamp the second turn.**

> **First turn land destruction is not faster than Sinkhole, it's a trip to Paris.**

**2. Decree of Justice is bad**, and the reason is a Philosophy of Fire reason:

> **Friends, you don't always get a third turn. Even though there are some control decks out there, this is
> a beatdown format. Even the slow decks can set up single-turn attacks for the kill. If you don't treat it
> like a beatdown format, you yourself will be doomed to be beaten down.**

## Notes for use

- **The one sentence to keep:** *your un-kicked Skizzik is not a loss of a card, it's a quarter of his life
  total.* That is the entire theory. Once life is the denominator, a card that trades down has still
  performed if it moved the number.
- **The conversion table is the practical tool.** Fix a default damage unit — Flores uses Shock, so 1 card
  = 2 life — and you can immediately ask how many cards a lethal hand requires and how many turns that
  takes. Then ask what happens when the average rises to 3. That's a deckbuilding calculation, not a
  metaphor.
- **The paradigm advantage is real and underrated:** when you measure in life and your opponent measures in
  board, **they will make correct-looking blocks that are wrong against you.** Not blocking the un-kicked
  Skizzik is the canonical example.
- **The tighter-play argument is the strongest non-obvious claim.** A deck with objectively worse cards
  forces precise sequencing because there's no slack. Flores explicitly identifies his own weakness —
  sloppiness when ahead — and notes the archetype prevents it. Compare Zvi's instinct-bias advice in
  `block-1-the-spine/27-playing-safe-and-playing-scared-2015-08-24.md`.
- **The "always wins one turn too late" failure is a fundamental-turn failure.** Read directly against
  `05-clear-the-land-and-the-fundamental-turn-2000-12-31.md`: Flores's deck had the right plan and the
  wrong number, and his own prescription is Zvi's — move the FT by one, in either direction.
- **The pet-deck warning is a genuine methodological point** buried in a joke: when your testers report
  losses and you keep tuning rather than abandoning, you've stopped evaluating. Note also his correct
  diagnosis that **cards can be objectively better and relationally worse** — the same insight as line-up
  theory (`block-1-the-spine/23-line-up-theory-2015-03-23.md`).
- **Cross-reference:** Flores lists this at **#13** in his own countdown
  (`block-0-orientation/04-shoulders-of-giants-2013-08-06.md`), where he credits Adrian Sullivan and notes
  the concept differentiates "the Lava Spike deck from Red Deck Wins." Reid Duke's **reach** and **damage
  racing** installments (Block 1, files 08 and 18) are the modern practical version.
- **Reading caution:** the article is roughly one-third theory and two-thirds a 2004 Regionals deckbuilding
  diary. The theory is in the first half; the second half is valuable as a worked failure, not as card
  evaluation.
