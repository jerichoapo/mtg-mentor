# it's about TIME

**Eric Taylor — Usenet (rec.games.trading-cards.magic.strategy), August 11, 1997; republished on
The Dojo, 1999**

> Why it's in the list: the article that established tempo as a resource separate from card
> advantage. Why a play that loses you a card can still win the game, and why the turn a thing
> happens matters as much as whether it happens.

## The setup

Taylor opens by admitting he has posted about the concept of time on Usenet several times and is
about to write another rather pedantic one. His reason this time: he has seen several MirVis-lite
cards misused, and he thinks the misuse traces to a lack of understanding of time in Magic. The
specific cards he names are **Memory Lapse**, **Power Sink**, **Man-o'-War**, and **Ancestral
Knowledge**.

## The two limitations

His framing move, and the foundation of the whole argument. Look at Magic's game mechanics and there
are only **two basic limitations**:

1. **Draw one card per turn.**
2. **Play one land per turn.**

The two are related in subtle ways.

**The first limitation** has been discussed at length by "the Godfather of magic theory," Brian
Weissman, and Taylor considers it well understood by tournament players. Even indirect card advantage
is fairly easy to spot. His example: when a Wrath of God kills three creatures, you gain the same
card advantage Ancestral Recall does. Three cards for one. You can just add it up.

**The second limitation is harder**, because of the more complicated interactions of mana, time, and
the casting costs of individual spells in a deck. That limitation is what the article is about.

## Time is interchangeable with mana

This is the central identity of the piece.

If you have a little mana on the board and a slew of creatures in hand, you can take a single turn to
play each creature. **That requires time.** If instead you had a mana base large enough to dump your
entire hand, you could do it in one turn. So you can spend either **a lot of time** or **a large base
of mana** to deploy the same cards.

The consequence for deckbuilding: if you play extremely efficient creatures like **Savannah Lions**
or **Kird Ape**, you are building a mana-efficient deck, which is a deck **trying to win by obtaining
a time advantage.**

## Decks tilt one way or the other

It is always good to have as much card advantage and as much time advantage as possible, but in
practice decks tilt.

- **Time advantage decks:** a Stasis deck, a creature swarm deck.
- **Card advantage decks:** Counterpost.
- **Both:** a few manage it. Taylor's example is classic Necro during the "Black Summer."

**Why control decks must win on card advantage.** With many counterspells you have to win via card
advantage. You allow the opponent to build a horde of creatures. You let him play the Lions and Kird
Apes and you take your medicine when he attacks. Finally you Wrath of God, obtain a huge card
advantage, and win by having more cards.

**The counter-pressure.** If the fast creature deck can deprive you of enough time with **Strip
Mine**, he defeats you via his time advantage. Winning with card advantage and counterspells takes a
long time, and you often don't care whether you can play all your spells at once. Control decks
therefore often contain larger, less mana-efficient spells that **violate the principles of a time
advantage deck.** Taylor's conclusion: it is difficult to build a deck that wins via both.

**Why the format mattered.** In MirVis-lite, decks were extremely dependent on a good draw, not too
much land and not too little. Because of that, and because a good burn deck could win so quickly,
most decks were trying to win via time advantage rather than the slower, more controlling card
advantage method.

## The application: Memory Lapse vs. Power Sink

Taylor's worked example, and the reason he wrote the piece.

**Memory Lapse is a card for preserving a time advantage. Power Sink is for a deck trying to win via
card advantage.**

- **Power Sink** requires a lot of mana to use effectively, which equates to not having the time to
  play out creatures and swarm the opponent.
- **Memory Lapse**, when the opponent plays a large spell and you force him to cast it again, gains
  you a huge time advantage.

**But in a deck designed for long games, Power Sink is better.** If you Memory Lapse, your opponent
will draw that spell again while you draw something else, which about one third of the time will be a
land instead of a useful spell. So **Memory Lapse slightly increases the effective card quality of
your opponent's library in a long game.** In the short term it gives you an incredible advantage in
time.

That is as clean a statement of the tempo/card-advantage tradeoff as the era produced: the same card
is good or bad depending on which resource your deck is built to accumulate.

## Two decks that illustrate it

Taylor uses two decks from the same Chicago PTQ, which look superficially alike and are entirely
different.

<details>
<summary>Tom Guevin — Chicago PTQ (MirVis-lite)</summary>

**Creatures (20):** 4 Man-o'-War · 3 Knights of Mist · 2 Serrated Biskelion · 4 Cloud Elemental ·
4 Waterspout Djinn · 3 Floodgate

**Spells (17):** 4 Memory Lapse · 4 Dissipate · 3 Foreshadow · 4 Impulse · 2 Boomerang

**Land (23):** 19 Island · 4 Quicksand

**Sideboard (15):** 1 Floodgate · 1 Knight of Mist · 2 Serrated Biskelion · 3 Rainbow Efreet ·
2 Suq'Ata Firewalker · 4 Undo · 2 Dream Tides
</details>

<details>
<summary>"Hammer" — Chicago PTQ (reconstructed from memory by Taylor, so not exact)</summary>

**Creatures (12):** 2 Rainbow Efreet · 4 Man-o'-War · 2 Ophidian · 2 Floodgate · 2 Hazerider Drake

**Blue spells (12):** 2 Impulse · 4 Dissipate · 2 Desertion · 4 Power Sink

**White spells (10):** 2 Gerrard's Wisdom · 2 Gossamer Chains · 2 Sacred Mesa · 4 Abeyance

**Mana and Land (26):** 4 Flood Plain · 7 Plains · 10 Island · 3 Mind Stone · 2 Quicksand
</details>

**The point of the pairing.** Look superficially and you might think both are counterspell-control
decks. They are entirely different decks with different goals and play styles.

**Guevin's deck is a fast attacking deck planning to win on time.** He has an immense army of
fearsome attackers. **The Cloud Elemental is not there to block, he's there to attack.** He will hit
the opponent so hard and so fast that the opponent will not have the time, or the mana, which Taylor
keeps insisting are practically the same thing, to stop him. **The counterspells are there to
reinforce the goal of doing damage, not for defense.**

**Hammer's deck relies on a much slower attack** with creatures that are more difficult to kill. He
cannot attack with that quickness. He must protect himself against threats and stay alive into the
middle or end game, where he finally wins via one of his card advantage engines such as **Sacred
Mesa**. Taylor notes **Gerrard's Wisdom** also functions as a card advantage engine, mainly against
burn, by trading several burn spells for one Wisdom.

**Therefore the counterspells must differ.** Guevin has to use Memory Lapse: he will be in situations
where he counts his mana, counts his opponent's life, and thinks *if I can just hit him two more
turns, he will be dead*. With that intensity of focus on generating time advantage, Memory Lapse is
far better. **Hammer's deck is willing to give up time for card advantage, so it would be suicide to
run Memory Lapse in it.** So what if you force the opponent to recast his Necrosavant and waste five
mana? Given the deck's slowness and its need to win by card-advantaging the opponent, Power Sink is
the card.

## Trading time for card advantage, and back

Taylor points out the conversion runs both directions, most visibly in Guevin's deck. If you Memory
Lapse a card, you often have the option of then **Foreshadow**ing it, gaining card advantage. But the
combo costs four mana total, and if you could simply kill the opponent by casting another creature,
you sometimes want to give up the card advantage for time advantage. He notes this is rare, since
Foreshadow plus Memory Lapse is so good for card advantage.

**The same card, played two ways.** The two decks use **Man-o'-War** differently:

- **Guevin's deck:** as soon as you draw it, you generally just play it out and go whack your
  opponent with whatever creature you already have in play.
- **Hammer's deck:** you often wait a long time. Sometimes you wait to reach eight mana for the
  Man-o'-War plus **Desertion** combo. Other times you wait until you have enough blue that bouncing
  your own **Floodgate** will kill all of the opponent's creatures. **You are trading off valuable
  time to generate card advantage.**

## The Ancestral Knowledge autopsy

Taylor closes on why **Ancestral Knowledge** is a bad card, analyzed in the same terms. He notes it
is in plain power much weaker than Impulse, that a large amount of testing had always come up weak,
and that there are several ways to analyze why.

**Problem one: it violates card advantage.** You play the card, it goes away, and a turn later you
draw the card you want. If you happen to want a single card it operates effectively like a
ten-card-deep Vampiric Tutor that costs two. Because it violates card economy, **it is impossible to
use in a deck that relies on card advantage to win.**

**Problem two: it ties up your mana supply with a cumulative upkeep.** Because of that cost, in a
fast time-advantage deck you too often simply lose the initiative by tying up your mana.

**The squeeze:** too card-inefficient for a control card advantage deck, too dangerous for a fast
beatdown deck. That leaves a trick deck like Sands-a-poise, where Taylor thinks it would fit nicely
except that **Vampiric Tutor and Enlightened Tutor are simply better** for that purpose. He allows
that when the Tutors rotate out, you might see Ancestral Knowledge in trick decks.

### The Land Tax digression, which is the best part

Taylor uses Ancestral Knowledge's deck-thinning to correct a widespread misunderstanding about
**Land Tax**.

Many articles had been written stressing that Land Tax thins your deck. **That isn't the true power
of Land Tax. Land Tax draws cards. Plain and simple.** Land is a resource just like a spell, and
stressing the deck-thinning attribute misses the huge card engine going on.

Taking advantage of it is relatively easy, most commonly with **Armageddon**. After you Armageddon,
it doesn't matter how thin your deck is. The most powerful aspect of Land Tax was drawing the extra
cards so you can play out a bunch of land after the Armageddon.

**A card is a card, even land, and when you are drawing more cards you are generating card
advantage.** If you want, there are easy ways to convert Land Tax's lands into higher quality cards,
with Brainstorm for instance, but it is still the *amount* of cards you draw that is the true power.

His closing logic: if the power of Land Tax were all in deck-thinning, then Ancestral Knowledge would
be a good card after all. But it isn't.

## Notes for use

**The idea to take away.** Mana and time are the same resource viewed two ways, and the one-land-
per-turn rule is what makes them convertible. Every efficiency question in deckbuilding is a question
about which of the two limitations you are trying to beat.

**Why this predates and underpins the rest of the library.** Taylor's two limitations map directly
onto later work. The draw-one-per-turn limit is the card advantage tradition (Weissman, Hahn). The
land-per-turn limit is what becomes tempo, the fundamental turn (Zvi, Block 2 file 04), Turbo Xerox
(Block 3), and Duke's Level One tempo installments. Chapin's *Theory of Everything* (Block 5) is an
attempt to unify exactly these two axes plus a third.

**The transferable diagnostic.** Taylor's real method is not the Memory Lapse conclusion, it's the
procedure: take two cards that look interchangeable, ask which resource each one accumulates, then
ask which resource your deck is built to accumulate. Run that on any pair of near-substitutable
cards and you'll usually find they belong in different decks.

**The Land Tax lesson generalizes hardest.** "Deck thinning" is still a phrase people reach for to
justify cards that don't draw. Taylor's correction is 29 years old and still catches people:
count the cards, not the thinning.

**Reading caution.** The card pool is 1997. Memory Lapse, Power Sink, Ancestral Knowledge, and Land
Tax are the vehicles, not the point. Read for the framework and translate.
