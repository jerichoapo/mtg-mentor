# Who's The Beatdown II: Multitasking

**Zvi Mowshowitz — Brainburst, February 20, 2003**

> Why it's in the list: this is where the word **inevitability** enters Magic's vocabulary, and it is the
> article that breaks the two-role model in Flores's original open. Flores himself ranks it **above**
> *Who's the Beatdown?* — in *Flores Friday: Top 11, Top 12* he says he rated it higher than *Clear the
> Land and the Fundamental Turn* too. Read it immediately after
> [01](01-whos-the-beatdown-1999-04-13.md) and [02](02-eight-core-principles-of-whos-the-beatdown-2012-03-16.md).

---

## The setup: Flores gave us the vocabulary, not the whole map

Zvi opens by crediting the original directly. He recommends reading Flores first, notes it references old
cards but is easy to follow regardless, and then makes the concession that matters:

> **I had already figured out much of what he says, but I only knew it instinctively and didn't have the
> right vocabulary with which to talk about it. Flores fleshed that out, providing a better way of thinking
> about and especially talking about the phenomenon.**

That is the whole relationship between the two articles in one sentence. Flores supplied language for
something good players already did. Zvi's project is to show that the language was too coarse.

His objection is specific. **Attacking for damage is only one way of gaining an advantage**, and treating
"the beatdown" as a role defined by dealing damage smuggles in an assumption. As he puts it, all of the
ways to gain advantage, damage included, **can be control or beatdown in the proper context**. So the
central question is not who the beatdown is. It is:

> **Who has inevitability?**

## The definition

Zvi states it formally, and this is the sentence the rest of Magic strategy quotes:

> **Definition: A player is said to have inevitability if and only if from the current position he will win
> a long game. A player is said to have inevitability in a matchup if and only if they have inevitability
> on turn one.**

He immediately concedes the definition is vague, and defends the vagueness as productive:

> **Yes, that definition is vague in some ways, but I think it is a good kind of vague because I have
> productively used all the different interpretations I can think of to describe different situations.**

Two things are doing work here. First, **inevitability is positional, not fixed** — it is evaluated "from
the current position," so it can change hands mid-game. Second, the matchup-level version is just the
position-level version measured on turn one, which means deck construction is a fight over inevitability
before a card is played.

**Inevitability is not binary.** Zvi is explicit that it "can easily be thought of in degrees." His example
is a mirror match where one player holds dead cards. If your edge is that the opponent has two extra
copies of Force Spike, letting them draw one extra card wipes out the whole margin. If they *also* have
eight dead creature-removal cards, then short of disaster you can do whatever you like. Same structural
advantage, wildly different tolerances.

## How inevitability relates to the two old roles

Zvi maps his concept onto Flores's frame rather than discarding it:

- Inevitability is **a close variant of the control role**, not identical to it.
- If your opponent has inevitability, **you cannot play control** without first radically altering the
  situation.
- If you have it, they are the one who can't, which forces them into the beatdown role **if the
  inevitability is strong enough**.
- **A control war is a war for inevitability**, even when one player is nominally taking the beatdown role.

The qualifier "if the inevitability is strong enough" is the degrees point again. A thin edge does not
force your opponent to abandon a controlling plan.

## The worked example: Psychatog

Zvi uses Psychatog as the clean case, then uses it again as the case that breaks cleanness.

**Why Psychatog has inevitability, two separate ways:**

1. **Psychatog plus Upheaval.** If the game goes long it wins automatically off that combination, unless
   facing another blue deck, "in which case things become complicated."
2. **Psychatog alone.** Every card the deck uses gives it an extra effect, while the opponent's cards are
   simply lost. If the enemy has nothing to compete with that effect, **all a Psychatog deck has to do is
   trade off and it will win.**

He notes the ease with which the deck seizes this advantage *during deck construction* may be its biggest
edge — inevitability bought in the deckbuilding phase rather than won at the table.

**Then the complication.** Take Red/Green against Psychatog in Standard. It looks maximally clear-cut:
Red/Green beats down, Psychatog controls. Zvi's answer is that it comes close, but is more fluid than that,
and the reason is a genuinely strange feature of the matchup:

> **The odd part of this matchup is that the Psychatog player's plan is to 'give back' his advantage to
> gain board position by pitching cards to Psychatog.**

The only Red/Green creatures the Tog cannot handle that way are **Phantom Centaur** and **Grim Lavamancer**.
And when Grim Lavamancer starts aiming at Psychatog instead of at the opponent's face, that is, in Zvi's
words, **a sure sign that the focus of the game has shifted**. Both players are now in an exhaustion war.

The reversal he draws out of this is the sharpest passage in the article:

> **If both players are constantly casting spells and the winner is the one whose spells pack a larger
> total punch, then who is who?**

Red/Green will win **when its opponent runs out of cards, not when it deals twenty damage**. By redirecting
the opponent's attacks from his life total onto his cards, the Psychatog player has **sacrificed the
beatdown war in order to force the fight onto the control war**. Both players are now competing for the
beatdown role. Zvi anticipates the objection — Red/Green is still attacking, so surely it is still the
beatdown — and answers it flatly:

> **But what is the difference between taking away cards and drawing them? The fact that he is attacking to
> force the result is incidental.**

And if the pressure is sustained enough, the roles invert outright: **the opponent has inevitability**, and
Psychatog must become the aggressor, tapping out to draw cards. Psychatog is a deck that must be understood
as switching roles, because the card itself lets you seize the beatdown role **at the cost of so much
inevitability that the control role can be put in danger**.

## The thesis

Stated plainly, twice — Zvi pulled it out as a callout in the original:

> **The whole idea of Who's The Beatdown isn't that you need to know which of these two roles you're
> taking, it's that you need to know what each player needs to accomplish to win.**

He credits the underlying instruction to Finkel, by way of Flores: **focus only on what matters**. Beatdown
and control are simply **the most likely pair of goals** in a given game. They are **in no way mandatory,
and they are certainly not static in time.**

## Seizing both roles

The second Psychatog example is the one that shows what happens when the model fully breaks. Go back to
before Judgment and Wonder, and look at Psychatog against Blue/Green — specifically at **Merfolk Looter**.

If a Looter activates without much Fact or Fiction having happened, the roles **immediately reverse**.
Blue/Green is drawing two cards a turn and now has inevitability in a big way. But Blue/Green also had a
strong beatdown game from the start. So:

> **By keeping that Looter on the table, Blue/Green has accomplished something great: He has seized BOTH
> roles.**

Zvi already flagged this outcome earlier in the piece: if someone holds both roles at once, **that is a
devastating advantage, and that player will almost always win the game.** Attacking into him is suicide,
and he is sealing the long game at the same time.

The Psychatog player's only out is to fight for the right to beat down **in the form of card advantage** —
which is why Zvi hated seeing **Deep Analysis** in that matchup. Without it the position is hopeless. With
it, the Psychatog player can at least be **the player doing something to disturb the status quo**.

He then names the trick he has just performed:

> **Note that card drawing and card advantage was just referred to within one page as being both the
> beatdown role in one situation and the control role in another.**

Which role any form of advantage occupies **depends on the context**. Attacking for two is normally
beatdown, but against Necropotence it can be a form of control. Drawing cards is usually control, but
**in control wars, drawing extra cards is the ultimate form of beatdown.**

## Countersliver: the roles invert on the board state

The third example pushes it to the extreme. Put Jason Zila's White/Blue control deck from the first Masters
against Countersliver. **Whose side is time on? That depends on how many Slivers are on the table.**

With an empty board, each turn favours the control deck. That is the situation it was built for. Zvi then
derives a constraint on the control deck's viability:

> **Doing nothing MUST be a route to victory for him.**

If the Countersliver player wins a game in which he has nothing on the table for an extended period, the
control player never had a chance to begin with. So the control deck's job is **guarding every opening at
every stage**, making sure the opponent can't profitably move until it's ready to kill.

**Now put two Slivers on the table** — a Muscle Sliver and a Crystalline Sliver, five damage a turn. Yes,
Countersliver is attacking and the control deck is trying to stop it. Reverse that and it makes more sense:

> **The Countersliver player is trying to preserve the status quo... He has control of the game right now,
> and he wants to keep it.**

He is the control player now, and the control deck **must become the active player**, beating down with
test spells and threats. Zvi flags his own terminology problem here — **be careful not to confuse "control
player" with "control role"** — and notes that the existing term **aggro-control** was already a hint that
this was possible.

He adds a related measure, **"winning on the table"**: who would win if there were a Standstill out that
neither player could ever break. It only matters when the player currently losing on the table **can't
change the situation at will** — because his hand is empty, or because he is facing counters. Between two
Yawgmoth's Bargain decks it makes zero difference who wins a long game.

### The historical footnote

Two real results, offered as evidence rather than illustration:

- **Masters at Nice, finals.** Alexander Witt on Miracle Grow against Justin Gary on Oath. Gary would
  normally take the control role, but his sideboard lacked removal for **Winter Orb**, so Witt would win
  the long game. Gary had to board in **Call of the Herd** and go beatdown despite being ill-prepared for
  it. He lost — but Zvi's verdict is that **a small chance is better than none.**
- **Zila's own final** against Tradewind-Survival: he lost because he could not convert a neutral table
  into a win **before his Mana Leaks went dead**, which left him without answers to Back to Basics.

## The close

Zvi's summary reframes the entire game:

> **In a sense, Magic can be looked at as the quest to obtain roles, and those roles are highly flexible at
> every level.**

Roles change between matchups, decks, games, and **between turns**. In all but the most clear-cut matchups
such switches will be frequent. He picks up Flores's own sideboard point — that the best sideboards don't
only add good cards but **change or reverse your overall strategy** — and notes that part of that is
changing the role you intend to play.

His verdict on the original article is generous and damning at once:

> **Assigning one player the beatdown role and another the control role in a matchup is valuable and will
> often grant new insight, but it only scratches the surface of the problem.**

And the skill claim:

> **Arguably the most valuable skill in Magic is the ability to recognize what matters so that one may, as
> Finkel says, focus on it.**

The reason he was still fascinated after ten years is that the game **forces you to re-examine what
matters**. A single game might start as a creature rush, become a card advantage war, then be about counter
allocation, then about mana denial to stop Urza's Rage, then finally about cards left in each library.
**Knowing in advance how all of this is going to play out is necessary if you are to decide how to play
such a game.**

## Notes for use

- **Read this as the middle panel of a triptych.** [Flores 1999](01-whos-the-beatdown-1999-04-13.md) names
  the roles. Zvi here shows the roles are **contested, contextual, and reversible**, and adds inevitability
  as the underlying quantity. [Flores 2012](02-eight-core-principles-of-whos-the-beatdown-2012-03-16.md)
  then folds inevitability back into a formalised eight-principle version — and explicitly credits this
  article for the term.
- **Dated card pool, undated argument.** Psychatog, Merfolk Looter, and Countersliver are Odyssey- and
  Tempest-era references. Nothing in the reasoning depends on them. Substitute any modern engine card that
  converts resources into an inexorable win and the analysis runs unchanged.
- **The "seizing both roles" idea is the practical takeaway.** Most matchup analysis stops at assigning
  roles. Zvi's version asks a better question at every turn: what does each player need to accomplish, and
  has anything just changed hands?
- **Connects forward to Block 5.** Zvi's insistence that every form of advantage is convertible into every
  other, depending on context, is the seed of the resource-unification project he ran under the name
  *Grand Unified Theory* — see
  [block-5-advanced-synthesis/04-grand-unified-theory-philosophy-of-fire-2004-05-03.md](../block-5-advanced-synthesis/04-grand-unified-theory-philosophy-of-fire-2004-05-03.md).
  Chapin's *Theory of Everything* is the endpoint of the same line.
- **Connects back to Block 2's opening.** Rob Hahn's 1996
  [Schools of Magic](00-schools-of-magic-1996-04-10.md) already distinguishes **Active** from **Reactive**
  strategies and insists resources compress to life, cards, and mana. Zvi's inevitability is what you get
  when you ask which side of that split wins if nothing changes.
