# Schools of Magic: General Philosophies

**Robert Hahn — version 5.4, last modified April 10, 1996**

> Why it's in the list: Flores ranks it **#2** of all time and calls Hahn "Plato to Brian Weissman's
> Socrates." It's where players first learned about **card advantage**, about exhausting threats from the
> opponent's deck, and where intuitive habits got **structure, names, and a way to clock them**. It held
> the title of "best article of all time" for roughly four years — an eternity when the game was five
> years old.

---

## Where this sits

**This is the oldest document in the collection and the root of the rest of it.** Everything in Blocks 2
and 5 is downstream: Eric Taylor's tempo argument, Flores's role assignment, Zvi's fundamental turn,
Chapin's resource taxonomy. Chapin's own intellectual history in Block 5 names Hahn in the founding
generation.

**Read it as an artifact as well as an argument.** The card pool is 1996 Type I and Type II, the
restricted list is different, and Hahn is writing about a game whose competitive culture barely existed
yet. What survives is the *method*: a way of describing decks by the philosophy that governs them rather
than by the cards in them.

---

## The premise: why write this at all

**Hahn's account of how players develop** is the frame for the whole document.

> **Most players begin with a fascination for the big heavy-hitters.** Think back to the first time you saw
> a Lord of the Pit or a Force of Nature and you might dimly recall that feeling of awe and excitement that
> ran through you.

**The stages he describes:**

1. **Beginner** — stuffing every useful card into a 150-card deck, two-hour games ending in a massive
   creature rush. **This phase ends quickly** once they meet a tightly focused 60-card deck.
2. **Intermediate** — arrives at the first tournament, running into people who understand timing and other
   arcane subjects. **These players explore strategies:** mono-colours, hand destruction, land destruction,
   fast resource, weenie.
3. **Beyond** — *"an extremely difficult process which involves understanding the nuances of the various
   rules, experience with many different types of decks, and the ability to see the synergy between certain
   cards."*

> **At this level, a certain amount of theoretical knowledge is helpful** to the intermediate player, and
> the exploration of these concepts might be useful to those who wish **to go beyond a specific deck or a
> specific strategy and see the strategies and the philosophies of Magic at a higher level.**

### The observation that motivates the whole document

> **One phenomenon I observed was that the best decks do not rely on a single theme or strategy.**

> **Discard is a very strong strategy, but a mono-black discard deck using every card that could make the
> opponent discard a card is not as strong as some mix of discard and other elements.**

> **Those decks which have successfully integrated different archetype strategies into a complementary
> scheme are the ones which seem to dominate play and win consistently against a wide variety of
> opponents. Many of these decks operate on a coherent philosophy about the game — a philosophy which
> guides every phase of game-play.**

## What counts as a "school"

**Hahn is strict about this, and the definition is the useful part:**

> **A deck or a particular insight about the game is not a "school." Having an effective strategy alone does
> not elevate a deck to the heights of being a school of Magic.**

> **Furthermore, I do not judge a school by the percentage performance of its decks or by the record of its
> founder — though clearly, both are factors to evaluate.**

> **Rather, a school is one in which a set of coherent principles about the game guides every phase of
> play: from deck construction, to general play, to situational play, and combination plays.**

**The martial-arts analogy he uses throughout:** *"each school uses the same tools, but focuses on
different approaches to the game. And like martial arts, some are easier to learn than others and some may
be more effective than others as well."*

**And an important instruction for reading it:**

> **Playing a Type II deck based on a certain school does not mean trying to replace Type I cards with
> their Type II substitutes.** The Type II Kim deck is G/W without a trace of red or blue, **but it
> incorporates a number of the insights of the Kim school.**

> **I hope this entire project will inspire others to look to the central *principles* as opposed to the
> simple deck contents of a particular school.**

**On the naming:** schools are named after those *identified* with a strategy through advocacy or first
description, **not necessarily those who invented it.** Hahn explicitly notes Garry Handelman "readily
admits is not *his* original thought but collaborative with others in his area."

# Definitions and general concepts

**This section is the reason the article is foundational.** It is an attempt to standardise vocabulary for
higher-level discussion, and much of it stuck.

## Resource

> **Something which allows you to play the game.** In Magic there are the following resources: **life,
> cards in hand, cards in library, cards in graveyard, permanents in play** (further divided into
> creatures, artifacts, land, enchantments). **In tournament situations you must add *time* as a
> resource.**

**And the compression that matters:**

> **In higher level play, I've found that only three resources really count — life, cards, and mana. Most
> of the effective strategies work on one of the last two areas.**

*(Life, cards, mana. Fourteen years before Chapin's Theory of Everything arrives at the same trichotomy
from a different direction — see `block-5-advanced-synthesis/07-innovations-the-theory-of-everything-2010-01-12.md`.)*

## Speed

Broken into **Resource Speed, Offensive Speed, Defensive Speed, and Strategic Speed**, with a separate
companion document (*Theories of Deck Speed*) treating it at length.

## Offense and defense

**Offense** — *"those elements which will make it possible for a deck to win."*

**Defense** — and this definition is broader than most players' intuition:

> **A surprisingly nebulous concept which I describe as "disruption."** Obviously staying alive despite
> opponent efforts is disruption of their game. **But things like card-denial or a first-turn Black Vise or
> Strip Mining their crucial color lands are also disruptive, and therefore defensive.**

> **Note that defense often incorporates huge offensive elements.**

## Efficiency, at three scales

| Scale | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Cards** | **The effect and utility of a card given its casting cost.** *"Lightning Bolt is more efficient than an Incinerate."* |
| **Decks** | **The ability to utilize every card as soon as it is drawn.** A weenie/blaster deck has high efficiency; *"a blue permission deck with Mahamotis cannot be said to have particularly high efficiency."* |
| **Strategies** | **The ability to implement primary goals compared to the time and resource cost of implementing them.** |

## Flexibility, at three scales

| Scale | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Cards** | **Usefulness in multiple situations.** *"Disenchant is more flexible than Divine Offering, since it can handle enchantments."* |
| **Decks** | **The ability to deal with multiple opposing strategies** — can it defend against weenies, blaster, big creatures, offensive discard, *and* still implement its own offense? |
| **Strategies** | **Having multiple ways of implementing deck flexibility.** A pure permission deck that counters everything **has greater deck flexibility**; a U/W deck that can counter *or* Disenchant *or* Swords later **has greater strategic flexibility.** |

## Active / Reactive

> **Active decks try to take charge right from the beginning, while reactive decks usually attempt to
> respond to opponent threats and wait for the appropriate opening.**

*(This is the distinction Flores would later build "Who's the Beatdown?" on top of, and Duke would teach as
proactive versus reactive — see `block-2-core-theory-paired/01-whos-the-beatdown-1999-04-13.md` and
`block-1-the-spine/15-threats-and-answers-2014-09-08.md`.)*

# The four theories of sideboarding

**Hahn presents these as an ascending ladder of sophistication.** This section predates the material in
Block 1's sideboard installments by nineteen years and covers most of the same ground.

## 1. Color-specific sideboards

> **The place that most Magic players begin.** Cards which shut down or hose a specific color — Gloom or
> Stromgald Cabal against White, Karma against Black, Red Elemental Blasts against Blue.

**His example of the extreme case:** *"the sideboard belonging to a friend which contains TEN Circles of
Protection — two for each color."*

## 2. Strategy-specific sideboards

> **The next level in theoretical complexity. The idea is to counter a *type* of strategy which poses a
> threat** — four Psychic Purge for discard decks, Wrath of God for weenie decks.

**Where it gets hard:** building a sideboard against a *complex* opponent. Against a Kim deck,

> **Simply putting in a CoP: Red would be disastrous**, as the Kim deck has four Disenchants, six
> counterspells, and plenty of non-red damage. **A color-specific sideboard would fare poorly.**

**The principle he extracts:**

> **The successful strategy-specific sideboards are those which identify the vulnerabilities in a
> particular strategy and build a sideboard to exploit those vulnerabilities.**

> **Against a complex multi-strategy deck, it means choosing something which can affect as many of them as
> possible, but if not, the most important one.**

**Applied:** against a Kim deck, *"the vulnerability of all of its strategies is that they are all fairly
mana-intensive"* — so **Blood Moon, Armageddon, Infernal Darkness, Strip Mine, Winter Orb.**

## 3. Transformative sideboards ("Surprise!" sideboards)

> **The idea is to transform the deck completely through sideboarding. The new strategy often catches the
> opponent by surprise and unprepared.**

**His worked example**, which he was on the receiving end of: an apparently creatureless R/W Millstone deck
with Bolts, Swords, Disenchants, Pyroclasm, Wrath of God.

> In the second game I sideboarded out most of my anti-creature cards for more anti-artifact and
> anti-blaster cards. **Suddenly I see Orders of Leitbur, Uthden Trolls, Serra Angels, Shivan Dragons — and
> no Millstone and no direct damage of any kind. My Disenchants sat in my hand as I was being pummeled.**

**The sideboard turned out to be almost entirely creatures:** 4 Order of Leitbur, 4 Uthden Troll, 2 Serra
Angel, 2 Shivan Dragon, 3 Divine Offering.

**And the honest cost:**

> **This type of sideboarding is a bit risky. Few decks are designed to perform as well in two forms. Even
> a slight inefficiency can cost a deck in a close game, so it becomes necessary to test the deck in all of
> its forms.**

> **Plus, if the opponent does not play a reactive sideboarding strategy transforming his own deck into
> something which cannot handle yours, then you may have lost the edge.**

*(Reid Duke reaches the same verdict in 2015 — transform sideboards are a hail mary, not a plan. See
`block-1-the-spine/39-the-sideboard-2015-08-10.md`.)*

## 4. Enhancement sideboards

**The subtlest of the four, and the one Hahn associates with the strongest players.**

> **The idea is not to try and "hose" a color or a strategy but merely to enhance one's own main strategy's
> ability to handle them.**

**The distinction, in one comparison:**

> **An Abyss hoses a weenie deck, whereas a Wrath of God merely enhances the ability to deal with a weenie
> deck.**

**Why it matters for defensive decks:** hoser cards can backfire or make the deck less efficient. The
Weissman deck *could* bring in Blood Moon against Kim — **"that would hose the Kim strategy as much as it
can be hosed. However, it would also slow down the Weissman strategy."** Bringing in Divine Offering
instead, reasoning that Jade Statues die alongside all other artifacts, is an *enhancement*.

> **Enhancement sideboards seem to be more popular among experienced players who are not comfortable with
> changing the basic character of their main deck with massive sideboarding. The idea seems to be that the
> basic strategy works and works well, so why change it completely?**

**Hahn's own worked application** in Type II is instructive because he uses two different theories against
two different opponents in the same sideboard:

- **Against mono-black Necropotence** (nine cards) — Karma, White Knights: **a color-specific hose**,
  because *"there is no other way for my deck to deal with it."*
- **Against other U/W decks** (four cards) — 2 Divine Offering, 2 Disrupting Sceptre: **pure enhancement**,
  simply replacing cards that are useless in the matchup (Wrath of God) with useful ones.
- **Two general cards** — Control Magic and Power Sink, on the reasoning that **"countermagic is the most
  enhancing of any defensive strategy as it hoses nothing but enhances everything."**

**And a note on the Type II metagame of the day that is pure rock-paper-scissors:**

> **Mono-black Necropotence decks usually run over U/W defensive decks, which usually beat R/G Ernham &
> Burn'em decks, which usually beat the mono-black Necropotence decks. As a result, my sideboard ignores
> burn decks completely.**

# The Schools

## The Weissman School — "The Deck"

**Originated by Brian Weissman.** The most influential deck ever built, and the one Hahn treats at greatest
length.

### The philosophy

> **The basic philosophy of the Weissman School is that defense wins games.** While most decks play both
> phases of Magic — defending against opponent threats and mounting an offense — **the Weissman School
> plays only the former.**

**His volleyball analogy:**

> **It doesn't matter how many points you give up as long as you only give up fewer than 15. In the same
> way, the Weissman School will happily give up 19 points of life as long as the final 20th point is
> denied.**

### Weissman in his own words

**Hahn quotes him directly, and this passage is the clearest statement of the idea:**

> **What I believe really sets "The Deck" apart from its competitors is its overall objective: survival.
> "The Deck" virtually forgets about its opponent, and concentrates only on establishing itself and
> building card advantage.**

> **Since it is only trying to survive — a goal that every deck has to achieve — it only has to do half the
> work and is not vulnerable to the billions of cards that hose offensive strategies.**

> **I am not going to lose to Moat or Abyss or Blood Moon like Handelman's deck. I am not going to lose to
> Blood Moon, Moat, and COP Red like Kim's deck. I am not going to lose to Ivory Tower and COP Red like
> Chang's deck. All these decks suffer the same problem, that if a threat arrives that is beyond their
> measure to deal with, they are finished.**

> **You simply concentrate on survival and drawing cards, and winning — through two angels or a
> Braingeyser — takes care of itself.**

### Hahn's correction to Weissman

**A sharp piece of analysis worth keeping:**

> **The Weissman deck does not build "card advantage" per se — that would be a misnomer. It builds card
> *denial*.**

> **It would be insufficient for the Weissman strategy to draw 2 cards to the opponent's 1. The opponent
> must have no cards in hand, and the Weissman player must have a Disrupting Sceptre in play with a couple
> of Counterspells as backup.**

### How it works

**Efficiency** comes from white defense (Swords, Disenchant) meaning it *need not counter everything*, and
from **group destruction — one card stopping many** (Moat stops all ground attackers, Disrupting Sceptre
stops essentially all countermagic).

**Flexibility** comes from countermagic, which can answer anything.

**Offense is deliberately minimal:** two Serra Angels, one Braingeyser, a sideboard Fireball, one Mirror
Universe.

**On recursion**, which the four-card limit forces: **Feldon's Cane, Recall, Regrowth, Timetwister** exist
to recycle spent countermagic.

**The win:** *"achieve a sceptre lock where the opponent has no cards in hand… once the lock is achieved,
the offensive strategy can kick into gear and a Serra Angel will decimate the opponent very quickly."*

**And a design principle that recurs across the whole document:**

> **The Weissman deck strives to have a key card neutralize an entire offensive strategy.** Moat, Balance,
> Abyss all hose creatures. Ivory Tower poses serious problems for blaster and weenie decks. **This style
> of deckbuilding attempts to neutralize the Offensive Overkill theory under which the more
> offensive-minded schools operate.**

### The Jester's Cap debate

**Hahn stages an actual argument between the principals, which is the best part of the section.**

**Hahn's position:** the Cap is the key vulnerability. Removing three of four Disenchants or three of four
Swords wrecks the defense; removing the Angels and Braingeyser wrecks the offense.

**Weissman's rebuttal:** the countermagic and anti-artifact spells make it *"exceedingly difficult for any
Cap to be used,"* and key cards may already be safely in hand.

**Maysonet's counter-rebuttal:** *"no one worth his salt as a player would ever play a Cap without six mana
on the board,"* which makes the Disenchants useless — and being Capped means the Cap player **only has to
neutralize the one or two cards that could win the game.**

**Hahn's verdict:** the Cap does pose a significant threat, *"perhaps necessitating a couple of Caps in the
Weissman deck itself. This is my current view."*

### The Chang critique, and Weissman's answer

**Warren Chang proposed four changes.** Weissman rejected them, and his answer contains a genuinely good
piece of strategic reasoning.

| Chang's proposal | Weissman's response |
|---|---|
| Cut Serra Angels for Racks | **Rejected** |
| Moat → The Abyss | **Rejected** (would kill your own Angels) |
| Add life gain | partially adopted (Mirror Universe) |
| Add Millstones | not adopted |

**Weissman on why the Angels must stay** — the argument is about denying the opponent good sideboarding:

> **I NEED creatures in my deck to prevent this from happening, as I give my opponent far too great of an
> advantage if he gets to sideboard out all of his creature defense.**

**And "The Deck Gambit":**

> **Contrary to popular belief, the "only play the Serra in the lock" rule is far from an absolute. Around
> here we have a name for an early Angel, called "The Deck Gambit."** I don't know how many hapless
> opponents I have killed with this. **They figure that once an Angel hits the table they are dead anyway,
> so they sideboard out all their Plowshares and die miserably when I summon an Angel on the second turn.**

> **The Rack does less damage than an Angel, cannot ever block or be Plowshared for life, and the last
> thing on earth I want to do is let my opponent's extra sideboard artifact defense act as damage
> prevention.**

> **Knowing when to play an Angel is just as vital a skill as knowing when to counter something; there are
> never absolute rules for either. I have found time and time again that two Angels are often excessive,
> and I will very often let one or both be Plowshared to maintain a positional advantage.**

**On who should play it:**

> **I believe that beginning players have no business with the Weissman school — they will only become
> frustrated after losing to far inferior decks. It is a mentally demanding and challenging deck to play.
> However, for intermediate and expert players looking to improve their playing skills, the Weissman School
> is one of the best training grounds.**

## The Kim School

**Originated by John Kim**, an outgrowth of the Balance deck.

### The three deckbuilding principles

**These are stated as universal, not Kim-specific, and they are the most quotable part of the document.**

**1. Every card must be maximally useful.**

> **This means that it can't be just useful, or useful against certain types of decks, but be the MOST
> useful card against MOST types of decks.** Shatter is a useful card, **but Disenchant is maximally
> useful.** Spirit Link is useful in many situations, **but Swords to Plowshares is maximally useful, as
> Time Elementals don't hurt you by attacking you.**

**2. No reliance on combinations.**

> **You want to build in the *possibility* of a combination but you don't ever want to be in a position to
> be *relying* upon it.** Initiates + Drain Life is a wonderful idea and a great combo if you could pull it
> off, **but heaven forbid someone should kill the Initiate.**

**3. Minimize mana requirements.**

> **As many of your spells should be the lowest casting cost possible, AND as little colored mana should be
> required.** Sometimes a Flash Counter is better than a Counterspell — against a pure red blaster deck —
> **simply because it costs one less blue mana.**

### The corollaries

**On multicolour decks:** *"unless it is central to your play, do not put in 4 Juzam Djinns, 4 Mahamoti
Djinns, and 4 Shivan Dragons. Each of those cards require 2 mana of a certain color… A well-placed Strip
Mine may send you spiralling into defeat."*

**On what "maximally useful" implies:**

> **Any serious tournament deck must be able to deal with creatures, artifacts, enchantments, and direct
> damage.**

**On threats — and this is the passage Flores would echo for a decade:**

> **Every serious tournament deck must be able to pose multiple threats.**

> **A Jade Statue by itself poses a threat, and a Wrath of God also poses a threat in and of itself.
> Together, you have a combination which deprives your opponent of any creatures while you still have a
> 3/6 nastiness. But each card is a self-contained threat.**

> **Contrast a Hymn to Tourach and the Rack. Together, they cause grievous injury and may win the game, but
> separately the Hymn poses a minimal threat at most and the Rack by itself does nothing.**

**And the note that defense counts as a threat:**

> **The Weissman Deck has very few "offensive" cards, but its effectiveness lies in the independent threat
> of each and every defensive card.**

**On artifacts, as a consequence of principle 3:**

> **Given the choice between a Hypnotic Specter and a Disrupting Sceptre, the Kim school would recommend
> the Disrupting Sceptre, despite its activation cost and lack of damage ability. At the least, the opponent
> must use an anti-artifact card on the Sceptre, as opposed to using one of the numerous anti-creature cards
> available.**

### The deckbuilding checklist

**Hahn ends the section with questions to ask of any deck** — a genuinely durable exercise:

> **What will I do if a Blood Moon drops? What about an Energy Flux? What about a CoP: Black/Red/Green?
> What about a Moat? Stasis? Black Vise? Strip Mines? What if I'm facing a first-turn Juzam? A lot of
> weenies very early on?**

### How it differs from Weissman

> **Weissman emphasizes card dominance as the means to achieving a lock, whereas Kim emphasizes a direct
> route to the end — killing one's opponent.**

> **Card superiority in and of itself is merely a means to an end — rendering one's opponent defenseless and
> disrupting their game. But it does not kill one's opponent. Damage does.**

**Its five interlocking threats:** creature attack (Mishra's Factory, Jade Statue, Rasputin, Serra) ·
direct damage (Bolt, Fireball) · anti-permanent defense (Disenchant, Swords, Balance) · countermagic (Mana
Drain, Counterspell, REB) · speed.

> **They are finely woven together in that many combinations are possible… However, the deck relies on none
> of these combinations for its effectiveness.**

**On difficulty:**

> **It takes a great deal of skill to play the Kim deck properly — even more skill, perhaps, than the
> Weissman deck.** The Weissman player only needs to play defense until the lock arrives. **The Kim player
> must play the fast offense which sets the tone of the game while maintaining adequate defense.**

**Its vulnerability:** *"Moat + CoP: Red + Blood Moon will shut down the Kim deck almost completely"* —
though Hahn notes a three-card combo is no easy accomplishment. **Blood Moon alone is the serious threat**,
which is why basic lands belong in the sideboard.

## The Handelman School

**Credited to Frank Trollman, Sam "Garry" Handelman, and one other.** A U/B discard deck built on creature
overkill.

### Offense Overkill Theory

**The core idea, and the one that gives the school its shape:**

> **If every defensive card takes out one offensive card, then the defensive player has not advanced
> towards winning as the game goes on, but the offensive player will reduce the number of defensive cards
> available to the opponent.**

**Summarised in one sentence:**

> **"I'm going to come kill you right now with this thing right here unless you do something about it."**

**Its natural enemy:** *"Global anti-creature cards, therefore, such as Moat / Wrath of God / The Abyss are
the greatest threats"* — precisely because they break the one-for-one assumption the theory rests on.

**Handelman's own correction to Hahn's initial reading**, which is worth noting:

> **Speed, while nice if you can get it, is not the important thing. The important thing is to keep your
> opponent defenseless over turns seven and eight.**

### Hahn's critique

**He is notably harder on this school than the others, and the criticism is specific:**

> **Reports from the Net and my own playtesting show that the Handelman deck lacks the consistency and the
> reliability that distinguishes the top-level designs.**

**Four named weaknesses:**

1. **No significant anti-permanent ability** — it relies on discard and permission, and with only eight
   white sources is *"extremely vulnerable to Blood Moon."*
2. **Reliance on creatures**, which are the most-answered permanent type: Moat, Abyss, Drop of Honey,
   Meekstone, Island Sanctuary, Maze of Ith, Wrath, Balance, Jokulhaups, and a City in a Bottle that ends
   *"just about all sources of damage."*
3. **The Spectres.** *"It's a well-known fact that 'spectre' is just another word for 'lightning rod.'"*
4. **Mana.** A B/U deck needing BB and UU consistently, running a low mana/spell ratio to ensure offensive
   cards appear.

**And the theoretical objection:**

> **All tournament decks have anti-creature measures, but the best have not only measures but well-planned
> strategies on dealing with them.** As such, **the Handelman deck requires extraordinary speed and some
> luck to overwhelm the opposition with brute force.**

### Handelman's tournament advice

**A separate, excellent idea that has nothing to do with his deck:**

> **Play the percentages. Don't worry about beating the best deck at the tournament you are going to.**

> **Chances are significantly less than 25% that any particular deck is making it to the finals — numbers
> outpower skill every time.** If there is an army of land destruction decks there, and one of the best
> editions of The Deck you've seen, **don't worry about beating Brian. Chances are excellent that he'll get
> unlucky and eliminated. Worry about beating the land destruction decks instead.**

> **Build a deck to beat the popular deck, rather than the dominant deck.**

*(This is metagame theory in 1996, and it's the same insight Duke teaches in
`block-1-the-spine/42-the-metagame-2015-06-01.md`.)*

## The Chang School

**Warren Chang's R/U/b Blood Moon deck.** Its distinctive contribution is a theory about *forcing role
reversal*.

### Chang in his own words

> **My basic philosophy is not only that the best defense is a good offense, but that multiple threats
> should be employed to force your opponent to play your game. That is the key, I believe, to winning
> tournament Magic.**

> **There are generally two types of decks: reactive and proactive… A proactive deck forces an opponent to
> react to your threats, to become reactive. If you're playing a proactive deck and force another offensive
> deck to respond to your threats — double Bolt a Juzam, toss to get under Vise — then you're halfway to
> your goal. You've disrupted your opponent's game plan.**

### The Blood Moon insight

**The sharpest line in the whole document, and a genuinely deep point about threats:**

> **Blood Moon is the ultimate disruptive strategy. A card that has minimal impact on me, completely alters
> the way my opponent plays his game, EVEN IF I NEVER CAST IT.**

**And the argument for Black Vise:**

> **And what if my opponent decides to Disenchant or counter one of my Vises? That's one less Disenchant
> for my Jades, or Blood Moon, or one less counter for my Timetwister, Mind Twist, Wheel, Fireball, or
> Blood Moon.**

### Sideboarding as attack surface

**Chang's most original contribution: making the *opponent's sideboarding* the vulnerability.**

> **After the first game, after my opponent sees a Blood Moon, he will reach into his sideboard for
> Sextants, Disenchants, Hydroblasts. Some might say that this is effective sideboarding — but what is he
> taking out for these?**

> **In most tourney decks there are no useless cards, so for the cost of 3 cards in my starting deck, I've
> forced my opponent to become reactive.**

> **In the first game my opponent saw Vises, Strip Mines, Mana Drain, Mind Twist, Blood Moon, Bolts,
> Psionic Blasts, and Jade Statues. Does he sideboard the COP Reds? The Disenchants and Offerings? The
> Sextants? The blue blasts and red blasts? Maybe he will sideboard 11 cards like the average tournament
> player did against me at the last NY Magic tournament.**

> **No tournament deck I've ever played can change 11 cards without changing its character.**

**Its vulnerabilities**, per Hahn: decks with significant lifegain (because the damage is *"almost
exclusively one-shot"*), Nether Void or Winter Orb decks that remove the speed advantage, and Jester's Cap
on the Blood Moons. **And fundamentally, the draw:** *"With a good draw — early Vise, Strip Mine, Blood
Moon — the mana-denial strategy can be put in place early… With a poor draw, the Chang deck could die a
horrible death."*

---

## The O'Brien School

**The shortest and bluntest school.**

> **As the Weissman and Handelman decks operate under the principle that "If you don't have any cards, you
> can't play," the O'Brien deck operates under the principle that "If you don't have any mana, you can't
> play."**

**The engine:** lay an early threat (Juzam, Juggernaut, Black Vise, Mishra's Factory), then resolve
**Nether Void** as fast as possible. *"Defense against the threats becomes virtually impossible, as a
Swords to Plowshares will cost W3."* Sinkhole, Icequake, and Strip Mine maintain the gap; Mana Vault keeps
the O'Brien deck functioning.

**O'Brien in his own words:**

> **This deck is almost entirely proactive. It basically cares little about the other side of the board
> except with respect to land. To sum it up: I am putting my opponent in my world, I am going to put down
> this Void NOW, on my first turn if possible, and we are going to play on my terms. I have had it with
> counters, fireballs etc. JUST DIE!!!**

## The Maysonet School — "Concede or Bleed"

**Adam Maysonet's U/W Jester's Cap deck.** It takes Weissman's premise and adds a third zone.

### The insight

> **Where the Weissman design focuses solely on the cards in hand and the cards in play, the Maysonet
> school also works on the cards in the *library*.**

> **The basic insight is that Magic is a card game. Luck plays a big factor. It doesn't matter that you
> have every single spoiler ever printed if you don't draw them.**

> **The Maysonet school contends that by disrupting the content of the opponent's library itself, you can
> decrease not only the effectiveness and the strategy of the opposing deck but ultimately destroy the
> strategy itself.**

### How the Cap is used, in priority order

1. **Remove the crucial cards** if the opponent relies on a particular card or lock. *Against Weissman: the
   Serra Angels and Braingeyser. Against Handelman: the Zur's Weirdings or the Hymns.*
2. **Remove cards which disrupt its own strategy** — anti-artifact, countermagic, discard.
3. **Remove power cards.** *"What happens to a power T1 deck without Ancestral Recall, Time Walk, and its
   Mana Drains or Library of Alexandria?"*

**And the intelligence value, which Hahn rates highly:**

> **The Cap allows the Maysonet player to examine the opponent's deck for any upcoming surprises… one side
> effect is that you can guess at the opponent's cards in hand with a fair amount of accuracy. If you saw
> Time Walk in game one and you don't see it in the library while you're searching, you can safely assume
> it's in their hand and prepare for it.**

> **Sideboarding is pointless against the Maysonet deck unless the cards are specifically to combat the
> Jester's Caps.**

**How it wins:** *"With continual Capping, the opponent usually concedes. After all, if all of your
offensive threats were removed, why would you continue with the 'game'?"* — hence the nickname.

### The practical warning

**A genuinely useful piece of tournament realism:**

> **In some tournaments, the Maysonet style is inappropriate while effective. The reason is time limits.
> Unless the opponent is gracious enough to concede, an average game may take forty-five minutes.**

> **Plus, there is the phenomenon of adjudication — if the opponent managed to score a few hits, even if he
> is helpless after three or four Caps he will be ahead in life. Oftentimes a judge may adjudicate against
> the Maysonet player who is FIRMLY in the driver's seat.**

**Its own vulnerability is symmetrical:** *"The biggest vulnerability is another Jester's Cap. In a strange
way, the Maysonet deck is just as reliant on its key cards as the Weissman deck."*

**The closing summary of the school:**

> **As much as the Weissman deck controls the immediate game environment by being unfriendly to permanents
> in play and cards in hand, the Maysonet deck attempts to control the *future* game environment** — by
> removing cards from play, and by letting the player know what to expect.

## Notes for use

- **This is where card advantage got structure.** Flores's assessment in
  `block-0-orientation/04-shoulders-of-giants-2013-08-06.md` is exactly right: players had intuitively
  jammed discard and land destruction into decks, and **Hahn gave them structure, names, and a way to
  clock it.** Hahn's three-resource compression — **life, cards, mana** — arrives at the same place Chapin
  reaches fourteen years later by a different route.
- **The definitions section is the most durable part.** Efficiency and flexibility split across *cards /
  decks / strategies*; defense redefined as **disruption**; active versus reactive. All three are still the
  working vocabulary.
- **The four sideboard theories predate everything in Block 1 by nineteen years and mostly hold up.**
  Color-specific → strategy-specific → transformative → enhancement is a real ladder. Hahn reaches Duke's
  conclusion on transform sideboards (risky, needs testing in both forms) and Duke's conclusion on
  enhancement (don't break your own deck to hose theirs).
- **The Kim School's three principles are the best deckbuilding checklist in the document** and translate
  directly: **maximally useful cards, no reliance on combinations, minimise coloured requirements.** The
  Hymn-and-Rack versus Jade-Statue-and-Wrath comparison is the cleanest statement anywhere of why
  self-contained threats beat combo pieces.
- **Chang's Blood Moon line is the one to memorise:** *a card that has minimal impact on me completely
  alters the way my opponent plays his game, even if I never cast it.* That's threat-as-information, and
  it's the same mechanism Duke teaches about holding lands back with a red deck
  (`block-5-advanced-synthesis/07-innovations-the-theory-of-everything-2010-01-12.md`).
- **Handelman's "beat the popular deck, not the dominant deck"** is metagame theory two decades early, and
  it's the argument Duke makes in Level One 42.
- **Watch how much of Block 2 is already latent here.** Weissman's "survival is half the work" is the
  ancestor of inevitability. Chang's forcing decks to become reactive is the ancestor of role assignment.
  Offense Overkill Theory is the ancestor of the Philosophy of Fire's card-for-life exchange. **None of
  these are named yet, and that's the point** — Hahn is describing the phenomena that the next fifteen
  years of writing would name.
- **Reading caution:** the card pool is 1996, Mind Twist and Chaos Orb bannings are recent news, Black Vise
  has just been unrestricted, and Necropotence is described as an emerging force. Hahn himself notes he was
  a law student writing this between exams, and that his Type II sections were weaker than his Type I. Read
  the philosophy; treat every card evaluation as history.
