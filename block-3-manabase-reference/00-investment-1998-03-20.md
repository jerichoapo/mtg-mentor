# Advanced Strategy: Investment

**Michael J. Flores — rec.games.trading-cards.magic.strategy, March 20, 1998. Republished on The Dojo.**

> Why it's in the list: this is the concept sitting underneath
> [Turbo Xerox](01-turbo-xerox-1997-06-27.md), and underneath every modern argument about cantrips,
> filtering, and how many "free" slots a manabase can absorb. Flores separates **card filtering that
> replaces itself** from **options that don't**, and shows why the difference decides which cards are
> playable at all.

---

## The question that opens it

Flores starts from an observation about the format rather than from a definition:

> **How many Jayemdae Tomes have you seen in Type II lately?**

Not many. With Browse gone he expected Jayemdae Tome to make a comeback, and it hadn't. His explanation is
that the decks that use Tome are usually blue-heavy, while decks that merely **splash** blue can use
**Whispers of the Muse**. And what makes Whispers so good is the thing he wants to name:

> **What makes Whispers of the Muse so good? Investment. That is, the Whispers does not force you to invest
> a card.**

**Investment** is the cost you pay in cards *committed to the board* before a card has done anything for
you. It is distinct from card advantage, symmetry, and synergy — the three terms he says the community was
already talking about — and he calls it "a long-neglected term of the game."

## The worked comparison: Tome versus Whispers

Two identical players, same match, same point in the game: **four cards in hand and six mana.** One holds
Jayemdae Tome, one holds Whispers of the Muse.

**The Tome player:**
- Taps **four of his six mana on his own turn** to cast it. He keeps two for Counterspell or Memory Lapse,
  but he has "reduced his options by four mana."
- Starts the opponent's turn with **three cards in hand.**

**The Whispers player:**
- Starts the opponent's turn with **four cards in hand**. Flores notes this matters on its own, because
  **blue Magic is often a bluffing game**.
- If Whispers resolves, he has **six cards on his next draw against the Tome player's four.**
- If he blows a Disk — "as blue players often do" — **he won't destroy his own Jayemdae Tome.**

Then the accounting point, which is the part people get wrong:

> **Were the game to end at this point, the Tome player would be effectively -2 in the card count when
> compared with the Whispers player.**

The intuitive objection is that the Tome player is only -1: he's -2 in hand but +1 permanent in play.
Flores says that is wrong, and gives the reason:

> **At this point in the game, the Tome player has done nothing with the Tome... it could have been a
> Pegasus Refuge or some other useless card.**

**A permanent that has not yet generated value is not a card. It is a promise.** That is the whole thesis.

He is careful not to overclaim: he is not slamming Jayemdae Tome, which he calls a fine card. He is
explaining **why it is not commonly played.**

## Instantaneous utility

The positive form of the rule:

> **In most cases, if you look at good players, the cards that they put investment into, that is, the cards
> that they put into play, generally give instantaneous utility.**

His example is **Serrated Arrows**: you put it down and it does something immediately. It always pokes a
pump knight after you side it in, and **if they Disenchant it, you just got a Hymn to Tourach out of it.**

The second worked case is **Soldevi Excavations versus Browse**. Why did the good big-blue decks run two to
four Excavations and no Browse, when Browse's effect is clearly more powerful in the long run?

- Excavations gives **immediate utility**.
- It doesn't even cost mana advantage, since the **1U it produces beats the U from an Island.**
- The same decks ran **multiple Impulses**, which were "like an immediate Browse in their own way."

Together, **Thawing Glaciers, Impulse and other cantrips, and Soldevi Excavations simulated Browse's card
advantage without the initial -1 investment.**

He runs the same comparison for **Ancestral Memories versus Browse**: either you play Browse and say
"done," generally not recouping the -1 investment for at least two turns, **or you tap one more mana for
Ancestral Memories and get +1 card advantage instead of -1 card investment.** When all you want out of it
is a Control Magic and a Force of Will, one extra mana is not much to ask for **"basically 2 Timewalks on
yourself."**

## When investment is unconditionally fine

Flores gives three exceptions, and they are narrow:

**1. Game-breaking cards.** An early **Ankh of Mishra** against most U/W control decks. They will Disenchant
it as soon as they can, and both players will have gone -1 in hand — symmetry maintained. **If you make
them play a land or more first, all the better.** A **Winter Orb** against a tapped-out opponent or a
Necrodeck works the same way, because you are **buying what amounts to multiple turns.**

**2. Creatures.** Playing a creature is automatic utility, because **almost every creature in the game can
block** — so on the turn it arrives, it can do something productive. He adds a synergy note: a deck with
small green creatures and Lhurgoyfs wants to **maintain creature symmetry by trading with the opposing
Sligh deck as much as possible, because it makes the long-term Lhurgoyf bigger.**

**3. Lands.** Land that doesn't come into play tapped **has instantaneous utility because it can make
mana.** Other lands have a specific purpose useful to your strategy.

## The failure mode: investment without long-run replacement

Then the core of the argument — **cards that require an investment but do not replace themselves in the
long run.** His two examples are **Ancestral Knowledge** and **Pegasus Refuge**.

**Ancestral Knowledge** is an immediate -1 in hand, like Jayemdae Tome, but unlike Tome it **cannot wreck
your opponent in the long run** (outside combo decks, which he explicitly excludes from the analysis). It
just ends up as -1 card advantage once you stop upkeeping it. Did it make you top-deck better? Sure. But:

> **Let's just say he is really good, and top-decks exactly as well as you do. In that case, you will remain
> more-or-less at relative parity, except he will be 1 card ahead of you. Guess who wins.**

Worse, using its ability to remove cards from the top ten of your library means **you just voluntarily gave
your opponent an additional road to victory.**

He then ranks it against two alternatives:

- **Portent.** Also helps you top-deck better, but **costs less mana, can be aimed at your opponent, and
  replaces itself.** He doesn't count it as giving the opponent a road to victory, because you are far more
  likely to win off an extra card in hand than they are to win off a one-card deficit in your inactive
  resources.
- **A Tutor.** Also -1 in the card count, but it **doesn't give your opponent an extra road to victory, is
  an instant, costs less mana, and guarantees you see the card** — whereas Ancestral Knowledge only raises
  the probability.

**Pegasus Refuge** gets the harshest treatment. It's -1 in hand for the ability to make 1/1 tokens, and
Flores calls it "a sealed deck card AT BEST."

> **To assume that the Pegasus Refuge is viable is to say that every card the opponent will draw is inferior
> to a 1/1 (quite a bold statement, wouldn't you say?) *and* that you can win with a -1 card advantage.**

The contrast is **Sacred Mesa**, which is also -1 in the short run but has long-run utility. The difference:
**Pegasus Refuge requires additional investment for each 1/1; Sacred Mesa does not.** He points at draft as
the cleanest evidence — when someone plays Pegasus Refuge (absent Cursed Scroll) the opponent doesn't care;
**when someone plays Sacred Mesa, the opponent concedes half the time.** Better still is **Lab Rats**, which
**requires no investment at all.**

## Symmetrical cards

**Howling Mine** gets a short, brutal treatment. Player A invests in it. If Player B draws an extra card
then Shatters it, B is clearly +1. But if he never Shatters it, **B sits at +1 or +2 card advantage
depending on whose turn it is** — and even after A gets his own draws back:

> **Player B gets the exact same effect without having to invest in the Howling Mine.**

## The two cards that break the rule

Flores names his own counterexamples rather than hiding them, which is what makes the piece hold up.

**Stormbind.** An automatic -1 investment "much like Pegasus Refuge, but it for some reason doesn't suck."
You only get card advantage from a goofy block or by killing a fizzle creature like Frenetic Efreet or Tar
Pit Warrior, and even then a single instance only reaches parity. He admits he is **unsure why the
non-Howling Mine Stormbind decks can be competitive given this model.** His best guess: with Pegasus Refuge
you won't overpower each additional card the opponent draws using 1/1s, but **a deck where each additional
nonessential card becomes a Guerrilla Tactics-level card could.**

**Sylvan Library.** He catches this one after the fact — "Oh wait, I lied." It has no immediate effect but
**does generate immediate utility, much like a creature.** A wise player destroys it at once because it's so
powerful; if he doesn't, **you have a remarkable selection engine that can also give long-term card
advantage, much like a more expensive Necropotence.**

## The rule, stated

> **In general, I would say that for you to play with cards that require investment, you need to have some
> way to get long-term card advantage with it.**

His examples of acceptable investment cards: **Disrupting Scepter** and **Goblin Bombardment**. And the
inverse:

> **On the other hand, cards that generate short-term utility but long-term card disadvantage, such as Mana
> Severance, should be avoided in non-combo decks.**

He signs off with credit to **Hahn, Kusumoto, Shuler, Taylor, and Wakefield** "for raising the intellectual
bar on this newsgroup," to altran "for reminding me that Sylvan Library is amazing," and jeers to himself
"for writing long-ass articles."

## Notes for use

- **This is why it sits in Block 3.** [Turbo Xerox](01-turbo-xerox-1997-06-27.md) is Investment applied to
  the manabase: cantrips that replace themselves let you cut lands, because a self-replacing card doesn't
  charge you for the slot. Read Investment first and Turbo Xerox stops looking like a trick and starts
  looking like a corollary.
- **The credit line is worth noting.** Flores's 2013 retrospective
  ([Shoulders of Giants](../block-0-orientation/04-shoulders-of-giants-2013-08-06.md)) says he learned the
  concept from the **Team CMU** players, notably **Andrew Cuneo** and **Erik Lauer**, and describes it as
  the first of many big ideas he wrote about that came from friends and associates. The 1998 post itself
  credits a different group in its sign-off: **Hahn, Kusumoto, Shuler, Taylor, and Wakefield.** Two of those
  are already in Block 2 — Rob Hahn wrote
  [Schools of Magic](../block-2-core-theory-paired/00-schools-of-magic-1996-04-10.md) and Eric Taylor wrote
  [it's about TIME](../block-2-core-theory-paired/04-its-about-time-1997-08-11.md).
- **Translate the vocabulary, keep the test.** Nothing here survives as a card evaluation — Jayemdae Tome,
  Browse, and Pegasus Refuge are all long gone. What survives is the question: **on the turn this resolves,
  has it done anything?** And if not, **does it eventually replace the card it cost?** That is still how
  you evaluate every Signet, every Sagas-style enchantment, every "do nothing turn one" engine piece.
- **Watch the accounting error he corrects.** Counting a just-played permanent as a card still in your
  possession is the single most common mistake in card-advantage arithmetic. Flores's fix — **it could have
  been a useless card, so it counts for nothing until it does something** — is the reason this article
  outlived its format.
- **Related reading in this collection.** Zvi's inevitability
  ([Who's the Beatdown II](../block-2-core-theory-paired/03-whos-the-beatdown-ii-multitasking-2003-02-20.md))
  is the long-game version of the same instinct: an investment is worth making when it buys a position you
  win from if nothing changes.
