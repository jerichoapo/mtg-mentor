# Turbo Xerox

**Alan Comer — The Dojo, June 27, 1997**

> Why it's in the list: cutting lands and replacing them with cheap card-selection spells, on the argument
> that a cantrip that finds a land is functionally a land you can also cast later. Twenty-nine years old
> and still why Brainstorm decks run fewer lands than the math says.

## The post

**Comer was replying to a thread asking whether a competitive deck could be built without rares.**

> **Even without Weatherlight, it is possible. The deck I took 2nd place in the So. Cal Regionals had no
> rares. It was more by coincidence than by design.**

**And then the sentence that names the archetype:**

> **Around here, it has been dubbed the Turbo Xerox deck, as everybody copied it due to the lack of
> rares.**

### The etymology, which is not what people assume

**"Xerox" refers to people photocopying the decklist** — it was cheap to build because it had no rares, so
everyone in his area copied it. **It does not refer to the deck copying or replacing its own cards**, which
is the folk etymology you'll often see attached to the cantrip-substitution concept.

Comer confirms the naming was accidental in a later piece (*Welcome to the Deck Clinic*, Sideboard, 2002):
**"Turbo Xerox went through Regionals with no real name."**

## Comer's own playing instruction

**The entire strategic content of the post is two sentences, and they're the important part:**

> **The important thing to remember with this deck is that early on, you MUST use the library manipulation
> to get to your land. Later, you can use it to get to cool spells.**

> **Things like: I Portent your library. I Foreshadow away your good spell…**

**This is the whole concept stated operationally.** The cantrips are **lands in the early game** and
**spells in the late game.** That dual function is what lets the land count come down: a card that reliably
finds a land when you need one is performing a land's job while remaining a spell you can cast later.

---

## The Turbo Xerox Rule

**The formalized version, which post-dates Comer's post and is how the concept is usually cited today:**

> **For every four one-to-two mana card-drawing spells in your deck, you can cut two lands.**

**The reasoning behind it:** cantrips that replace themselves **effectively shrink your deck**, so the
*ratio* of lands to relevant cards is preserved even though the absolute land count falls. And because a
cantrip digs more than one card deep, **the practical result is that you hit your early land drops more
consistently, not less.**

**The consequences for how such decks behave:**

- **Fewer flooded games**, because a smaller share of the deck is land.
- **Fewer screwed games**, because the selection finds the lands you do have.
- **A tighter, more consistent draw overall** — variance reduced from both tails.

## Notes for use

- **The idea in one line: a cantrip that finds a land is functionally a land you can also cast later.**
  That's the substitution that makes the whole archetype work, and Comer's own instruction — cantrip for
  lands early, spells late — is the cleanest statement of it anywhere.
- **This is the counterweight to Block 1's manabase installments.** Reid Duke's 40%-lands guideline
  (`block-1-the-spine/02-the-basics-of-mana-2015-07-06.md`) and his colored-source tiers
  (`block-1-the-spine/37-building-a-mana-base-2014-11-24.md`) describe the default. Turbo Xerox is the
  principled exception, and the two are compatible: **Karsten's tables tell you the count for a deck
  without selection; the Turbo Xerox rule tells you how much to subtract when you have it.** Read
  `02-how-many-lands-do-you-need-2017-05-30.md` alongside this.
- **The mechanism is the same one Flores calls "Investment"** in his own 1998 article — the distinction
  between self-replacing filtering (Brainstorm) and non-replacing tutors (Mystical Tutor). Only the
  self-replacing kind substitutes for land. See entry #4 in
  `block-0-orientation/04-shoulders-of-giants-2013-08-06.md`, and Duke's broader treatment in
  `block-1-the-spine/22-investment-2015-03-02.md`.
- **Also read against Eric Taylor's *it's about TIME*** (`block-2-core-theory-paired/04-its-about-time-1997-08-11.md`),
  written weeks after this post. Taylor's Memory Lapse appears in Comer's list, and Taylor's framework —
  mana and time as interchangeable, decks tilting toward one resource — is the theory Comer's deck is an
  applied instance of.
- **The archetype never died.** Library of Leng shows the lineage running from Comer's 1997 post through
  Draw-Go and "Turbo Jellybean" (1998), to Miracle Grow, to Legacy U/R Delver, to Modern Death's Shadow,
  which Brian DeMars in 2017 called the best Turbo Xerox deck in the format. Any deck running Brainstorm,
  Ponder, Preordain, or Opt on a low land count is applying this.
- **The historical caution about the name is worth carrying:** "Xerox" is about photocopying a cheap
  decklist, not about cards copying themselves. The concept and the name have separate origins that later
  got conflated.

## Appendix: the file at the reading-list URL

For completeness, `classicdojo.org/deck/dk15.970818acom.txt` (Alan Comer, August 18, 1997, subject **"Re:
Turbo Xerox"**) contains a **black/blue Reanimator deck**, which Comer calls **"The Re-animator."**

**Its core:** 4 Bazaar of Baghdad · 4 Vampiric Tutor (*"MUST have the bazaar!"*) · 4 Dark Ritual ·
4 Animate Dead · 4 Shallow Grave · 1 Nevinyrral's Disk · 4 Ashen Ghoul · 4 Nether Shadow ·
4 Krovikian Horror · 4 Nicol Bolas · 3 Crimson Hellkite · 4 Deep Spawn, on 4 Badlands, 4 Underground Sea,
1 City of Brass, 7 Swamp.

**Comer's annotations are the fun part:**

- On **Krovikian Horror**: *"You rarely cast these. They are to turn the bazaar into a library of
  alexandria."*
- On **Nicol Bolas**: *"Ha Ha! Woe be the white player who taps out turn 1 to cast his Savannah Lions!"*
- On **Deep Spawn**: *"Lets see, not only does he trample, and mills 2 cards for me, but my opponent can't
  target him?? Wow, such a deal for 1B!"*
- On an optional **Diamond Valley** block: *"This block is VERY good against burn heavy environments. 7 Life
  is just too much for them to burn through!"*

**And a genuinely interesting note on sideboarding**, which connects to the transformational-sideboard
material in Block 1:

> **When I go to most tournaments, I tend to leave the sideboard at home.** I realize that it is obviously
> better to have a sideboard, but the deck is a lot of fun to play… **Besides, it wins SO often that a
> sideboard is really just a luxury. This makes the deck one of the best candidates I have ever seen for a
> switcheroo sideboard.**

He gives two examples: pulling Shallow Graves and Nicol Bolas against players prepared for them, bringing in
Necromancy and Crimson Hellkite; and switching to Shivan Dragon and red creatures against Blood Moon. **"The
sanity of these switches can definitely be called into question."**
