# The Book

**Everything the library teaches, in the order it becomes useful.**

Assembled 2026-09-06 from the 74-article corpus. Every claim here traces to a named author
and a dated article. Where the corpus argues with itself, both sides are here, because the
argument is usually more instructive than either position.

This is written to be read straight through once, then used as a reference.

# Part I — The Resources

## The three-resource model

Every serious theorist in this library eventually arrives at the same reduction, and the
first to write it down did so in 1996. Robert Hahn compressed the game to **life, cards,
and mana**. Patrick Chapin closed the loop fourteen years later with a more abstract
version:

> **"In a game of Magic, resources are options... The most important of these, like a King
> on the chess board, is the option to continue playing the game."**

Chapin's framing is the one to hold, because it explains why every resource is convertible.
You are not collecting cards. You are buying options, and the ones you buy determine what
you are allowed to do next.

> **"The way you do this is by manipulating your resources to get more and better options
> while denying your opponent the same."**

Everything below is a specialization of that sentence.

## Card advantage

Reid Duke states the orthodox position plainly:

> **"Card advantage is, very likely, the single most important concept in competitive
> Magic. A tremendous proportion of games are decided, in one way or another, by card
> advantage."**

The classical definition, which he quotes: *"Card advantage is any process by which a
player obtains effectively more cards than his or her opponent."*

**And then the immediate qualification, which most people skip:**

> **"It's not always the player who technically gains card advantage who wins. One player
> might be casting Weave Fates until the cows come home, but if they're only drawing into
> Bellows Lizards and Dragon Fodders, one high-impact card like Shivan Dragon on the other
> side can make it all look silly."**

Card count is a proxy. What you actually want is more *useful* cards, and the gap between
those two ideas is where the next three concepts live.

## Virtual card advantage

**A card your opponent cannot usefully cast should be counted as though it were not there.**

Eric Taylor originated this. Zvi Mowshowitz credits him as *"the first source of virtual
card advantage theory"* and formalizes it: whenever you render an opponent's card dead or
useless, you gain +1, and each card you spend doing it costs you 1.

Taylor's four cases, as Zvi walks them:

1. **A lone creature holds off an army.** Creatures that cannot profitably attack or block
   are doing nothing. *"If you can't attack, you can't block and you're not doing anything
   else, what good are you?"*
2. **Token generation.** Tokens are not full cards, *"but counting them as zero is even
   worse."*
3. **Cards prevented from being used.** Play a Masticore and you blank everything you have
   spare mana to kill, for as long as it lives.
4. **Cards dead before the game began**, blanked during deck construction.

## Blanking

Zvi's own term, and the most checkable definition in the corpus:

> **"A card is blank if and only if given full knowledge of both decks and hands you would
> physically be willing to exchange it for a blank card."**

**The exclusion is the part that matters:**

> **"A card that you don't have time for isn't blank unless there is no way for it to cause
> you to win the game at some point in the future. Even if I have to draw ten perfect cards
> in a row to get out of this mess, as long as this card will end up winning me games I
> would otherwise lose it is not blank."**

A card you cannot cast right now is not blank. A card that cannot win you the game under
any sequence of draws is blank, and it was blank before you shuffled.

## Investment

Mike Flores, 1998. **The cards you commit to the board before they have done anything.**

His accounting correction is the whole idea. When you play a permanent that has not yet
generated value, you are down a card, and counting it as an asset is wrong:

> **"At this point in the game, the Tome player has done nothing with the Tome... it could
> have been a Pegasus Refuge or some other useless card."**

**A permanent that has not yet produced value is not a card. It is a promise.**

The rule that falls out: for a card requiring investment to be worth playing, **you need
some way to get long-term card advantage from it.** Cards that give short-term utility but
long-term disadvantage should be avoided outside combo decks.

Duke's modern restatement is a time-value-of-money argument:

> **"The same effect early in the game, for example putting a 2/2 creature into play, is
> worth much more than the promise of getting the same effect later."**

> **"Being quick to impact the game is a valuable quality of a card, while being slow to
> impact the game is a very real drawback."**

Zvi's 2004 update, which is more true now than then: *"Most modern decks do almost no
investing, because they don't have that kind of time."*

## Tempo

**A resource like mana and cards, with one crucial difference: you cannot count it.**

> **"Like mana and card advantage, tempo is a resource. It differs in that it cannot easily
> be 'counted' like the other two can."**

The practical diagnostic is a habit rather than a calculation:

> **"If you find yourself very often ending the turn without using all your mana, this
> should be a red flag that there's an inefficiency in either how you built your deck or
> how you're playing."**

**Two mechanics worth memorizing.** First, once you are ahead on tempo, every turn where
nothing changes converts into another kind of advantage, usually damage. Second:

> **"Removal spells are great for pushing a tempo advantage because, unlike creatures,
> there's no initial investment. Their impact on the board is immediate."**

That is investment theory and tempo theory meeting: removal is efficient at pushing tempo
precisely because it skips the investment step.

**The sentence that reframes card advantage entirely:**

> **"Traditional measures of card advantage go out the window when one player loses the
> game with five or six cards left in hand."**

If you die holding cards, you won the card war and lost the game. That is not bad luck. It
is a category error about which resource was scarce.

## Tempo against card advantage

There is no exchange rate, and Duke says so:

> **"There's no equivalency formula between the two resources that holds in every
> situation. It's impossible to say 'this much tempo is a fair trade for this much card
> advantage.' And it's impossible to say one is more important, since falling too far
> behind in either loses the game."**

What does hold is a **phase shift**. Early, mana is the limiting factor and tempo dominates.
Later:

> **"The limiting factor shifts away from mana (and tempo) and toward card advantage and
> other resources like life total."**

**Two terms worth having.** An aggro deck's ability to win in the late game is called
**reach**. And **"to break serve," the player on the draw has to trade resources, slow the
game down, and reach the late stage on even footing.**

One concrete tempo heuristic: *"cast your bounce spell the first time you don't have
something else to spend your mana on."*

## Card impact

Zvi's term, coined against a rival's misuse of "card quality." **It names the hole in every
counting system.** Both cardboard advantage and virtual card advantage treat every live
card as worth exactly one, which means **Mountain Goat and Rorix Bladewing are identical**
under both. They are not.

He is honest that he had the terminology wrong himself until a conversation corrected him.

## The Rule of Reflection

The standard against which every other tool is measured, and the single most useful
sentence in the library:

> **"Rule of Reflection: Everything in Magic has value equal to the amount it increases
> your chance to win the game."**

> **"If it doesn't make you more likely to win the game, it has no value. If it does, it
> has value equal to the probability it will make the difference. Once you hear it that's
> an easy thing to say, but a difficult thing to properly apply."**

Zvi credits **Justin Gary** with the phrasing and admits he only found the right words after
finishing all five parts of his series. Every counting method in Part I is an attempt to
approximate this well enough to use at a table. **None of them is the rule. All of them are
tools for guessing at it.**

# Part II — The Roles

## Who's the beatdown

Mike Flores, 1999, in five words:

> **Misassignment of Role = Game Loss.**

**In any matchup one deck is the aggressor and one is the control.** Duke's version of why
it matters:

> **"Answering correctly won't guarantee victory. However, answering wrong, or worse,
> failing to ask at all, will most certainly lead to a quick and convincing loss."**

The error Duke actually sees in practice:

> **"Once you know the basics, you can decide whether to play offense or defense. The
> problem is that many players make this decision far too early."**

Flores's original test was crude and useful: **which deck has more damage?** The deck with
more raw damage output is usually the beatdown, regardless of what its pilot feels like
doing. His load-bearing example is a player holding removal for a "better" target while the
game slipped away, when the removal was in hand the entire time.

## Zvi's correction

**Flores rates this above his own original.** Zvi Mowshowitz, 2003, argues the two-role
model is too coarse.

His objection: **attacking for damage is only one way of gaining an advantage.** All forms
of advantage *"can be control or beatdown in the proper context."* So the central question
is not who the beatdown is. It is:

> **"Who has inevitability?"**

**The roles are contested, not assigned.** They can change mid-game, and one player can seize
both at once, which Zvi calls *"a devastating advantage. That player will almost always win
the game."*

And the thesis that supersedes the original:

> **"The whole idea of Who's The Beatdown isn't that you need to know which of these two
> roles you're taking, it's that you need to know what each player needs to accomplish to
> win."**

He credits the underlying instruction to Jon Finkel, by way of Flores: **focus only on what
matters.**

## Inevitability

**Who wins if the game goes forever.**

Zvi's formal definition, where the term enters the game:

> **"A player is said to have inevitability if and only if from the current position he will
> win a long game. A player is said to have inevitability in a matchup if and only if they
> have inevitability on turn one."**

It is **positional, not fixed**, so it changes hands. It comes **in degrees**, not as a
binary.

Duke's practical test is a thought experiment:

> **"If you were to let both players draw 30 cards and spot them 100 free mana every turn
> for the rest of the game, who wins?"**

And his standard is stricter than most people's:

> **"I might have a powerful deck with lots of card advantage, but if my late-game strategy
> does not utterly and unquestionably crush my opponent's, then I don't really have
> inevitability."**

**When you have it, all you need to do is survive.** Defending is usually easier than
closing, so it changes your goal on every turn.

**The expensive mistake:**

> **"The most dangerous scenario is to believe you have inevitability when you really
> don't."**

**How the two concepts lock together:** whoever lacks inevitability is forced to be the
beatdown. Not by preference. If you do not win the long game, you must end the game before
long arrives.

## Flores's eight principles

His 2012 rebuild, which folds Zvi's vocabulary in and retracts part of his own 1999 piece:

1. **There are two kinds of decks and their positions are fluid.**
2. **Greed must be balanced against speed.**
3. **Thou shalt play differently going first and going second.**
4. **Inevitability, plus a general rule.**
5. **All war is based on deception.**
6. **Identify position; take action.**
7. **One definition of a bad matchup.**
8. **Sideboarding can be used to eliminate dead weight.**

Principle 1 is Zvi's correction absorbed. Principle 4 credits Zvi for the term outright.

## The fundamental turn

Eric Taylor, 1997, formalized by Zvi in 2000. **The turn on which the aggressive deck is
trying to win, which sets the deadline for everyone else.**

If the beatdown deck's kill lands on turn five, the control deck's answers must be online by
turn four. That single number determines whether a card is playable, and it is why control
decks in small formats need lower curves than their pilots want.

Related, from Zvi's *Clear the Land*: decks that use mana to get mana have a structural
problem. **They end up playing too much land and too few spells**, so *"too often they'll
have nothing to do."* His ideal, drawn from a Kai Budde list: **every other spell in your
deck can win you the game.**

# Part III — The Turn

## Attacking and blocking

Duke's first lesson is a correction of the median player:

> **"You should probably be both attacking and blocking much more than you are."**

> **"Playing too passively is just as bad as playing recklessly."**

And the read that follows from it:

> **"People bluff less often than you'd expect and people block less often than you'd
> expect."**

**One rule that costs games when forgotten:** *the active player must act first.* If you are
the active player, you pass priority, and your opponent also does nothing, **it is too late
to go back and cast your trick.** Combat tricks are excellent on your terms and dangerous
when you are forced to reveal your hand first.

## Sequencing

**The order you take actions in matters as much as which actions you take.**

> **"Proper sequencing will make the game as easy as possible for you and as difficult as
> possible for your opponent."**

The entire technique is one instruction:

> **"Think through your entire turn before you do anything."**

Duke anticipates the objection that these details are too small to matter:

> **"Trust me when I say that this is not the case. You never really know when something
> that seems small might be important to the outcome."**

**This is the cheapest skill in the book.** It costs attention, not knowledge.

## Timing

Distinct from sequencing, and about three things at once: **minimizing risk, maximizing
opportunity, and making things as inconvenient as possible for your opponent.** With an
instant you might cast it at end of turn, in combat, in your main phase, in upkeep, or a
dozen other points, and information management runs through all of them.

## Play or draw

Duke removes the ambiguity:

> **"To play first is to make the popular choice. If you choose to play first 100% of the
> time, you'll rarely be making a mistake. In the corner cases where you might be making a
> mistake, you're making a very, very small one. So I'll save us some time and headache: you
> want to play first."**

The reason is structural:

> **"There's nothing in Magic to guarantee both players get the same number of turns. Quite
> the opposite. The fact that the player going first has a substantial advantage more often
> than not means the game will end on their turn, in their favor."**

## Mulligans

**A mulligan is a large disadvantage, not an inconvenience.**

> **"We work so hard to earn card advantage, and by taking a mulligan your opponent is
> getting free card advantage."**

**Why you will never master it:**

> **"You will never ever face the same mulligan decision twice."**

> **"Mulligans are not an exact science. Someone might tell you to mulligan your hand, but
> under normal circumstances it would be impossible for them to prove it."**

**So the standard is calibration, not correctness:**

> **"Even world-class players can have different opinions on a close mulligan question.
> Whether you mulligan slightly more or slightly less, so long as you're consistently
> falling in the 'reasonable' part of the spectrum, you can feel like you're doing pretty
> well."**

Stop trying to solve individual hands. Start noticing whether you are systematically too
loose or too tight across fifty of them.

**One tournament rule to know cold:** free mulligans are never legal in sanctioned play. If
an opponent offers one, refuse. **"A mulligan impacts different decks in different ways, and
has different consequences whether you're on the play or on the draw."** It is never a wash.

## Damage racing

> **"What's the best way to kill an opponent who's also trying to kill you?"**

A race appears when **neither player can take firm control**. Duke's warning is that it looks
like the crude part of Magic and is the opposite:

> **"Ironically, it can be the aspect of Magic that requires the most precision, thinking
> ahead, and careful planning."**

> **"It's easy for a race to come down to just a few life points in either direction."**

Chapin's contribution here is the question to ask before every trade:

> **"Is the resource you are trading worth less than the life points?"**

> **"There is nothing wrong with throwing away creatures and other resources to deal extra
> damage."**

And the corollary most players get backwards:

> **"Many players make the mistake of thinking that lifegain is bad. The truth is that
> lifegain can be a very [strong effect]."**

His example: Kitchen Finks against a red deck is not two bodies, it is two bodies **plus the
life**, and against a burn deck life is the resource that decides the game.

## Playing from ahead and behind

Duke's observation is uncomfortable and correct:

> **"Most people play their worst when games aren't close. They either lose hope or give up
> completely when things look bad. They get overconfident and careless when everything seems
> to be going their way."**

**The fix in both directions:** when ahead, take the line with the fewest ways to lose rather
than the best line. When behind, identify the specific sequence of draws that wins and play
toward it, even if it is unlikely, because playing toward a likely-but-losing line has a
success rate of zero.

## Playing safe and playing scared

**Playing safe means avoiding a catastrophe you can specifically name.** *"I won't play a
fourth creature in case of Languish."* That is correct when Languish is real.

**Playing scared is the same behavior without the specific threat.** The distinction is
whether you can name the card you are playing around and whether it is actually in their
deck. Vague caution costs more games than boldness.

## Flexibility

> **"Choosing flexible cards or building flexibility into the structure of your deck will
> lower your risk and increase your consistency. Flexibility helps prevent things from going
> wrong."**

> **"If flexibility doesn't come at the cost of too much power, you should jump at the
> opportunity."**

That last clause is the constraint. Flexibility is not free, and a deck of purely flexible
cards is a deck with no top end.

# Part IV — The Deck

## Threats and answers

> **"Magic is a game of threats and answers. Mana, card advantage, and other fundamental
> concepts are in the background of every game, but threats, like creatures and
> planeswalkers, are what win games."**

The asymmetry, which is the practical takeaway:

> **"While there are wrong answers, there are no wrong threats."**

> **"There's little risk when you fill your deck with lots of potent creatures."**

An answer can be dead. A threat is almost never dead. This is why proactive decks are more
forgiving and why *"in the Magic world, the word 'proactive' has a positive connotation."*

Zvi sharpens it: **threat status is a modelling decision, not a property of the card.** Which
of your opponent's cards you decide to respect determines your entire read of the position.

## The archetypes

**Aggro.** The goal is speed, and the mana math is the whole strategy:

> **"Ideally, you should spend less mana to deploy threats than your opponent spends to
> answer them. You should spend less mana answering threats than your opponent spends to
> deploy them."**

> **"Your goal is to win before your opponents get their more expensive and powerful cards
> online. Your goal should never be to beat those cards in a fair fight."**

And the mindset that separates good aggro pilots: **no single card needs to win alone.** A
two-drop that deals four damage before dying has done its job.

**Control.** Named for what it does, not how it wins:

> **"Their goal is not to kill the opponent, but merely to control the game. Their top
> priority is to stop the opponent from executing his or her game plan."**

> **"Control decks are built on the premise of inevitability. The deck must be powerful
> enough in the late game that its pilot can be confident of a win so long as they can defend
> themselves and drag the game out indefinitely."**

On card draw: with enough of it, *"one will draw you into another into another and you'll
never have to worry about mana flood."*

**Midrange.** Neither speed nor longevity:

> **"Its strategy is based neither on speed nor longevity, but on grinding small advantages
> and setting up a winning board state."**

> **"The perfect card for a midrange deck is one that's good on either offense or defense."**

Midrange decks are adaptable because their **cards** are versatile, which connects directly
to flexibility. *"You will never be disappointed to draw Abzan Charm."*

**Linear.** Focused entirely on one theme:

> **"Every card contributes to that goal, and you have little interest in deviating.
> Worrying about what your opponent is doing is largely a distraction."**

> **"Linear strategies are often designed to spiral out of control. If my linear deck is
> allowed to 'do its thing,' and your normal deck is allowed to 'do its thing,' the linear
> deck will come out on top, often in impressive fashion."**

The line that explains linear decks completely: **"One on one, Kitchen Finks beats Elvish
Champion. Four on four, Elvish Champions beat Kitchen Finks."**

## Symmetric effects

> **"A symmetric effect impacts both players the same way, so it's difficult to glean an
> advantage from one under ordinary circumstances."**

The exploit:

> **"No event can ever really impact two people in exactly the same way, because no two
> people ever exist under exactly the same conditions."**

> **"If you have the creativity and the vision to find a use for them, you can wield weapons
> other players cannot."**

Flores's 1998 warning applies here. **Howling Mine is asymmetrical against you** because you
paid a card for it. Your opponent gets the identical effect for free.

## Board sweepers

> **"One of the first things you need to learn about a new format is what board sweepers are
> out there. This impacts both how you build your deck and how you play."**

A dominant sweeper means **most or all decks in the format are warped around its existence.**
Duke's blunt advice: **play the sweeper regardless of your own creature count**, because the
games it wins are games nothing else wins.

## Permission

> **"Permission has slightly higher value on the play than on the draw, since you're less
> likely to fall behind."**

The failure mode: once you tap out, **your shields are down**, and the opponent resolves the
threat you were holding up mana for. Playing permission well is largely about managing that
window.

## The high-water mark

Flores, 2012. **A property of the format**, and widely misquoted as something else.

**It is the damage threshold the format's common removal reaches**, which tells you how much
toughness a creature needs to be durable. Burn sets it, because burn is the removal everyone
plays and it is never dead.

That is why four toughness has historically felt so much better than three. **Where Lightning
Bolt is legal, a 3/3 dies to one card and a 3/4 does not.**

**The number moves.** Flores contrasts it with the Philosophy of Fire, which is fixed at a
base-two model of ten cards against twenty life. The high-water mark *"fluctuates from format
to format based on what people play."* It is a reading of the room.

Two examples he gives: **Olle Rade won a Pro Tour attacking with 2/3 Spiders** because the
number in that format was 2, set by Pyroclasm and Stormbind. **Jon Finkel splashed Wildfire
Emissary** into a prison deck because it survived Swords to Plowshares, the base removal of
the day.

**When you can ignore it:** a deck full of creatures, or a creature whose impact does not
depend on surviving combat.

## The manabase, quantified

Frank Karsten did the work so you do not have to guess.

**The land count.** One formula, a regression on 110 winning decklists:

**`lands = 16 + 3.14 × average mana value`**

A deck averaging 2.5 wants about 24 lands. Averaging 3.0 wants about 25.

**The colored sources.** For a 60-card deck, to cast the spell on curve:

| Cost | Sources needed | Cost | Sources needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5C | 8 | 2CC | 16 |
| 4C | 9 | 3CCC | 16 |
| 3C | 10 | 1CC | 18 |
| 2C | 11 | 2CCC | 18 |
| 1C | 13 | CC | 20 |
| 4CC | 13 | 1CCC | 20 |
| C | 14 | CCC | 23 |
| 3CC | 14 | | |

**Read the ordering, not only the numbers.** A 4CC card needs 13 sources; a single-pip
one-drop needs 14. **Cost and color requirement pull in opposite directions**, because by
turn four you have drawn more cards. The cheapest, most color-intensive spells are the
hardest to support, which is why **Goblin Chainwhirler at 23 sources effectively forces
mono-color.**

**The counting rules, without which the table gets misapplied:**

- **Turn one, only untapped sources count.** Taplands and Evolving Wilds do not help a
  one-drop.
- **Fetchlands count as a full source** for any color they can fetch.
- **Fragile mana creatures count as half.** *"Bolt the Bird has been a saying since the early
  '90s."*
- **Gold cards add one to every requirement**, because you need both colors present.
- **Cheap cantrips count as the fraction of your deck that makes the color.**

**Karsten's own caveat, which people drop:** the tables are *"useful guidelines only,"* and he
points at Pro Tour winning decks that ran below his thresholds. The number tells you what you
are paying. It does not tell you the price is wrong.

## Turbo Xerox

Alan Comer, 1997. **Cut lands because cheap self-replacing card selection does the work lands
would have done.**

This is investment theory applied to the manabase: a card that replaces itself does not
charge you for the slot, so a deck dense with cantrips can profitably run below the formula.

Two corrections the corpus carries. The URL most citations use points at a different article
entirely. And **"Xerox" refers to photocopying a cheap decklist**, not to cards copying
themselves.

# Part V — The Field

## The metagame

**What decks you are actually likely to face, as opposed to what decks exist.**

Duke's process: predict the field, then pick a deck that beats it. His six criteria for deck
choice, and note there are six:

1. **A deck that's good for the metagame.**
2. **A deck with raw power.**
3. **A proactive game plan.**
4. **Inevitability.**
5. **The nature of sideboarded games.**
6. **Do you enjoy the deck?**

The sixth is not filler. You will play this deck for eight rounds while tired, and **a deck
you resent, you misplay.**

On netdecking, which he settles:

> **"Once you venture into tournament Magic, netdecking is the norm and you should be
> prepared to face, if not embrace, it. If anything, building your own deck from scratch can
> be considered admirable, but netdecking should not be looked down upon."**

## Information cascades

Patrick Chapin, 2006. StarCityGames' Article of the Year, and the best thing in this library
that is not really about Magic.

**A cascade is a chain of decisions where everyone is copying the people ahead of them rather
than acting on what they know.**

His image is a column of army ants that got separated from the colony and fell back on a
single rule: follow the ant in front of you. They circled for two days until most of them
died. **The rule that normally works is the rule that kills them.**

**The failure condition, precisely stated:**

> **"Once an individual stops relying on his own information and starts imitating the group,
> the cascade stops being informative."**

**The detection method, which is the actionable part:** watch for **popularity diverging from
win rate.** In his case study, one deck was the most-played in its color pair for weeks while
consistently posting lower qualification rates than its less popular sibling. The results were
saying one thing and the field was doing another.

**The compressed lesson:**

> **"Imitation typically benefits the individual now; innovation tends to benefit the group
> eventually."**

**And the timing rule, the single most actionable sentence:**

> **"The most effective strategy... involves diversity and innovation at their highest when it
> doesn't matter, and heavier imitation (though not slavish) when individual performance
> does."**

**Innovate in testing. Imitate at the tournament.**

**Watch what he does not claim.** He never says netdecking is bad. He says it **usually wins
more games for the individual** and **usually costs the group information.** Most restatements
drop the first half.

His correction rule cuts both ways: **if you typically netdeck, innovate more. If you
typically go rogue, imitate more.**

## Sideboarding

> **"Sideboards win tournaments. Because your sideboard cards can be more specialized,
> pinpoint focused for a certain task or matchup, they're often your most powerful tools."**

**Duke takes a minority position on plans, and defends it:**

> **"The topic of sideboard plans is actually a rather controversial one. Many of my
> colleagues recommend against having firm sideboard plans, instead emphasizing improvisation
> and flexibility. In my opinion, this advice does more harm than good."**

His reason is that a plan **unifies deck construction with in-match decisions**. You choose
sideboard cards knowing which fifteen come out, which means both halves are designed
together. Deviate when the game demands it, but arrive with a plan.

**Hahn had four generations of sideboard theory in 1996**, nineteen years ahead of this:
color-specific, then strategy-specific, then transformative, then enhancement.

**And Zvi's addition:** the best sideboards **change or reverse your role**, not merely your
card quality. If you are the beatdown in game one and the control in games two and three,
that is the sideboard doing its most valuable work.

# Part VI — The Player

## The cool-things problem

Chad Ellis, 1999. **The only article in the library about how good players lose games they
had already won.**

His examples all share a shape: a player finds a winning line, then finds a *better* line,
and the improvement quietly violates a constraint the original plan depended on. In his own
case, a late change to a lethal sequence dropped the step that avoided killing himself.

**His four rules:**

1. **If the simple line wins, take it.**
2. **When you improve a plan at the last minute, re-check the original.** Ask whether there
   was a reason the first version was built that way.
3. **When you're close to winning, look for the disruption.** Ask what your opponent could
   have, and whether a different path is safer.
4. **"Take the time to check what you are doing, make sure you aren't giving unnecessary
   chances to your opponent and don't be fancy for its own sake."**

**Rule 2 is the most valuable habit in this entire document.**

A second lesson sits inside his analysis. Reviewing another player's report, Ellis finds a
misplay the author missed entirely, because the author had written it off as the opponent
getting lucky. The opponent had a tutor in hand the whole time. **"He got lucky" is almost
always a place where analysis stopped early.**

## Asking why

Duke's improvement method, and the engine that makes everything else compound:

> **"When you finish a game, you should reflect on it, and ask yourself why the winning
> player won."**

> **"The exercise is more challenging than it sounds, particularly when you've just lost. It
> requires patience, honesty, and a level head. Asking why is about taking responsibility for
> your own decisions."**

**Work the levels in this order:**

1. **Technical mistakes and judgment calls.**
2. **The mental game.**
3. **Sideboarding and deckbuilding.**
4. **Deck choice.**
5. **Bad luck, and only last.**

**The order is the entire technique.** Most players start at five, find a satisfying
explanation, and stop, which is how someone plays for years without improving. Do it on wins
too. **A win you cannot explain is a loss that has not arrived yet.**

## Volume

> **"If your goal is to improve at Magic, the best thing to do, by far, is play a lot of
> Magic. The most dominant winning streaks in the game's history have been put together by
> players who were simply eating, sleeping, and breathing Magic."**

The mechanism is automaticity, not experience points:

> **"When you're playing a lot, you see winning lines more easily, you make fewer mistakes,
> and you understand what's going on in the games on a deeper level."**

He frames the inverse as being out of shape. **If the fundamentals cost you attention, you
have none left for the decisions that actually matter.**

## The five traits

Duke closes the course with these. **Two of them are in tension on purpose.**

**Confidence:**
> **"You must have the confidence to trust yourself and believe victory is possible. If you
> don't believe, deep down, that you can win, then you won't search your hardest for those
> subtle plays that define a great player."**

**Humility:**
> **"You must have the humility to see how much you have yet to learn. If you become
> overconfident and disdainful toward other players, you'll destroy the most valuable
> resources for your own growth."**

**Striving for perfection:**
> **"Even though perfection may not be possible, you should still reach for it... 'Good
> enough' should never really be good enough."**

**Dedication:**
> **"If you enter Magic tournaments, you're going to lose, and you're going to lose a lot. If
> you allow yourself to give up when things aren't going your way, then you'll have no chance
> of ever achieving your goals."**

**Love of the game.**

Most players pick one of the first two. The ones who pick confidence stop learning. The ones
who pick humility stop trying lines they have not seen work. **You need both, simultaneously.**

## Tournament craft

**On goals**, and this is the one to internalize before your first big event:

> **"Your goals shouldn't be completely rooted in your final ranking. Magic is a challenging
> game with many random elements; no one in the world can do well in every tournament they
> enter."**

Set goals you can work on regardless of your record. That is not consolation. It is the only
way to keep learning through a 2-3 start, and a 2-3 start is coming.

**On judges:** call them early and call them often. Never take an opponent's word on an
unclear rule. Duke's habit, worth stealing: **any time you hit a rules question at home, write
it down and ask a judge at your next event.**

**On pace:** draws are worse than they look, because a draw is closer to a loss than to half a
win in most standings.

# Part VII — The Synthesis

## Flores: the three phases

Mike Flores, 2007, divides the game **chronologically**.

**Phase I** is everything before the **Minimum Game Threshold**, which he defines as *"a mana
point where a deck can reasonably operate, interact, make plays."* For Standard control decks
it has been **four mana** for as long as he can remember.

**The consequence for deckbuilding is the useful part:** a control deck must survive to its
threshold, which makes it **draw dependent**, and forces it to run cards whose only purpose is
to reach Phase II. *"Some cards exist for no other reason than that the control deck needs to
be able to play them."*

**Phase III** is where a deck **actively dictates the field**, which most decks never reach.

He also notes that **mana requirements rise as the format shrinks**, which is why Legacy and
Vintage decks operate on lower thresholds than Standard.

## Chapin: the three resources

Patrick Chapin, 2010, divides the game **by resource type** rather than by time, explicitly
taking Zvi's work to *"the inevitable extreme."*

His genealogy: **Weissman gave us card advantage. Eric Taylor gave us tempo**, followed by
Hacker, Johns, Comer and Wise. **Zvi unified them.**

His abstraction:

> **"In a game of Magic, resources are options... The most important of these, like a King on
> the chess board, is the option to continue playing the game."**

> **"The way you do this is by manipulating your resources to get more and better options
> while denying your opponent the same. This is at the core of every element of Magic
> strategy."**

**The practical version** is a question to ask at every decision point: **what are life points
worth to me and to my opponent right now?** Against burn, life is the game. Against a control
deck with no clock, life is nearly free. The same resource has wildly different value across
two matchups and across two turns of the same game.

His concrete applications: **do not play out all your lands late against decks with punisher
cards**, because holding cards is itself a resource. **An opponent who cannot afford to tap out
is a constrained opponent.** And **countering a burn spell is a form of card advantage**, since
it saves whatever you would have spent surviving it.

## Zvi: the exchange rate

The bridge between Part I and Part VII, and the reason burn decks work.

> **"Life is the resource that only matters when you're about to run out of it."**

Life is **non-linear**. Taking someone from 20 to 15 is worth very little. Taking them from 5
to 0 is worth the game. Which means:

> **"Every point of burn makes every future point of burn more valuable."**

**That is why the Philosophy of Fire is all-or-nothing.** A deck with six burn spells has six
bad cards. A deck with twenty-four has a strategy, because critical mass is what converts a
terrible exchange rate into a winning one.

**And the definition of what such a deck actually is:**

> **"A Fire deck looks like a bunch of horrendously bad cards because that's what it is. The
> reason the cards can win is that they work together to invalidate your opponents' strategy
> and increase each others' value."**

## The argument the library never settles

**Is card advantage the most important thing?**

Duke says it is *"very likely, the single most important concept in competitive Magic."*
Weissman built a deck that won on nothing else. A whole generation of theory treats it as
sovereign.

**Zvi says no**, and the demolition is the most useful passage in Part VII. Someone claimed
*"every game is won with VCA."* His correction is a two-word edit:

> **"I would correct his statement by taking out two words: Every game is won with
> Advantage."**

His reason: **"Magic's great charm is that what is important varies from game to game."**

**The trick he refuses**, which is how the maximal claim survives: *if I have won, all your
cards are useless, therefore anything that wins me the game granted me card advantage equal to
your whole hand.* It fails because **my cards are equally useless once I have won**. Something
other than cards decided it.

**His counterexamples:** a pair of turn-one Phyrexian Negators, which is a clock advantage
that *"trumps any amount of CA or VCA."* Two control decks at Chicago where neither could ever
damage the other, and **players won by letting their opponent draw cards so they would deck
first.** Games won by Millstone.

**And the cause-and-effect correction**, which generalizes far past Magic:

> **"Lost VCA is not causing you to lose the game. Losing the game is causing you to lose VCA,
> and rather directly."**

**Any metric that collapses precisely when you are already behind explains nothing.**

**Zvi's operating rule, and the last thing in this book:**

> **"Only use card advantage in situations where card advantage matters, or might matter in
> the future. When the battle isn't about cards, choose another unit of measurement."**

# The short version

If you remember eight things:

1. **Everything has value equal to how much it increases your chance to win.** Every counting
   method is an approximation of this.
2. **Ask who the beatdown is, and ask again when the board changes.** Misassignment of role
   equals game loss.
3. **Whoever lacks inevitability is forced to be the aggressor.** Not by preference.
4. **Think through your entire turn before you do anything.**
5. **If you die holding cards, you were measuring the wrong resource.**
6. **When you improve a plan at the last minute, re-check why the original was built that
   way.**
7. **Innovate in testing, imitate at the tournament.**
8. **After every match, ask why the winner won, and work down from technical mistakes.
   Bad luck last.**
