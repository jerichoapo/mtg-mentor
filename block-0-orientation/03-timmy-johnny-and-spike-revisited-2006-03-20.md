# Timmy, Johnny, and Spike Revisited

**Mark Rosewater — Making Magic, March 20, 2006**

> Why it's in the list: the deepest of the three versions, written once Rosewater had figured out
> how to design for each profile rather than just describe them. This is where the subgroups appear,
> and where he corrects the stereotypes his own 2002 article created.

---

## Why the rewrite

Rosewater opens by acknowledging two audiences: readers who know the terms and readers meeting them
for the first time. He walks through each profile for the newcomers but goes into much greater
detail than in 2002, including the subgroups inside each profile.

**Where the framework came from.** When he was hired into R&D roughly ten years earlier he was an
oddity: the one R&D person who had studied words in college. Everyone else majored in something
numeric, whether mathematics, engineering, or a science. He had majored in communications. He was a
writer.

That meant he approached card design the way he approached writing a story, since both were forms of
creative expression to him. He writes from the heart, to create an emotional response in readers, and
he designs cards the same way. To create an emotional response he had to understand which emotions he
was trying to evoke, which meant asking: what does a Magic player want when they play, what are their
reasons for playing, what makes them happy.

Communications school required sampling all three sections. He majored in broadcast and film, where
watching television and movies was homework, but had to dip into journalism and advertising/public
relations. In an advertising class he found the **psychographic profile**: by isolating personality
traits and behaviors you can understand what motivates a type of person to act a certain way. In
advertising it explains why people buy one item over another, but the field had broadened into many
domains. His question was, why not game design?

## The core value, and the mistake he made the first time

The profiles are valuable to a designer or developer for a simple reason: R&D's job is to make you
happy, and knowing what makes you happy makes that job much easier.

It took him years to assemble the three profiles and more years to understand how each worked, with
substantial help from the rest of R&D on both interpreting what each group liked and figuring out
how to make cards that satisfied it. He flags this as ongoing learning, which is why the 2006 column
is so much more advanced than the 2002 one.

**The stumbling block:** each group had a clear stereotype that pulled focus. When R&D discussed the
profiles they tended to talk about one particular subset at the expense of the rest of the profile.
That, he says, is exactly what he did in the first article. He oversimplified to the point of
reducing each profile to a stereotype, which produced inaccuracies and misunderstandings he's willing
to call myths. The 2006 column exists to set the record straight.

## Timmy: wants to experience something

The first question he asks of any profile is what that profile wants when they play. **Timmy wants to
experience something.** He plays because he enjoys the feeling he gets while playing. Which feeling
varies from Timmy to Timmy, but all Timmies share an enjoyment of the visceral experience of playing.
Johnny and Spike have a destination in mind. **Timmy is in it for the journey.**

**The great myth about Timmy** is that he's young and inexperienced. Rosewater's diagnosis: a
non-Timmy, particularly a Spike, watches a Timmy play and reads his choices as inexperience. Why else
would anyone play overcosted fatties, coin-flipping cards, or cards that simply aren't good? Because
Spike misses the point. Timmy plays cards that make him happy, that create cool moments, that make
him laugh, that let him hang out with friends, that cause him to have fun. Winning and losing isn't
really the point, though Timmy understands winning is fun. For Timmy the entire reason to play is
having a good time.

Fun varies enormously between players, which is why each profile gets broken into subgroups. These
lists aren't exhaustive, just the larger groups.

### Timmy's subgroups

**Power Gamers.** The stereotype: loves big creatures and big spells, smashing his way to victory.
Rosewater notes his 2002 column really reinforced this image. It's true for this group but not all
Timmies. The Power Gamer equates power with fun and enjoys the vicarious thrill of dominating the
game. Magic is about seeing how much he can do: how big a creature can he play, how much damage in a
single turn, how much can he win by.

**Social Gamers.** Timmies who thrive on the social aspect. It's about interacting with friends, and
Magic is a way to spend a fun Saturday night. This group is much more partial to multiplayer
variants because they want the whole playgroup in one game, though some prefer pairing off for
traditional two-player. Social Gamer Timmy picks cards and decks to maximize fun interactions, and
of all the Timmies is the most likely to issue house rules and self-ban cards. If the point is
having a good time together, why let irksome cards get in the way.

**Diversity Gamers.** They find fun in the variety inherent in the game. Magic's modular design and
large following produce many deck types and formats, and Diversity Gamer Timmy wants to experience
all of it. Fun is constant exploration, trying something different each time. He occasionally
returns to old ground, but only as a breather before leaping back into the unknown. This is the
group most likely to try cards nobody else is playing, not to prove anything the way a Johnny would,
but because they're honestly curious whether the card might be fun. Same for deck types and formats:
every color, every archetype, every way to play is fair game.

**Adrenalin Gamers.** They embrace variance. They enjoy cards and decks without predictable
outcomes, because the fun is seeing all the different things that could happen. This is the group
that loves coin-flip cards and cards that work differently every time. Adrenalin Gamer Timmy likes
flying by the seat of his pants and enjoys the rush of adapting to the unpredictable, so he's drawn
to decks and formats that make every game as different from the last as possible.

**Rosewater's defense of Timmy.** Of the three profiles he believes Timmy has gotten the worst rap.
Timmy isn't an idiot. Timmy chooses his cards for his own purposes, which aren't Johnny's or
Spike's, and that is the entire point of psychographic profiles: different players are motivated by
different criteria. He hopes readers will recognize themselves as Timmies and be proud of it. His
closing line paraphrases the 80s song: Timmies just want to have fun.

## Johnny: wants to express something

**Johnny wants to express something.** Magic is an opportunity to show the world something about
himself, whether how creative he is, how clever, or how offbeat. He's therefore focused on the
customizability of the game. Deckbuilding isn't *an* aspect of the game to Johnny, it's *the* aspect.

One of Magic's strengths is that players can imbue much of themselves in their decks. Playing
Monopoly you don't get emotionally attached to the board, but in Magic your deck becomes an extension
of yourself. When your deck wins, you win. When your deck is complimented, you're complimented. That
principle drives Johnnies.

The difference in how the subgroups divide: Timmy's subgroups form around *how to make the game fun*.
Johnny's form around *how one can express themselves*.

### Johnny's subgroups

**Combo Players.** As Power Gamer defines the Timmy stereotype, Combo Player defines the Johnny
stereotype. Fascinated by card interaction, on a quest to find combinations nobody else has, wanting
to build a deck that impresses everyone who sees it. Drawn to cards with a sense of potential,
especially cards he can build a deck around. He's focused on the game's modular aspect, looking for
connections between cards, so most of his attention is on the individual cards themselves.

**Offbeat Designers.** They also produce strange decks but start from a different place. The Combo
Player is driven by cards; the Offbeat Designer is driven by ideas. What if the deck only had lands?
What if it never played permanents? What if it stole every card the opponent played?

Rosewater draws the distinction sharply: the Combo Player is proving he can master the system by
finding gems in the midst of chaos. The Offbeat Designer is proving his ability to find answers for
any challenge. **The former is an explorer. The latter is an inventor.**

**Deck Artists.** They also build decks, in a different vein again. The Deck Artist isn't trying to
find or demonstrate anything. He's using deckbuilding as a form of self-expressive art: decks that
embody elf culture, or represent *The Empire Strikes Back*, or play in a way that makes the opponent
appreciate the uniqueness of the card choices. The Combo Player and Offbeat Designer show off *what*
they're doing. The Deck Artist shows off *how*.

**Uber Johnnies.** They build on sheer stubbornness, out to prove that what conventional wisdom says
can't be done, can be done. No card is too bad to find a use for, no archetype too unworkable,
nothing off limits. They thrive on doing the undoable and live to demonstrate they succeeded where
everyone else failed. This is the group that makes R&D design things like **One with Nothing**, which
Rosewater points out showed up in multiple sideboards at Pro Tour Honolulu.

**The caveat.** Johnnies aren't restricted to deckbuilding. Choice of format, play style, even choice
of tokens can all be means of expression. The common bond is a mission to show the world something
about themselves. What they're showing varies enormously, but the motivation underneath is the same:
look at me.

## Spike: wants to prove something

**Spike plays to prove something,** primarily how good he is. He sees the game as a mental challenge
by which he can define and demonstrate his abilities. He gets his greatest joy from winning because
his motivation is using the game to show what he's capable of, so anything short of success is
failure. That's the yardstick he's judging himself against.

What separates Spike's subgroups is **how he has chosen to dominate**, since different Spikes focus
on different aspects of the game.

### Spike's subgroups

**Innovators.** The closest Spike gets to Johnny sensibilities, though Rosewater is careful: the
Innovator wants to win first and foremost and feels no need to be novel or unnecessarily different.
He prides himself on judging new cards and his goal is finding the next broken thing. His dream is
to spawn the next dominant deck. He wants to break the game, and like Johnny, he wants credit.

Because he's focused on breaking new cards he spends a great deal of time understanding the nuances
of the mechanics. If something broke once, the odds are better that R&D will misjudge the same thing
again. Because this group wants to understand how the game ticks, **they're the ones most likely to
be interested in Magic game theory**, wanting to understand card advantage and card utility because
that intimate knowledge pays off later.

**Tuners.** They don't try to innovate, leaving that to the Innovators. They're next in line: once
decks are produced and played, this group dominates by fine-tuning known decks. Called min/maxers on
the role-playing side, they squeeze every ounce of advantage out of the resources at hand. Tuner
Spike's expertise is in what optimizes decks, such as mana ratios, card counts, and sideboard
technology. Innovator Spike has surprise on his side; **Tuner Spike has to win by being more
efficient than the people he plays against.**

**Analysts.** They also collect information but aim at a different advantage. Analyst Spike focuses
on the **metagame**. He plans to win not by having the best deck in a vacuum but the deck best suited
to a particular environment. He understands every deck has a weakness, so if you can understand what
will be played, you can figure out how to beat it. He tinkers with decks mainly to prime them for the
expected field, and the tournament version of this Spike is heavily focused on the sideboard: with
only fifteen slots, proper sideboarding depends on understanding the threats you should expect.

**Nuts & Bolts.** They've moved past decklists and metagames to perfecting their own gameplay,
believing the ultimate key to victory is flawless play. Nuts & Bolts Spike looks inward, trying to
understand his own internal flaws and working to improve them. Because of that focus he tends to
spend more time on Limited, which offers the most opportunity to improve general skills, mostly
because Limited has more variety and a wider level of power variance. Some focus on Constructed, but
they're the minority of this subgroup.

**The two things to understand about Spikes.** First, Magic is a means to test themselves, so their
enjoyment comes from marking their own progress. That often means winning, but some Spikes measure
success other ways, for instance by how perfect their play was rather than the result. Second, Spikes
are neither limited to organized play nor necessarily good. There are casual Spikes and there are
downright horrible Spikes. **Being a Spike is measured by why you play, not where or how well.** The
inverse holds for Timmy.

## The hybrids

**Timmy/Johnny.** Timmy wants to experience something, Johnny wants to express something. Together
you get someone who wants to show others how much fun he can have. He wants to enjoy himself but
likes to be innovative about how. He enjoys making up new formats or deck constraints, and builds
decks specifically for offbeat and fun formats, often multiplayer.

**Timmy/Spike.** Torn between wanting to win and wanting to have fun. He resolves it by searching
among the *viable* decks for the one that looks most fun to play. He's the player who goes out of his
way to fit a dragon into his deck, not one that shouldn't be played, but if there's a dragon that
makes sense he's all over it.

**Johnny/Spike.** Wants to win, with style. He's the rogue deckbuilder, the one who comes up with the
crazy decks that just might work, and crucially he takes the next step and actually plays them. He's
out to prove he can win under the added constraint of being innovative while doing it.

**Timmy/Johnny/Spike.** Wants it all: to prove he can win, while being innovative, while having a
rousing good time. A rare breed, because staying centered between all three desires is hard. Most
players who lean toward all three lean harder toward one or two. Rosewater calls the true
triple-hybrid the AB Negative of player profiles.

## Vorthos, and why he isn't a fourth profile

Rosewater addresses the "fourth player type" introduced by Matt Cavotta in his column on Magic's
creative side, "Taste the Magic." Cavotta named him **Vorthos**, and described him as the player who
never puts more than one of a legend in his deck because it wouldn't be right; who will only play the
Ice Age Icy Manipulator because it's the one they call the Bone Crank; who won't play the Fallen
Empires cards with the stinky alternate art. Vorthos started collecting because he liked the art,
read some Magic novels, saw his favorite characters on cards, and decided to learn to play. Some
Vorthoses collect without playing. Some enjoy getting artists to sign cards. Some won't read flavor
text until they've finished the novel in case it spoils the ending. Vorthos understands that Magic
can be fun even when you aren't playing.

**Rosewater's ruling: Vorthos doesn't fit, and that isn't a knock on him.** Vorthos is defined by
*what he cares about*, not *why he plays*. It's a subtle distinction, but it means Vorthos is
examining players along a different axis. An interesting axis, but not a psychographic profile.

The consequence is that Vorthos crosses with all three. There are Timmy Vorthoses who recite the
flavor text to Fodder Cannon at every opportunity, Johnny Vorthoses with a Weatherlight Crew deck,
and Spike Vorthoses who have read and remember the characters from every Magic novel printed.
Rosewater teases the companion profile on that axis: wait until you meet Melvin.

## Notes for use

- This is the version to internalize. The one-line summaries (experience / express / prove) come from
  here, and the subgroups are what make the framework actually diagnostic rather than just
  descriptive.
- The most useful practical content is **Spike's four subgroups**, because they map directly onto
  four different ways to get better: break new cards (Innovator), optimize known decks (Tuner), read
  the field (Analyst), and fix your own play (Nuts & Bolts). Those are four different study plans,
  and most players only run one.
- Rosewater's note that Innovator Spikes are the ones drawn to Magic game theory is effectively the
  bridge to the rest of this reading list.
- The Timmy defense is worth taking seriously if you're inclined to dismiss casual card choices as
  errors. They're often optimizing a different objective function.
