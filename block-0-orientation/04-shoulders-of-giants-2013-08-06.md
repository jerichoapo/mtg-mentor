# Shoulders of Giants

**Mike Flores — DailyMTG, August 6, 2013**

> Why it's in the list: a numbered countdown of the Magic articles the author considers most
> important, each with a paragraph on what it changed. Useful less as reading than as a map. It tells
> you which old pieces are load-bearing and which are only famous.

**Correction to the reading list:** this is Flores's countdown, not Rosewater's. Rosewater's
Timmy/Johnny/Spike is Flores's entry #0.

---

## The framing

Flores opens by calling the task of choosing the twenty greatest Magic strategy articles of all time
impossible, even for someone with exhaustive familiarity with the landmarks, which he says he doesn't
have. He notes there are whole wings of Magic writing that were vital once but, read in 2013, would
raise more eyebrows than IQs, whether for loose language or suspect subject matter. And there are
more than twenty worthy articles regardless.

He picked nineteen strategy articles, plus a zeroth that isn't one.

## 0. "Timmy, Johnny, and Spike" — Mark Rosewater (Mothership)

Flores calls it possibly the greatest Magic article of all time, and not a strategy article at all.
It's an article about thinking, and about how we think about ourselves. He includes it because it
would be inappropriate to write about the great works without tipping his hat to the best of the
genre as a whole. Rosewater rewrote how we talk about Magic players and put us in boxes even more
effectively than "Finding the Tinker Deck."

Flores identifies himself as a Spike.

## 1. "Prerelease Primer" — Brian David-Marshall (Mothership)

Flores calls it probably the most underrated Magic strategy article of all time, and the rare Magic
article that actually changed the world after publication. His point: most writers, at best, can
claim a little movement in the metagame, maybe spiking a tournament or producing a World Champion.
Impressive, but what about changing the game itself?

That's roughly what "Prerelease Primer" did, or at least it was an early domino in Magic's growth.
BDM's idea was to equip players who might be interested in tournaments but didn't know the first
thing about what to do once they arrived with some basic tools. Obvious in hindsight, and a decade
later it had many successors, because printing the article turned out to be highly effective at
driving Prerelease attendance for the *Legions* debut. Flores's summary: it's nice to affect the
outcome of a tournament, but here BDM changed the course of tournaments, and dramatically for the
better.

# The Foundations of Magic Strategy

These four laid down the idea of Magic as a serious intellectual endeavor. All were highly
influential on release and lasting in impact.

## 2. "Schools of Magic" — Robert Hahn (USENET)

Hahn was the first notable Magic writer who didn't work at Wizards of the Coast, and then went to
work at Wizards of the Coast. He bought The Magic Dojo from Frank Kusumoto and invented the idea of
infusing Magic publishing with venture capital. But before he became a columnist and editor at *The
Duelist*, he wrote "Schools of Magic," which functioned as his calling card to everything and
everyone that mattered in the niche.

Flores's line: Hahn was Plato to Brian Weissman's Socrates, the mouthpiece of early strategy. It was
through Hahn that players first learned about **card advantage** and about exhausting threats from
the opponent's deck. Many players had intuitively jammed discard and land destruction into decks, but
Hahn gave them structure, gave them names, and taught them how to clock. It remained "the best
article of all time" for something like four years, an eternity when the game was only five years old.

## 3. "Sligh Decks — Histories and Concepts" — Frank Kusumoto (USENET / The Magic Dojo)

The first legitimate challenger to "Schools of Magic." Kusumoto initially saw himself as a sort of
historian for decks, a sponge for deck histories and concepts, and an innovator in both Magic
publishing and website publishing in an era very different from today. Flores notes that thousands or
millions of people now ape Kusumoto's curation of information, and we call it the Internet. His first
punch was Jay Schneider's paradigm-crushing Red Deck, which was a vital first move for his new site
The Magic Dojo and immediately affected the next Pro Tour.

## 4. "Investment" — Michael J. Flores (USENET)

Flores's own first major strategic concept, and the first of many big ideas he wrote about that were
inspired by, or as he puts it lifted from, work by friends and associates. He learned the concept
from the Team CMU players, notably Andrew Cuneo and Erik Lauer. The vocabulary for card advantage and
card economy already existed, but "Investment" helped players think separately about **card filtering
that replaces itself** (Brainstorm) versus **options that don't** (Mystical Tutor). Both can be
effective, but some are inappropriate or overrated. He calls it a subtle concept that most players
now use intuitively.

## 5. "Who's the Beatdown?" — Michael J. Flores (The Dojo)

A not-so-small mistake playing for Top 8 of a PTQ unfurled into a series of falling dominoes that
would recategorize all of Magic thought. Flores is wry about it: you have maybe heard of this one,
and fourteen years later he supposes it's still pretty good.

# The Big Ideas

## 6. "Tempo and Card Advantage" — Eric Taylor (The Dojo)

Flores notes EDT uses the word "pedantic" in his own intro and that it's probably appropriate, but
the article remains worth reading more than a decade on. It's influential in a fundamental way, like
"Who's the Beatdown?", in that it changed how future writers approached strategy writing itself. It
paved the way for general concepts, for instance relating the card advantage of a Wrath of God to
that of an Ancestral Recall, and laid out explicitly the two fundamental symmetries of the game.

*(Related piece in this repo: `block-2-core-theory-paired/04-its-about-time-1997-08-11.md`)*

## 7. "Clear the Land and the Fundamental Turn" — Zvi Mowshowitz (The Dojo)

Flores calls the Fundamental Turn one of the most important advanced shortcuts for tournament play.
Is my deck fast enough to compete in this metagame? Often you only have to look at the deck idea.
This kills on turn six? Then you had better be able to win, or at least not lose, by turn four in
this room. Too slow, move on. Thank Zvi.

*(Full treatment in this repo:
`block-2-core-theory-paired/05-clear-the-land-and-the-fundamental-turn-2000-12-31.md`)*

## 8. "Who's the Beatdown II: Multitasking" — Zvi Mowshowitz (Brainburst Premium / TCGPlayer)

Flores poses the questions it answers: how many times does the opposing control player have to
activate a Nephalia Drownyard in game one before you reach for your sideboard? When two decks go to
Pyre Zombies or Hammer of Bogardan, which is favored, the beatdown or the control, contextually? He
says the answers to either might be surprising.

He has often thought Zvi's follow-up was the better article. **Inevitability**, the idea of which deck
in a matchup wins a long game, is extraordinarily powerful. It informs how you play, which cards you
think matter, and which cards you leave in your deck for the next game. Flores calls it very likely
Zvi's masterwork in a library littered with notable articles.

## 9. "Information Cascades in Magic" — Patrick Chapin (StarCityGames Premium)

The StarCityGames 2006 Article of the Year, and Flores says still Chapin's best piece, which is
saying something. Why do large groups of people do dumb things, like choose bad decks en masse? What
decks are metagame frauds? Who should you hire as a financial consultant? It asks tough questions and
dares to answer at least some. Flores says he revisits it repeatedly and learns new things each read.

# How We Think About Everything

## 10. "The Danger of Cool Things" — Chad Ellis (The Dojo)

Flores: this article is so good, and better yet, so cool. Ellis makes a good-faith attempt to teach
Spikes to play like Spikes. It is easy to fall into cool, fun, crazy, exciting plays that may or may
not help you win more.

## 11. "Finding the Tinker Deck" — Michael J. Flores (The Sideboard)

Who is favored between Junk Reanimator and Junk Aristocrats, and for that matter what kind of deck is
either? The article drew heavily on the example of "Schools of Magic" but had the benefit of running
on an official Wizards site. It simplified big concepts and helped shape the language we use to talk
about decks.

## 12. "Tomfidence" — Jonathan Becker (StarCityGames)

Hall of Famer Mike Turian called it the perfect Magic article. Flores agrees: personal and
actionable, full of useful tidbits and heartwarming name-drops. Want to win your next big tournament?
Read "Tomfidence."

# Red Decks

## 13. "The Philosophy of Fire" — Michael J. Flores (StarCityGames)

Flores sets the scene: a Jackal Pup, Goblin Guide, or Mogg Fanatic dies after a hit or two, and Brian
David-Marshall's voice chuckles on the mic, "doesn't matter, he did his job." What does that mean? If
a one-drop gets in for two or even four points of damage before trading, everything might be coming
up Piznarski for the red mage.

He credits **Adrian Sullivan** with the inventive connection between card economy and life totals.
"The Philosophy of Fire" co-opts Adrian's unifying concept and changes how red players approach their
hands and their deck strategies. The line it draws helps differentiate the Lava Spike deck from Red
Deck Wins.

*(Full treatment in this repo: `block-2-core-theory-paired/06-the-philosophy-of-fire-2004-04-23.md`)*

## 14. "Who's Afraid of the Big Bad Circle?" — Dan Paskins (StarCityGames)

Flores quotes its opening: the story of why the author is not afraid of Circle of Protection: Red,
Silver Knight, Worship, Pulse of the Fields, or any other stupid white cards.

He describes Paskins as basic Mountain's personal Shakespeare: hilarious, unflinching, and British.
Everything he writes is about how triumphant red decks make the world go round, and there's nothing
on the other side of that conjunction. Structurally it's like "Who's the Beatdown II," a long-named
article introducing an important, pithy macro concept. Where Zvi gave us Inevitability, **Paskins
donates The Fear.**

# Strategy and Tactics

## 15. "The Story of Low Tide" — Eric Taylor (The Dojo)

The setup: Chapin is playing Extended boogeyman High Tide, and Theron Martin successfully Lobotomies
the card High Tide. What now?

The article describes Patrick's quest to win without the core piece of his combo deck, its namesake.
How does the future Hall of Famer get the resources to Stroke of Genius opponents who already hit the
lottery and keep accumulating more? Because he does, and Flores says it's spectacular. Out-of-the-box
thinking and stealing victory from the jaws of defeat at its best.

## 16. "Casting Blue Spells" — Nick Spagnolo (TCGPlayer)

Flores calls Spagnolo possibly history's most underrated Magic theorist, a fine player, deck
designer, and writer who cut himself short by becoming a buyer for StarCityGames. Printed a decade
after "The Story of Low Tide," the two are mirror images: one asks how you win with your combo after
the combo has been gutted, and Spagnolo talks about playing commonly played cards differently, and
much more successfully, than everyone else.

At the time, Preordain was the most successful blue card in Standard, slightly more played if less
heralded than Jace, the Mind Sculptor. It was in all the decks, and the vast majority of players were
playing it wrong, mistake after undetected mistake. Spagnolo set that straight with concrete, sober,
results-backed reasoning.

# The Tournament Reports

## 17. "Dragons and Deep Dish" — Brian Kibler (The Dojo)

Two-time Pro Tour champion, Hall of Famer, Dragonmaster. In 2013 the name evokes Baneslayer Angel
beating Zoo, the perfect Primeval Titan draw, Junk and Naya manabases, Knight of the Reliquary. But
every story has a beginning, and this is Kibler's. Taking the absolute last ratings invite for Pro
Tour Chicago 2000, Kibler passed on Fires of Yavimaya, Ramosian Sergeant, Nether Spirit, and Fact or
Fiction to make his first Pro Tour Top 8, which Flores calls the greatest Top 8 of all time. The
detail he wants you to have: Kibler's first Sunday stage involved hitting fellow Hall of Famer Zvi
Mowshowitz with a 6/6 dragon wearing an Armadillo Cloak.

## 18. "Get Big or Die Trying" (Parts 1–3) — Jeff Cunningham (StarCityGames)

For a series that ends with "forget about the story, just tell me your record," Cunningham tells a
hell of a story. Split across multiple parts, it's the tournament report masterwork of arguably
Magic's greatest stylist: the struggle to escape mediocrity, rooming with your mother to defray
travel costs, playing in the Invitational as the culmination of a career of aspiration, and repeated
tidbits, Easter eggs, and conversations with Magic luminaries.

## 19. "A Champion Returns" — Paul Rietzl (TCGPlayer)

Flores calls it the finest tournament report maybe ever. Rumor had it this was the most expensive
single Magic article ever commissioned by a website, and he thinks it was worth it. The classic
hero's journey from low to triumph, with preparation, strategy, and mental game. For someone on top
of the world, Rietzl approaches the report with poise and humility.

## Notes for use

**What this list is actually good for.** It sorts the canon by *what each piece changed*, which is
what you need when deciding what to read. The pieces Flores describes as changing how future writers
worked, rather than winning a tournament, are the load-bearing ones: Schools of Magic (#2), Who's the
Beatdown? (#5), Tempo and Card Advantage (#6), Clear the Land (#7), Who's the Beatdown II (#8).

**What's in this repo already:** #0 (Block 0), #5, #6, #7, #13 (Block 2).

**The notable gaps this list exposes.** Five entries Flores rates highly that aren't in the reading
list and are worth chasing through the Library of Leng:

- **"Who's the Beatdown II: Multitasking"** — Zvi. Flores thinks it beats the original. Source of
  **Inevitability**, which Reid Duke also devotes a Level One installment to.
- **"Schools of Magic"** — Hahn. The origin of card advantage as a named concept.
- **"Information Cascades in Magic"** — Chapin. Why metagames converge on bad decks.
- **"The Danger of Cool Things"** — Ellis. The direct counterweight to Johnny instincts.
- **"Investment"** — Flores. Self-replacing filtering vs. non-replacing tutors, which is the concept
  underneath Turbo Xerox in Block 3.

**Attribution worth carrying forward:** Flores credits **Adrian Sullivan**, not himself, with the
original insight linking card economy to life totals that became the Philosophy of Fire.
