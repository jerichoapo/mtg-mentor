# The Three Magic Psychographics

**Mark Rosewater — Making Magic, May 26, 2025**

> Why it's in the list: the same framework rebuilt after two decades of use, with the names updated
> and the edges sanded off. This is the current version of the idea. The 2002 piece is the original
> argument, and the 2006 revisit is the deepest of the three.

---

## Why he wrote it again

Rosewater runs a Tumblr where he answers Magic questions, and he uses enough insider vocabulary that
readers regularly ask him to define terms. Someone asked about Timmy and Tammy, Johnny and Jenny,
and Spike. His habit is to link the DailyMTG article, so he went looking for a recent one and found
that the first was 23 years old, the main one nineteen, and even the 2013 revisit was a long time
ago. Since his thinking had moved considerably in the intervening years, he wrote a fresh one.

## What a psychographic profile is, and where he got it

Rosewater went to Boston University for communications, focused on broadcast and film with an
emphasis in writing, because he expected to spend his life writing and creating television. Game
design wasn't something you could study in the 1980s.

The College of Communications required students to take classes across all three majors, so he had
to take advertising. On one assignment students chose a product and built a campaign, and were first
introduced to the **psychographic profile**. The core concept: you need to understand who is buying
your product and why, meaning their core psychological motivation. Good advertising convinces
someone you have a product they want and need, which requires understanding what need it fills. His
professor's line for it was that you aren't selling a product, you're selling a solution to a
problem.

His mother was a psychologist and he had considered the field himself, so the tool stuck with him.

## How the three got named (the compressed version)

**Timmy.** Leading *Tempest* design, Rosewater had put Verdant Force in the file and the team was
arguing about whether it should stay. He gave a speech arguing that a type of player would adore the
card, and gave that player a name because he'd learned in his Hollywood pitching days that details
help sell a pitch. "Timmy" came out of thin air. It was the first time R&D discussed a player who
wasn't a serious tournament player. Everyone agreed the type existed and that they should make cards
for them.

**Johnny.** A year later, on *Urza's Saga*, a card in development made him realize there was a third
unclassified type: himself. Not a serious tournament player, not a Timmy. He gave another speech and
named the player Johnny. Everyone agreed, and the card stayed.

**Spike.** Not named by Rosewater or by anyone in R&D. R&D had been saying Timmy, Johnny, and "the
tournament player." The Magic Brand team liked the designations and decided the third deserved a
name. They supplied Spike, and R&D adopted it.

It took another year before Rosewater recognized these as psychographic profiles. He had
subconsciously understood that designing cards for players required understanding the psychology of
those players. He tried repeatedly to identify a fourth, and every candidate collapsed into one of
the existing three.

He credits the *names* as the key move. R&D adopted the profiles quickly and they became internal
language. When DailyMTG and Making Magic launched in 2002 he introduced the terminology publicly and
it stuck. Years later he concluded he should have used gender-neutral terms, and since the originals
were so entrenched in the community he chose similar names rather than replacements: **Tammy** and
**Jenny**. He left Spike alone, having known Spikes of many genders.

## Timmy and Tammy: they want to experience something

The 2025 framing is new. Rosewater organizes each profile around *why they play games at all*.

Some players play for what he calls **visceral opportunity**: the ability to experience something
hard or impossible in everyday life. He breaks it into four categories.

1. **Things that are impossible to do.** You can't cast a giant fireball, summon a dragon, or walk
   through time. Games let you experience those through play. You're only pretending to summon a
   dragon, but the excitement of casting Hellkite Tyrant is real. That's the joy of imagination.
2. **Things that are risky to do.** Games let you perform dangerous stunts with minimal
   consequences. At worst you lose the game. In real life the equivalent acrobatic leap might hurt
   you. The stakes are limited to things like pride, which frees players to live out extraordinary
   experiences.
3. **Things you don't feel comfortable doing in the real world.** You can be aggressive in ways you
   normally wouldn't, or take on a new persona. A game involves suspension of disbelief, so people
   read your actions in a different light.
4. **Things you can do in the real world, but that are easier in a game.** Social interaction is the
   example. Starting a conversation with a stranger can be hard, but a game supplies structure and
   rules for the interaction and turns an intimidating task into a guided one.

That's the core of what Timmy and Tammy want: **to experience something, to feel something, to
become part of something.**

**Designing for them.** Make cards that let them do something they normally can't. A big, powerful
creature or spell that's exciting to resolve. An effect that's chaotic and unpredictable. Something
that pulls them into a setting and lets them explore. Something that creates an interaction with
other players they'll tell stories about. The through line is that it lets them experience
something.

## Johnny and Jenny: they want to express something

Rosewater frames this one through identity. Much of life is spent answering "who am I," and then
even more time communicating that answer to everyone else. You shape your identity through the
decisions you make, and the games you play are part of that. Johnny and Jenny see Magic as a vehicle
for expressing identity.

**Through deckbuilding.** Magic hands control of the gameplay to the player. There are almost 30,000
cards and you need 40, 60, or 100 of them depending on format. That gap is the room for
self-expression.

Rosewater's own case: he loved building decks, but not normal ones. His specialty was decks that won
in untraditional ways, beating you in a way you didn't see coming. That said something about who he
was: creative, clever, untraditional. Friends asked to borrow his decks because they were fun to
play, which he found deeply satisfying. The deck was how he communicated his identity.

The examples he gives of how varied this gets:

- **Theme decks.** A friend who built around pop culture. His *Wizard of Oz* deck ran Shatter, but
  specifically the Ice Age printing whose art looked like the Tin Man. Casting it, he'd say "Death
  to the Tin Man."
- **A favorite effect.** A friend who loved attacking with lands and collected every card that let
  him do it. The deck's only damage source was lands.
- **A deck theme.** Rosewater went through a phase where every deck used the opponent's resources
  against them, all copying and redirection. The opponent's downfall had to come at their own hand.
- **A task to accomplish.** His father loved finding obscure combos and building decks that won by
  assembling them, preferring the more obscure combinations. The thrill was an opponent losing and
  saying "wow, that was cool." A coworker's version was building around cards everyone else
  considered unplayable.

Colors, card types, subtypes, names, and art can all carry expression. Sometimes the *absence* of
something, such as running no creatures, is the expression.

**Through gameplay.** How you play. A friend of his likes aggressive play and will be aggressive
with any deck you hand him. He drafts whatever color is open, then plays one- and two-drops to
attack with even when the archetype wants to control.

**Through demeanor.** Tabletop Magic involves interacting with opponents, and a distinctive
interaction style is itself expression. His example is a friend who showed no emotion, always stoic,
never talking, never cracking a smile, regardless of deck or format.

**Through accessories.** Sleeves, deck boxes, tokens, counters. A friend who always used
three-dimensional tokens, using miniature toys, matching them flavorfully where he could. Where he
couldn't, they were dinosaurs.

**Designing for them.** Four goals. Make many cards that represent things no other card has, both
mechanically and creatively. Make cards open-ended enough to inspire new ideas. Make build-around
cards that open up new kinds of decks. Make cards that present a challenge so that a select few
rise to it.

## Spike: they want to prove something

Rosewater builds this one off the same "minimal consequences" property that serves Timmy. A game is
a safe space to experiment, which also makes it a safe space to **practice skills you need in real
life**. Games are a mental challenge where you test your own abilities. Spike is the player who uses
game time to improve those abilities. He lists six.

1. **Analysis.** Nearly 30,000 cards, not created equal. Being good means learning to tell good from
   bad, and a card's utility varies by format and metagame. Expert analysis means learning the
   underlying systems, including mana costs, rules text, and card templating, and developing
   heuristics for interacting with cards in play.
2. **Memory.** The game is deep and you start without knowing what your opponent brought. You have
   to remember what cards exist and how they interact with yours, and hold a working knowledge of
   the current metagame and the archetypes you're likely to face.
3. **Processing.** A lot happens in a game. You have to track every piece of information. What your
   opponent did on turn three might be the key to winning on turn seven. That takes constant
   attention and the ability to monitor several things at once.
4. **Planning.** The key to a game is often not what happens this turn but what could happen on
   future turns. Winning means considering what could happen, on your turns and your opponent's, and
   your opponent's is harder because you have incomplete information about their options, which
   means mapping more potential outcomes.
5. **Strategy.** Understanding what does and doesn't matter. Knowing when to be aggressive and when
   to be defensive. **Understanding when you're in the "beatdown" role and when you're not.** Cards
   matter, but how you play them matters more.
6. **Interaction.** What you can do depends on what your opponent is doing. Hiding your intentions
   while reading theirs is its own minigame. Sometimes the route to victory isn't what you draw but
   what you convince your opponent you've drawn. Reading their intentions lets you hold the right
   resources to shut down their winning play at the moment they're vulnerable.

Spikes see all of these as opportunities to learn and improve. The joy is gaining knowledge, applying
it against worthy opponents, adapting, gaining new insight, and evaluating how to change their
behavior to do better next time. Different Spikes focus on different skills. The through line is that
**Spikes set goals for themselves and strive to meet them.**

**Designing for them.** Make cards where knowledge matters, so that the more you understand the game
the more powerful the card becomes. Push into new spaces that force Spikes to re-evaluate how the
game works. Lean into synergies, so cards get more powerful paired together. Challenge conventions to
keep them on their toes. Spikes want to prove their capability, so R&D has to keep innovating and
pushing boundaries to let them.

## Notes for use

- The load-bearing change from 2002 is the reframing from *what they like* to *why they play*.
  Timmy experiences, Johnny expresses, Spike proves. That formulation actually comes from the 2006
  revisit; 2025 keeps it and builds each section out from first principles.
- Note the Spike section explicitly names **role assignment ("the beatdown role")** as a core skill.
  That's the direct handoff to Block 2 and Flores.
- The four categories of visceral opportunity are the most genuinely new material here and don't
  appear in the earlier versions.
