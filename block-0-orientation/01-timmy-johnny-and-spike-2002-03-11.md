# Timmy, Johnny, and Spike

**Mark Rosewater — Making Magic, originally March 11, 2002 (live version dated Dec 3, 2013)**

> Why it's in the list: Rosewater's three player psychographics. Timmy plays to experience
> something, Johnny to express something, Spike to prove something. R&D designs cards for each.
> Knowing which you are tells you what deck will actually make you happy, and knowing which
> your opponent is tells you what they'll do with a bad board.

---

## The premise

R&D's job is to make a game that makes players happy. To do that they had to find out what players
actually like, which turned into a long research process: questionnaires, focus groups, lurking on
Magic websites and bulletin boards, talking to players in person, and tracking which articles got
read on the site.

The conclusion after several years was that there are three basic types of Magic player. The formal
term is a **psychographic profile**, a category based on psychological makeup rather than
demographics. The questions it answers are: what motivates that player to play, what kind of cards
do they like, and what keeps them playing.

R&D named the three Timmy, Johnny, and Spike.

Rosewater's caveat up front: very few players fall cleanly into one profile. Most overlap, which is
why the accompanying quiz returned hybrid results for many readers.

## Timmy

**How the name happened.** Timmy wasn't the first profile created but was the first named, and it
happened by accident during *Tempest* design. The team was arguing over whether Verdant Force would
be popular. Rosewater, by his own admission fond of the dramatic, launched into a speech. He picked
"Timmy" more or less out of the air. He wanted to personify the kid, and the name had an innocent
little-kid quality that struck him in the moment.

The speech, in substance: imagine a kid walks into a game store. Call him Timmy. He doesn't have
much money, so he buys a single pack of *Bogavhati* (Tempest's codename). He tears through it
looking for the rare, and there it is, a big green creature, seven power, seven toughness. Huge. His
eyes flick to the casting cost. Boring, move on. He reads the rules text. Every turn he gets another
creature. An entire creature. Small, but in ten turns he'll have twenty of them, plus a 7/7. How
does the opponent stop that? It can't be stopped. Timmy exhales. He has found the Holy Grail.

The name stuck.

**What Timmy is.** R&D's term is the **power gamer**. Timmy likes to win big. He doesn't want to eke
out a last-minute victory, he wants to smash his opponent. He likes impressive cards, big creatures,
big spells.

**The misconception.** Timmy does not have to be young. Younger players skew that way, but any age
can be a Timmy. What separates Timmy from the other two is that **Timmy is motivated by fun**. He
plays because it's enjoyable. He is very social, and sitting around with friends is a real part of
the game for him.

**The measuring stick.** Timmy cares more about the *quality* of his wins than the *quantity*. Play
ten games, win three, but dominate the opponent in all three, and Timmy walks away happy.

**Designing for him.** Every set gets a certain number of Timmy cards: big creatures or spells with
big effects, exciting but not especially economical. The efficiently costed ones get picked up by
Spike instead. Examples given: Krosan Beast, Iridescent Angel, and essentially any dragon.

## Johnny

**How the name happened.** Johnny was named second, during *Urza's Saga* development. R&D had
accepted Timmy and "the tournament player," but Rosewater believed a profile was missing, because he
wasn't a Timmy and he wasn't a tournament player. He was the missing third type. Trying to explain
who that was, he stumbled into calling him Johnny. That stuck too.

**What Johnny is.** The **creative gamer**, to whom Magic is a form of self-expression. Johnny likes
to win, but wants to win *with style*, and on his own terms. It matters to him that he's using his
own deck. Playing is an opportunity to show off his creativity.

Johnny likes a challenge. He enjoys winning with cards nobody else wants. He likes decks that win in
innovative ways. **What separates him from the other profiles is that he enjoys deckbuilding as much
as or more than playing.** He loves card interactions, loves combo decks, and is happiest exploring
uncharted territory.

**The measuring stick.** Like Timmy, quality over quantity. Johnny builds a new deck with a neat but
difficult win condition, plays ten games, and gets it to work exactly once. He walks away happy.

**Designing for him.** Johnny cards have unique effects worth building around, cards with real
potential, some of which eventually excite Spike. Examples given: Holistic Wisdom, Radiate, Battle
of Wits.

## Spike

**How the name happened.** Spike was the profile R&D was aware of first and named last. It's the
only one Rosewater didn't name, and nobody in R&D did. For years they said "Timmy, Johnny, and the
tournament player." When the three profiles were explained to the Magic brand team, brand felt the
tournament player needed a name and supplied "Spike," apparently because it sounded like a serious,
play-to-win name.

**What Spike is.** The **competitive player**. Spike plays to win. To do that he'll play whatever
the best deck is, copy decks off the internet, borrow other players' decks. The thrill for him is
the adrenaline of competition, the stimulation of outplaying the opponent, and the glory of winning.

**The measuring stick.** Spike cares about *quantity* over *quality*. Play ten, win nine, and if he
feels he should have won the tenth, he walks away unhappy.

**Designing for him.** Spike cards are comparatively easy to make. Spike plays what wins, so if R&D
makes a card good enough, Spike plays it. Examples given: Call of the Herd, Shadowmage Infiltrator,
Fact or Fiction.

## The hybrids

Most players don't sit cleanly in one box.

**Timmy/Johnny.** Likes the big cool effects and the creative side. Wants to smash the opponent, but
with style. Example card: Haunting Echoes, a huge effect for Timmy with real deck potential for
Johnny.

**Timmy/Spike.** Wants to win, but wants to indulge the fun side while doing it. Given a choice among
good decks, picks the one with the biggest creatures or effects. Example card: Rith, the Awakener, a
big dragon that is aggressively costed with a good ability.

**Johnny/Spike.** Wants to win, but only on his own terms. Most rogue tournament deckbuilders are
here. They go to great lengths to win with original decks, and even handed a stock list they'll
tweak it to put their own spin on it. Example card: Basking Rootwalla, interesting deckbuilding
space with the raw power needed to actually win.

**Timmy/Johnny/Spike.** Wants it all: big cards, innovative decks, and a high win rate. Rare, because
few players are pulled in all three directions at once, so R&D doesn't design many cards aimed at all
three. The example that did hit all three was **Verdant Force**. Timmy liked the size, Johnny
appreciated the combo possibilities, and Spike liked it in reanimation and Natural Order decks.

## The closing caveat

The profiles describe **motivation, not format**. Timmy can play in tournaments and Spike can play
multiplayer. The profile is about *why* you play, not *where*.

## Notes for use

- This is the original statement of the framework and the one people cite. It is also, by
  Rosewater's own later admission in the 2006 revisit, oversimplified to the point of stereotype.
  Read it for the argument, then read the 2006 piece for the corrected version.
- The practical read at the table: the profile tells you what an opponent will do with a *bad*
  board. Timmy holds the exciting card. Johnny holds the piece the deck is built around. Spike makes
  the highest-percentage play available and doesn't care how it looks.
- Rosewater later added gender-neutral variants (Tammy, Jenny) while keeping Spike as is. See
  `02-the-three-magic-psychographics-2025-05-26.md`.
