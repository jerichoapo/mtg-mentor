# Grand Unified Theory - Philosophy of Fire

**Zvi Mowshowitz — Brainburst, May 3, 2004**

> Why it's in the list: this is the hinge between card advantage and the Philosophy of Fire. Zvi takes
> Flores's claim that **Shock is the baseline burn spell** and works out what that actually implies about
> exchange rates between life, cards, and mana. It is the direct antecedent of Chapin's *Theory of
> Everything*, and it belongs at the front of Block 5 because the two later articles here are both building
> on the same unification project.

---

## What this article is, and what it isn't

Two things to get straight before reading, because the title misleads.

**First, this is not "The Grand Unified Theory."** It is a follow-up applying that framework to a specific
case. Zvi says so directly: the ideas here **"can be viewed as an expansion of where I left off at the end
of part five of that series."** The GUT proper is a multi-part series on the relationships between
resources. What that series ended on was an abstracted model — a long-term battle between a burn deck and a
control deck trying to stop it — which Zvi describes as having **no direct modern application.** This
article is him applying the abstraction to a live format.

**Second, it is a response piece.** Flores published *The Philosophy of Fire* days earlier, and Zvi's
opening is unambiguous about the relationship:

> **Michael Flores has struck a powerful blow recently for Magic theory, and I think it would be valuable to
> examine what he's trying to say... Read Flores' article first, it's a great read.**

Follow that instruction. Block 2 has it at
[06-the-philosophy-of-fire-2004-04-23.md](../block-2-core-theory-paired/06-the-philosophy-of-fire-2004-04-23.md).

His summary of what the Philosophy of Fire actually is:

> **The Philosophy of Fire is all about changing the value of resources.**

And the whole justification for treating burn as a theory problem rather than a deckbuilding problem:
**Magic is all about trade-offs.** There is far more you can do, he says, **looking at life as a resource
and trading it for others.**

## Two reasons to play burn, and they are not the same action

This is the distinction the article turns on. From an advantage-theory point of view, killing a creature and
going to the head are **completely different actions.**

**Burning a creature** trades cards in hand — or another resource, if the burn isn't from a spell — for
creatures, which translate into your opponent's cards. But the real goal is usually not card parity:

> **Most of the time your real goal is to prevent a loss of tempo or create an advantage there, because your
> burn costs less than your opponents' creature.**

He notes burn was useful in that format both for tempo and as **a form of lineup**, taking out key creatures.
The biggest targets were Disciple of the Vault, Goblin Warchief, and Goblin Sharpshooter. (He calls them
"space bunnies" and footnotes the phrase as coming from a video game title he once read in a magazine.)

**Burning to the face** trades your cards and your mana for **their life total**, and the default verdict is
harsh:

> **Most of the time, going to the face is a bad deal because your opponent will shrug it off and you'll be
> down a card.**

Then the sentence that is the actual theoretical payload:

> **Life is the resource that only matters when you're about to run out of it, and taking your opponent down
> from 20 to 15 tends to be worth very little.**

Life is **non-linear.** This is why burn to the face looks bad on a card-for-card ledger and can still win
games — because:

> **Every point of burn makes every future point of burn more valuable unless other considerations get in
> the way, and that's where The Philosophy of Fire comes in. By playing a critical mass of burn, you hope to
> make your burn valuable.**

**Critical mass is what converts a bad exchange rate into a good one.** That is the Philosophy of Fire
restated in resource-theory terms, and it is the reason the strategy is all-or-nothing.

## Why Shock is the baseline, and why that matters

Zvi accepts Flores's premise and then explains *why* the baseline sits where it does. He is describing card
development, not just card evaluation.

**At one mana you get two damage.** If you only get one damage — Spark Spray — you get something back, in
that case cycling. If you get more than two under conditions — Electrostatic Bolt — **you pay for it with a
restriction**, in that case the card cannot go to the head. His explanation of why the one-mana slot is
narrow:

> **It is very hard to create interesting one mana burn spells, because cutting down to one damage cripples
> them and offering three damage makes them broken.**

On Lightning Bolt: it **"was simply far out of line, and would be much farther out of line now."** The
reason it wasn't as broken then as it would be today is that **"it didn't have enough friends"** — a
throwaway line that is really a claim about how card power scales with the size of the card pool.

**At two mana you get three real damage, but just barely.** Volcanic Hammer is the baseline; in most block
formats your burn will be worse. Same reason as before: it's hard to add abilities or instant speed to a
three-damage spell without breaking it or imposing an ugly restriction. **Shrapnel Blast** is the exception
that gets a lot of extra damage in the right situation.

Above that, the equation shifts: **it becomes trading mana and a card for damage, rather than the card being
the primary consideration.**

The consequence for deckbuilding is the important part. Shock is the baseline for one mana, but **it sits at
the very bottom of the mana spectrum, where its mana cost is trivial compared to its card cost.**

> **If your deck is all Shocks you run out of cards fast, the same way that decks in old wild formats did
> when they ran nothing but Lightning Bolts — except that their opponents were far more likely to die before
> it happened because you needed seven to kill instead of ten.**

**Ten Shocks to the face wins the game. That's ten cards.** Even in the perfect scenario you draw first and
win on turn six, **"which won't win many games."**

### So what is Shock actually for

Three things, none of which is "deal two to the face":

1. **Clearing a path** or stopping an attack while your heavier burn does the real work.
2. **The option.** Shock is good because for one mana you remove a problematic creature **and retain the
   choice** to go upstairs. Zvi's justification is a general principle: **"Options are highly valuable."**
   With Electrostatic Bolt the value is often zero, because the opponent has nothing worth killing. **Life
   has variable value, so the option is worthy even if you rarely use it.**
3. **Mana efficiency.** If you can plan your other spells so you have no spare mana, **Shock becomes a fine
   burn spell again, because cards are no longer your limiting factor.**

That third point is the resource-substitution argument in miniature. **The same card is good or bad
depending on which resource is currently scarce.**

## The construction problem

> **One of the biggest problems with trying to implement The Philosophy of Fire is finding enough of those
> heavier burn spells that are efficient and the right size for the amount of time you'll be given.**

The solution shape: **a few very powerful spells that make up for the average size of the others, plus enough
solid cards to complete the mix.** You can model your plan with cards like Volcanic Hammer **if you have the
stars** like Pulse of the Forge — efficient on one casting, with the chance of multiple oversize shots from a
single card. With enough mana **you can potentially implement your entire game plan with that card alone**,
which Zvi says he did many times testing for Kobe.

### Deck one: the historical example (Flores's)

```
4 Chimeric Idol
3 Firebrand Ranger
4 Flametongue Kavu
3 Ghitu Fire
4 Kris Mage
3 Rage Weaver
4 Seal of Fire
4 Shock
4 Skizzik
4 Urza's Rage
19 Mountain
4 Rishadan Port
```

Zvi's read: **there isn't a ton of burn here**, and the deck plays "eight Shocks of various types" to clear
a path for its early attack. **That is the true goal of those cards in this build**, with the option to go
upstairs later. Flametongue Kavu looks off-plan but earns its slot by clearing a path, buying time, or
handing you control by removing a creature and then trading — **"if your opponent's control is in doubt,
that's more time for you to draw and cast your burn."**

His verdict is qualified, and the qualification is the lesson:

> **This deck does espouse the Philosophy of Fire to a certain extent, but it is far from a pure model
> because this deck makes a very serious effort to do the bulk of its damage on the attack. This deck is
> unlikely to deal sixteen to twenty direct damage unless its opponent drops the ball.**

What it does right: **it plays multiple ways to do far more than two damage with one card.** Urza's Rage is
three, Skizzik is five on its first attack, Ghitu Fire can be that much on the last turn — **and the last
turn is exactly when it should be cast**, unless used early as removal. The central deckbuilding tension he
names: **how much to put into the early fight for free damage versus how much to concentrate on the endgame
and the burn.** Most successful versions are hybrids.

### The blanking argument

Why a pure burn deck can win at all despite terrible card-for-card economy:

> **Your advantage by dodging the battle over card advantage is to try and blank your opponent's cards.**

If you have no creatures, their removal is dead. Same for artifacts and enchantments. But it goes further:

> **If you're not trying to fight a war over the board or over card advantage, then any spell designed to
> help gain that advantage doesn't matter either unless it hits your hand.**

Card drawing still helps them win, but the point of drawing is usually **to get ahead after a series of card
trades. If there are no trades, those spells no longer make sense. Your opponent is just wasting space and
time.**

**Blanking is how you pay for the strategy's structural weakness:** your opponent generates continuous
effects, you get one-shots. The ideal supplement is **one-drops** — they do their share of quick damage and
then **serve as a distraction, especially during sideboarding**, so anything beyond what's needed to answer
them stays blanked.

And the timing constraint: **you need to win on turns four and five to keep pace in a pure race, and that's
hard.**

### Deck two: what a real Fire deck is not

```
4 Talisman of Impulse
4 Fireball
4 Flamebreak
2 Hammer of Bogardan
4 Menacing Ogre
4 Pulse of the Forge
4 Rorix Bladewing
4 Shrapnel Blast
4 Starstorm
1 Forest
4 Great Furnace
13 Mountain
4 Tree of Tales
4 Wooded Foothills
```

This is Flores's own initial version, **which Flores rejected after realising what it was.** Zvi's diagnosis:
it can launch haste attacks and has a few worthy burn spells, but the rest of the deck offers **neither
quick attacks nor the option to go to the head.**

> **You're not taking enough advantage of the increased value of your opponents' life total, and you're not
> getting off to a fast start. Instead, you're trying to stabilize and then win with one shot effects.
> That's not a good plan.**

This is **Big Red**, the archetype that won Kobe — **kill the attack with mass removal, win with large
men.** It does not subscribe to the Philosophy of Fire, whatever its colour.

His contrast case is a person, not a deck: **Adrian Sullivan playing Landslides "was doing it for real."**
Zvi's evidence is his own experience across the table:

> **I never felt like he could do thirty damage to me or take control, just that he often managed to finish
> me off.**

Versus Flores's pet deck: **"a deck that will often do thirty or forty damage if it has to and hardly
blink."**

## The definition of a Fire deck

The closing paragraph is the article's thesis, and it is deliberately unflattering:

> **A Fire deck looks like a bunch of horrendously bad cards because that's what it is. The reason the cards
> can win is that they work together to invalidate your opponents' strategy and increase each others'
> value.**

**Most burn decks are actually just straightforward removal decks that become burn decks against creatureless
opponents.** True Fire decks are only viable with the right cards, and Zvi's verdict on his own format is
that those cards weren't in Standard then — though they could exist in Extended or Vintage, and **"it is an
idea whose time will surely return at some point."**

## Notes for use

- **Read the pair in order.** Flores's
  [Philosophy of Fire](../block-2-core-theory-paired/06-the-philosophy-of-fire-2004-04-23.md) makes the
  claim; this works out the exchange rates underneath it; Flores's
  [2/3/4 and the high-water mark](../block-2-core-theory-paired/07-2-3-4-the-philosophy-of-fire-and-the-high-water-mark-2012-07-27.md)
  is the 2012 revisit. All three are about the same question: **what is a point of life worth, and when.**
- **The transferable idea is non-linear resource value.** "Life is the resource that only matters when
  you're about to run out of it" generalises past burn. It is the same shape as Zvi's *inevitability*
  argument in [Who's the Beatdown II](../block-2-core-theory-paired/03-whos-the-beatdown-ii-multitasking-2003-02-20.md):
  **the value of any resource depends on the position, and every form of advantage is convertible into every
  other at a rate the board state sets.** That convertibility is the whole Grand Unified Theory project.
- **This is the direct ancestor of Chapin's synthesis.**
  [The Theory of Everything](07-innovations-the-theory-of-everything-2010-01-12.md) is the same move at
  larger scale — reducing Magic to a small set of resources and their exchange rates. Read Zvi first; Chapin
  is building on a foundation this article poured.
- **The "blanking" section is the most under-quoted part.** It is a clean statement of why a strategy that
  loses every card exchange can still be favoured: **you win by making your opponent's cards mean nothing,
  not by trading favourably with them.** That reasoning applies to any deck that opts out of a resource war
  rather than trying to win it.
- **Format-dated:** every card here is from Mirrodin- and Invasion-era Standard, and the specific verdict
  ("those cards aren't out there in Standard right now") expired within months. The two decklists are
  preserved because Zvi's analysis is attached to specific slots in them.
- **The parent series is files 01 through 03 of this block.** When Zvi says he is picking up "where I left
  off at the end of part five," he means *Advantage Theory - Putting it all Together*, published
  2004-03-01 — **which is one of the three installments that could not be recovered.** See
  [03: the unrecovered installments](03-advantage-theory-parts-3-5-unrecovered-2004-03-01.md) for the full
  record. That makes this article unusually important: **the abstract burn-versus-control model built in
  part five is lost, and this is Zvi's own modernised restatement of it**, written because he judged the
  original too abstract to apply. Read [01](01-advantage-the-grand-unified-theory-2004-02-09.md) and
  [02](02-advantage-vca-and-tempo-2004-02-16.md) first for the framework this is applying.
