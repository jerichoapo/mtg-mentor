# Information Cascades in Magic

**Patrick Chapin — Star City Games, June 28, 2006**

> Why it's in the list: this is the article that explains **why metagames get stuck on wrong answers**, and
> it is the reason "netdecking" has an actual theory attached to it rather than just a stigma. It won
> StarCityGames' 2006 Article of the Year. Chapin puts it first under Metagaming in his own
> *Magic Curriculum*, and Reid Duke leans on it in
> [Level One 42: The Metagame](../block-1-the-spine/42-the-metagame-2015-06-01.md).

---

## The army ants

Chapin opens with a long quotation from James Surowiecki's *The Wisdom of Crowds* (p. 40), about the
naturalist William Beebe finding a column of army ants in the Guyana jungle moving in a **circle 1,200 feet
in circumference**. Each ant took two and a half hours to complete the loop. **They went around for two days
until most of them dropped dead.**

Biologists call it a **circular mill**. It forms when army ants are separated from their colony and fall back
on a single rule: **follow the ant in front of you.** It usually breaks up only when a few ants wander off by
chance and others follow them away.

That image carries the whole article. **The rule that normally works is the rule that kills them.**

## The case study: Ghost Dad

Chapin's live example is the Standard season just past. Several B/W options existed with no obviously
superior choice — B/W control and B/W Hand in Hand as the default Orzhov strategies, with **Ghost Dad** and
**Ghost Husk** as the two innovations available.

How the cascade formed:

1. Early on, more people were drawn to **Tallowisp and Sleepless Pillory.**
2. Others imitated them, **assuming the first group had good information** — for instance that Thief of Hope
   and Strands of Undeath were stronger than Nantuko Husk and Promise of Bunrei, or that Ghost Dad's synergy
   made up for its lack of power.
3. By the Team PTQ season, **Ghost Dad was the most popular B/W deck**, ahead of Hand in Hand, ahead of Ghost
   Husk.

And the estimate that makes the point:

> **Out of 100 players playing Ghost Dad, perhaps 90 assumed it was good because everyone else said it was.**

**The results contradicted the popularity the entire time.** Chapin quotes Brian Hacker — *"You know what I
like? ... Math."* — and points at qualification rates: **every week Ghost Husk won a higher percentage of the
time than Ghost Dad**, and every week that happened it sent a signal that Ghost Husk was better.

His verdict on Ghost Dad:

> **In reality, Ghost Dad was a gimmick.**

It had surprise value against a field not expecting Spiritcraft, Shoals, and auras. It took players a few
weeks to learn to play against it. It was even a reasonable metagame call **for a metagame that quickly
vanished**. But: **its cards are notoriously underpowered, it is slow, it's weak versus all the best decks,
and it is easy to play around once you know how.** Ghost Husk, meanwhile, was **"a brutal, lightning quick
aggressive force that is strong versus good decks, while containing about as much synergy."**

He is careful to note the self-correcting property too: **in the long run, imitation has to be effective for
people to keep doing it. People are not slavishly imitative, no matter how much it may look like everyone
netdecks. The results will carry information, despite a cascade.**

## Why groups get dumber as they get closer

Before defining the mechanism, Chapin lays out the condition that makes a group smart.

**Independent individuals are more likely to have new information rather than the same old data everyone
already has.** So the best small groups are made of **people with diverse perspectives who are able to stay
independent of each other.** And crucially, independence is not the same as being right:

> **This doesn't imply rationality or impartiality, though... you can be biased and irrational, but as long
> as you are independent, you won't make the group dumber.**

Then the dilemma:

> **Typically, the more influence a group's members exert on each other, and the more personal contact they
> have with each other, the less likely it is that the group's decisions will be wise (i.e. inbred play
> testing).**

A new testing group starts with a variety of ideas. If it stays closed and tight-knit, everyone begins
imitating everyone else — which makes each member **individually smarter** (better card choices for a
specific deck and tournament) while making them **collectively dumber** (fewer good new ideas), because
everyone makes the same mistakes.

The compressed version, and the most quotable line in the article:

> **Imitation typically benefits the individual now; innovation tends to benefit the group eventually.**

**The statistical argument underneath it:** if independent individuals each decide based on **primitive
information plus error**, then with a large enough group **the errors cancel out**, leaving mostly useful
information. That is why a compilation of PTQ winners generally makes the best decisions — more useful
information than any individual has. **But it assumes the individuals aren't unduly influencing each other.**

He gives Wizards R&D as the working example: given how small the playtest group is and how easy it is for
ideas to become inbred, it's remarkable they don't make more questionable choices. He credits **the skill and
independence of thought of R&D, plus their outside playtest groups**, which are unlikely to make the same
mistakes as the primary developers.

## The mechanism, defined

Chapin builds it with a thought experiment. An unusual tournament: fifty players on identical U/W decks
against fifty on identical R/G decks in a new format. **One round is played and nobody sees anyone else's
result.** Players are then asked in sequence which deck they think won more matches. **Everyone hears each
answer, but not the results behind it.** Each correct guess wins a box of product.

So from the second player onward, everyone has **private information** (their own match) and **public
information** (what everyone before them said).

**You are the fourth player. Your U/W deck won easily. The first three all said R/G.** What do you say?

> **Most people would go with the group (against their own results), which is the rational thing to do. This
> would produce the correct answer more often than not, but would also usually start a cascade, essentially
> dooming everyone else if they were wrong.**

The definition:

> **The information cascade is a chain reaction of decision-making where almost everyone involved is basing
> their decision on the decisions of others, who in turn base their decisions on others, regardless of
> personal information.**

Two illustrations of the same mechanism running in opposite directions:

- **Useful:** you're at a street corner and everyone starts crossing. Even if you can't see the walk sign,
  **it's a fairly safe bet the crowd knows what it's doing.**
- **Harmful:** **everyone panicking and running out of a movie theatre when someone yells "Fire."**

### The incentive fix

Change the payoff: everyone gets a box **if the group arrives at the correct answer by vote**, still taken
one at a time. Now the group can assure success **if everyone votes their private information rather than the
group's decision.** Even with three R/G votes ahead of you, you say U/W to give the group the most useful
information about your match.

The conclusion is genuinely counter-intuitive:

> **This would make you more likely to "guess" wrong, however the group is more likely to be collectively
> right. Encouraging people to make incorrect guesses actually makes the group as a whole smarter. Quantity
> and variety are actually better for the group than focusing on quality.**

### Why Magic resists cascades somewhat

Chapin then argues cascades should be *more* common than they are, and gives three reasons they aren't:

1. **Decisions aren't fully sequential.** Each week players choose simultaneously, so sequential effects take
   several weeks to play out.
2. **Surprise value.** Cards or strategies opponents aren't prepared for.
3. **Metagame inversion.** "Weaker" decks can post winning percentages against the "best" decks — so **if
   enough people play the best decks, the weaker decks that beat them may be better choices.**

Plus the non-strategic reasons: enjoying a different strategy, or not owning the cards.

### The failure condition, precisely stated

Cascades spread information fast and efficiently — Chapin's examples of useful ones are **Memory Jar and
Skullclamp**, where "do we really need to wait around for everyone to test thoroughly these cards and
determine just how broken they are?" But:

> **The fundamental problem with an information cascade is that after a certain point, it becomes rational
> for people to stop paying attention to their own private knowledge and to start looking at and imitating
> the actions of others... But, once an individual stops relying on his own information and starts imitating
> the group, the cascade stops being informative.**

**The signal stops carrying information the moment people start reading it instead of contributing to it.**
Applied back to the case study:

> **Everyone (or many, anyway) thought everyone else was making decisions based on what they knew, when in
> reality they were all making them based on what they "thought" the people before them knew.**

## The solution: the overconfident

> **The solution? Those army ants that wander off. The overconfident individual who insists he or she is
> right and the crowd has it wrong.**

Chapin's argument here is unusual, because he grants that overconfidence is a genuine flaw and defends it
anyway. **People are generally overconfident.** They overestimate their ability, knowledge, and
decision-making — **and they are more overconfident on hard problems than easy ones.** (His aside: the two
exceptions are **professional bridge players and weathermen** — "it really does rain 30% of the time
weathermen predict a 30% chance of rain.")

So overconfidence is **bad for the overconfident**, since it means choosing poorly more often. But:

> **It is good for society as a whole (well, in this case the Magic community). This is because overconfident
> people are less likely to get sucked into a negative information cascade, and in the right circumstances
> can even break them. This is why many great deck builders are often chronically overconfident (hi
> Flores).**

The mechanism, stated cleanly:

> **In short, cascades are created by people valuing public information more than private information.
> Overconfident people don't do that. They tend to go on "gut" and place a higher value on private
> information. When they do so, they disrupt the signal that everyone else is getting. They make the public
> information seem less certain. That encourages others to rely on themselves rather than just follow
> everyone else.**

**Cascade-breaking works by degrading the confidence of the public signal, not by being right.**

He names the deckbuilders whose value is variance rather than accuracy — **Michael Flores, Alan Comer, Erik
Lauer, Eric Taylor, Mark Herberholz, Darwin Kastle, Andrew Cuneo** — and says they **"may make nine bad decks
for every good deck they design, but it is this diversity, this variety of options, that gains the collective
the greatest opportunity to make the best decisions."** He appends an apology to the Japanese deckbuilders
who had come to dominate over the previous five years.

The division of labour he draws from this:

> **Groups are better at deciding between possible ideas than coming up with them. Innovation is an
> individual enterprise.**

Intelligent imitation therefore **depends on an initial wide array of options**, and on **at least some
people being willing to put their judgment ahead of the group's even when it's not sensible to do so.**

His example of why this is worth paying for: **Jon Finkel may be friends with Mike Flores, but he certainly
values him highly as a playtest partner. Why? Creativity can be more valuable than individual performance.**

## Heezy Street: the ant that wandered off

The concrete case. **Mark Herberholz found himself in a circular mill in the beach house in Hawaii.** Almost
everyone there had convinced each other that their B/G/W control deck was *the* deck.

> **Mark wandered off, though, deciding that his perception (Heezy Street) was better than the group's
> perception (the failed Beach House deck). Heezy, of course, went on to win the Pro Tour, beginning a
> cascade of his own.**

And then Chapin turns the knife on his own example. Are Scorched Rusalka, Frenzied Goblin, Dryad
Sophisticate, Scab-Clan Mauler, Giant Solifuge, and Flames of the Blood Hand **really** the best cards for a
Gruul deck? Perhaps for that tournament, perhaps even now. But:

> **How many people tested all the options and arrived at Mark's build versus how many took it for granted
> that someone else already had?**

His own view is that Herberholz's build **was** the best — he wouldn't have changed a maindeck card. Which
makes what followed **a positive cascade**: it let everyone quickly settle what the best Gruul deck was and
whether they wanted to be R/G mages, **instead of spending large amounts of time determining if R/G is even
playable.**

**The same mechanism produced both the Ghost Dad disaster and the Heezy Street success.** Cascades are not
good or bad. They are fast, and speed is worth having when the first movers were right.

The line he draws:

> **It is a fine line one walks between choosing what is "best in a vacuum" (knowing when to go with the
> crowd, i.e. Necro) versus what is "best in a given situation" (which often involves being that upstart who
> insists that they know something everyone else doesn't, i.e. Turbo-Stasis).**

## The practical advice

This is the part to act on, and it is more specific than the theory suggests.

**The individual/group split.** People who netdeck would improve their group's decision-making by innovating
more, even if their ideas are usually bad — **but it is their friends and playtest partners who benefit most,
since it exposes the group to more possibilities at the cost of the individual using less safe ideas.** If
everyone innovates, the group's gain is maximised; but for any one person, **it is most beneficial to play it
safe and let everyone else innovate**, assuming your goal is to win games now.

**The honest consequence:**

> **Typically, people who net deck do better than people who innovate. This makes sense, since net decks are
> generally good and new strategies are generally not. However, if you want to get an edge over the
> netdeckers, innovation is the way; you just need to be able to generate enough ideas that you can select
> the best.**

**The group optimum:** **the nut high is for everyone in your group to innovate, though not at the same time,
and not just ride the coattails of a few.** This applies especially to players who want to contribute more
than their own results. **If actual tournament play is not your strong suit, supplying a variety of ideas —
again, even if most are bad — will bring everyone up.**

**The correction rule**, which cuts both ways:

> **If you typically net deck, you should innovate more. If you typically go rogue, you should imitate more
> (both assume your goal is to win more).**

He warns the overconfident specifically: **typically, one's ideas are not as good as one thinks**, so
consistently ducking the crowd puts you at a disadvantage. The fix is social, not analytical — **find
playtest partners who recognise the difference between your good and not-so-good ideas and are willing to say
so**, and sort your friends' ideas the same way. He also recommends **involving yourself with two or three
playtest circles**, which "will increase your chances of choosing the best deck exponentially."

**The timing rule**, which is the single most actionable sentence in the article:

> **The most effective strategy for a group of gamers to win the most when it counts involves diversity and
> innovation at their highest when it doesn't matter, and heavier imitation (though not slavish) when
> individual performance does.**

**Innovate in the off-weeks. Imitate at the PTQ.**

He signs off: **"Of course, knowing who to imitate is a whole other story..."**

## Notes for use

- **Pair it with Duke.** [Level One 42: The Metagame](../block-1-the-spine/42-the-metagame-2015-06-01.md)
  summarises this article and applies it at a beginner's level. Duke gives you the practical metagaming
  workflow; Chapin gives you the reason the workflow works. Read Duke first if you're new, Chapin first if
  you already metagame by instinct and want to know what you're doing.
- **This is the social half of Block 5.** [Zvi's GUT](04-grand-unified-theory-philosophy-of-fire-2004-05-03.md)
  and [Chapin's Theory of Everything](07-innovations-the-theory-of-everything-2010-01-12.md) are about the
  game's internal economy. This one is about **the information economy around the game** — how the community
  collectively decides what is good, and how that process fails. Both matter, and only one of them is about
  cards.
- **The transferable model is not about Magic.** Cascades are a general result about sequential decisions
  under uncertainty, borrowed from economics via Surowiecki. The test — **am I acting on private information
  or on the fact that others acted?** — applies anywhere a crowd converges fast.
- **Watch what Chapin does not claim.** He never says netdecking is bad. He says netdecking **usually wins
  more games for the individual** and **usually costs the group information**. Most restatements of this
  article drop the first half.
- **The Herberholz case is the honest one.** Chapin uses his own best example to show that the same mechanism
  he just criticised also produced a Pro Tour win and a genuinely correct consensus. **A cascade's value
  depends entirely on whether the people at the front were right**, and you cannot tell from inside it.
- **Dated material:** Ghost Dad, Ghost Husk, Heezy Street, and the 2006 Standard and Ravnica-block context.
  The decks are gone. The qualification-rate-versus-popularity divergence that exposed Ghost Dad is a test
  you can run on any format with public results, and that is the durable part.
