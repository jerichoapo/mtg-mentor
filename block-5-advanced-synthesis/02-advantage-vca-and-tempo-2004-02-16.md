# The Grand Unified Theory: Advantage - VCA and Tempo

**Zvi Mowshowitz — Brainburst, February 16, 2004. Part 2 of 5.**

> Why it's in the list: this is where the series stops surveying and starts arguing. It contains the
> **definition of Blanking**, the demolition of "every game is won with virtual card advantage," and the
> cause-and-effect correction that most theory writing still gets backwards.

---

## What part two is doing

Zvi states the plan and flags the difficulty in the first paragraph:

> **Part two is all about tempo and past attempts to relate tempo and card advantage. It is important to be
> careful, because tempo can easily be misused. I'll only get around to defining it formally in part five.
> It's that slippery.**

The structure: establish where tempo came from, which gives enough tools to look at virtual card advantage,
**"and VCA is where theory starts to say useful things rather than obvious things."**

Like part one, this is an annotated reading of prior work, with Zvi's corrections attached.

## Eric Taylor on tempo

He points readers at four Taylor pieces (one Sideboard article and three Dojo posts) and says you can skip
them if you already know the classic meaning of tempo. His summary of what Taylor achieved:

> **Taylor draws a very logical line separating card advantage and tempo advantage, which are profitably
> thought of as different things. He does a very good job of showing how different decks go after one more
> than the other.**

But he flags a historical note that turns into a principle. Taylor argued Memory Lapse was a problem card
for Hammer's deck. Zvi's update:

> **A modern player would probably put it in anyway, knowing that he can't afford the tempo loss involved in
> not doing so and illustrating the principle of a deck that has inevitability giving back the resource it
> has more of than it needs in order to not lose on tempo.**

That is the same manoeuvre as the Psychatog player pitching cards in
[Who's the Beatdown II](../block-2-core-theory-paired/03-whos-the-beatdown-ii-multitasking-2003-02-20.md).
Generalised:

> **Modern players have been forced to play cards that work against their deck's theme more often because
> their competitors are playing too efficient a game to grant them the advantage of using better cards.**

He adds a note on **Land Tax**: its deck thinning matters more than it looks, because **you are also
removing lands from your deck, making drawing land pointless**, so comparing it directly to Ancestral
Knowledge is unfair.

**On the two sites, and why the same author wrote differently for each:**

> **The Dojo was about the free exchange of ideas, The Sideboard was and still is at least half presentation
> when it has something to present.**

The Sideboard piece equates mana and tempo outright, where Taylor's Dojo writing is **"more carefully worded
and nuanced... they're closely related but not identical."**

**His rule of thumb from all four:**

> **A net loss of mana or the loss of an opportunity to get ahead on the effective amount of mana spent
> should invite an evaluation of the game in terms of tempo, and any game thought of in terms of tempo
> requires a close look at all mana spent.**

And the Finkel quotation he pulls out of the third article:

> **"There's the right play, and then there's the mistake." No excuses, no whining. If I had a Magic
> university I would put that quote in bold letters above the entrance.**

## Chad Ellis on tempo: tempo is about life

Ellis's contribution, per Zvi, is that **tempo isn't only about mana and the board**:

> **The result of tempo loss is typically life loss, or lack of an opponent's life loss, and life totals
> determine how concerned players should be about tempo.**

But Zvi immediately widens it, and this is the move that makes the series a *unified* theory:

> **Tempo does not have to be about life. It can be, and the majority of the time it is, but in some matchups
> the 'beatdown' that one or both players seek to inflict takes a form other than life, and tempo remains
> important there. Tempo can be reflected in any of the game's other resources over time, depending on the
> cards involved; life is just the most popular choice.**

## Andrew Johnson: two articles that contradict each other

The most instructive section. Johnson wrote one article claiming card advantage is everything and another
claiming tempo is everything, and Zvi treats the contradiction as the lesson.

First, a terminology correction he makes against himself:

> **Watch out for his jargon, because it even tripped me up until I had a discussion and realized I was
> misusing the term card quality that he uses when he should be using something like card impact, which is
> what I've chosen to call it.**

**On the card advantage article,** which claims *when we have gained card advantage, we have won*:

> **If you're careful enough with your definition of card advantage, which Andrew never tries to nail to the
> wall, and choose your definition with an eye towards this end his statement might even be true, but in that
> direction lies a technical mess of Herculean proportions.**

What Johnson actually means is weaker and defensible: **if you get more value from your stuff than your
opponent and use all your stuff without accidentally getting killed along the way, you win** — and that can
come from **more stuff, better stuff, or better use of your stuff.** Zvi notes approvingly that Johnson
counts **unused cards as not counting, "which is the most important aspect of VCA."**

**Then the reversal:**

> **Now move on to his article on tempo and watch the world reverse itself. From the perspective of this
> article, tempo is all that matters in Magic. If you didn't know any better, you'd walk away thinking card
> advantage is all but irrelevant.**

Johnson's tempo claim is **"whoever spends the most mana will almost always end up winning the game."** Zvi
calls that a stretch and doesn't think even a realistic version is true, but salvages the useful core:

> **Every point of mana spent efficiently generates an advantage for the player spending it, and every point
> not spent efficiently (or worse, not spent, or even worse early on not played at all) generates a
> disadvantage.**

And he locates exactly where card advantage re-enters a tempo game:

> **Card advantage matters when a lack of useful cards starts to punish one player, or the threat of a
> future lack of cards punishes that player.**

The verdict on holding both models at once:

> **By looking at the entire game through two separate sets of eyes, each of which subdivides again, you gain
> the ability to choose the one that is most applicable to the current situation.**

He notes the series was never finished.

## The overreach: "every game is won with VCA"

Zvi's target here is a writer (Tait) who tried to make VCA universal. He grants the good part — **"He's
trying to provide a clear description of VCA, which is excellent"** — and is with him for the first four
examples, then draws the line:

> **He claims that "Every game is won with VCA." This isn't even remotely true. VCA is just a method of
> fleshing out simpler versions of card advantage to correctly assess more situations. It's very good at it,
> but Magic is not a game uniquely about card advantage.**

The reason, stated as the charm of the game:

> **Magic's great charm is that, while two games are on occasion the same no matter what the marketing
> department says, what is important varies from game to game.**

**His correction is a two-word edit:**

> **I would correct his statement by taking out two words: Every game is won with Advantage.**

And then the concession that keeps it honest: **more often than not, but not always, the proper way to look
at a situation ends up looking a lot like a form of VCA.**

### The trick he refuses

He names the circular manoeuvre that lets you force everything into card advantage:

> **If I've won the game, all your cards are useless. You've lost! Therefore, anything that wins me the game
> must grant me VCA equal to the number of cards you have available.**

Why it fails: **"For some reason my cards are still useful, even though I've already won so mine aren't
doing much of anything either. What just happened was that some other aspect of the game made card advantage
irrelevant."**

**Five counterexamples where something other than cards decided the game:**

- **A pair of first-turn Phyrexian Negators.** Better understood as a tempo or clock advantage **"that
  trumps any amount of CA or VCA."**
- **A deck of forty-one Islands against forty Plains.** Neither can ever damage the other.
- **The real version of that:** two W/U control decks at Chicago, where **"players who realized that and let
  their opponents Fact or Fiction so they would have less cards in their deck ended up winning the match."**
  Drawing cards was actively losing.
- **Two decks of nothing but Phantom Warriors** racing to deck out.
- **Almost any game won by Millstone** that wasn't already locked up — he cites Humpherys vs. Ziegner,
  Worlds 2002.

And he explains the temptation with unusual generosity:

> **Magic is a battle of cards, and everything is done by those cards and a few tokens those cards created.
> When you lose, those cards didn't do their job because their job is to win you the game. Bad cards! Dead
> cards!**

Which leads to blaming tempo losses on non-tempo cards being dead, life losses on cards that don't gain
life, and so on for Millstone or poison. **"You could do a lot worse, as long as you keep in mind where the
battle is and what you have to do."**

His operating rule:

> **Only use card advantage in situations where card advantage (in some form) matters, or might matter in the
> future. When the battle isn't about cards, choose another unit of measurement.**

On the specific claim that tempo reduces to VCA because you are only denying your opponent time to play
spells: **"laughable to me. Tempo is a lot more than just stopping your opponent from playing his spells;
games are won all the time with tempo despite both players casting all their spells."**

## Cause and effect

The sharpest analytical point in the article, and it survives entirely intact into modern play.

The argument he is answering: *when I'm about to lose, all my cards are useless because they won't stop me
from losing; therefore in any game I lose I'm at a severe VCA disadvantage; therefore every game is won by
VCA.* Zvi emailed Tait about it and calls it **"more dangerous than I first gave it credit for."**

His answer:

> **The key thing to keep in mind here is that lost VCA is not causing you to lose the game. Losing the game
> is causing you to lose VCA, and rather directly.**

**The metric moves because the game is being lost. It is a symptom, not a cause.** Any statistic that
becomes extreme precisely when you are already losing tells you nothing about why.

## Definition: Blanking

Zvi's own contribution, defined formally:

> **A card is blank if it has become functionally a blank card, either because of the game state or because
> of the contents of the two decks. Blanking is the act of making a card blank, or effectively useless.**

The test, which is the useful part:

> **A card is blank if and only if given full knowledge of both decks and hands you would physically be
> willing to exchange it for a blank card or you would be willing to put it in the bottom half of your deck
> and put a blank card into your hand to take its place.**

And the crucial exclusion:

> **A card that you don't have time for isn't blank unless there is no way for it to cause you to win the
> game at some point in the future. Even if I have to draw ten perfect cards in a row to get out of this
> mess, as long as this card will end up winning me games I would otherwise lose it is not blank.**

He notes this often reduces to inevitability: **"any deck with enough of it no longer has any use for any
long term cards it draws."**

## Uncastable cards

The hardest case, and Zvi does not pretend it resolves cleanly. Declaring every card you can't currently
cast to be blank **"ignores natural mana development and other things that will happen in the future.
Technically it might work, but the answers you get will often be highly misleading."**

He then writes the contradiction out on purpose:

> **This card is currently dead, but it counts because in the future it won't be dead, but at the same time
> cards that bring it to life still add value despite the fact that in some ways you were counting that value
> anyway, but if you need such cards to make the card live then that makes the card less valuable.**

And collapses it into one line, which is the Rule of Reflection applied to a card in hand:

> **A card in your hand is valuable to the extent that having it in your hand increases your probability of
> winning.**

## The close: where classical ends

> **That takes things into the realm of planning. Without considering the course of the entire game, it is
> impossible to evaluate what cards are worth, which is something that I haven't even attempted at this
> point. To get farther, the entire course of the game must be considered and cards will have to start being
> thought of in terms of the impact they have rather than either counting or not counting. In a way, this is
> where the classical ends and the modern begins.**

## Notes for use

- **The cause-and-effect correction is the most portable idea in the series.** "Losing the game is causing
  you to lose VCA" is a general warning about any metric that looks terrible exactly when you are already
  behind. It applies well beyond Magic.
- **Blanking is the term you will actually use.** The exchange test — would you swap this for a literally
  blank card, knowing both decks — is a clean, checkable definition where most writers gesture at "dead
  card."
- **Note what Zvi does with Johnson's two contradictory articles.** He does not pick a winner. He treats
  holding both incompatible frames as the skill, and choosing between them per situation as the technique.
  That is the actual method of the whole series.
- **Reads directly onto Block 2.** The Memory Lapse note is
  [inevitability](../block-2-core-theory-paired/03-whos-the-beatdown-ii-multitasking-2003-02-20.md) giving
  back a surplus resource to avoid losing on tempo. Chad Ellis's "tempo loss shows up as life loss" is the
  bridge to [The Philosophy of Fire](../block-2-core-theory-paired/06-the-philosophy-of-fire-2004-04-23.md)
  and to Zvi's own coda at [06](04-grand-unified-theory-philosophy-of-fire-2004-05-03.md).
- **This part is why the rest of the series was findable.** It is the only installment the Library of Leng
  indexed. Everything else was located from the Brainburst category listing it led to.
- **Dated material:** Psychatog-era Standard, Masticore, Fact or Fiction, Phyrexian Negator, Worlds 2002.
  The examples are historical; every argument attached to them is not.
