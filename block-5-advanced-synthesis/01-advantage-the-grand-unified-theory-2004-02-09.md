# Advantage: The Grand Unified Theory

**Zvi Mowshowitz — Brainburst, February 9, 2004. Part 1 of 5.**

> Why it's in the list: this is the opening of the series Chapin builds on in
> [The Theory of Everything](07-innovations-the-theory-of-everything-2010-01-12.md) and Flores cites for
> years afterward. It contains the **Rule of Reflection**, which is the single most useful sentence in
> Magic theory, and a systematic teardown of every card-advantage model that preceded it.

---

## What the series is

Brainburst's own promotional blurb, preserved on the paywalled 2004 capture, states the ambition without
much modesty:

> **Over the years, there have been countless attempts to distill the game of Magic down to its fundamental
> parts, and ultimately all of them have failed. Whether the writers of the past theorized on card advantage
> or tempo, their works were universally flawed or incomplete... Now, for the first time, Zvi Mowshowitz
> presents a theory of Magic advantage that will re-invent the way we think about the game, including a look
> at all the important theories to come before it.**

Zvi's own framing is more careful, and the warnings he gives up front are worth taking seriously.

## Chess, and where "advantage" comes from

He opens autobiographically. Before Magic, his game was chess, played at speed in the student lounge, and
a large part of the fun was trash talk about advantage:

> **If you could put it in front of the word advantage, we claimed it.**

His list: **material advantage** (the equivalent of card advantage), **space, development, time** (not to be
confused with **clock**), **pawn structure, open file, king safety, centre**. The point of the list is that
chess players had long since accepted that advantage comes in many incommensurable kinds, and argued about
which one mattered in a position.

Moving to Magic, that vocabulary vanished. He and his friend Adam Hulse "didn't debate advantage much
anymore" and may not have known the term card advantage at all. In his corner of the community, early
players **knew card advantage well, almost as well as we do now, but didn't understand concepts like tempo
or the mana curve.** What they did keep doing was asking who was winning, while understanding without
saying it that this is **much harder to work out in Magic than in a deterministic complete-information game
like chess.**

## The problem he set out to fix

The immediate occasion was a revived argument about card advantage on another site. Zvi describes the shape
that argument always takes:

> **The debate quickly introduced examples in which any given simple technical explanation of "card
> advantage" would break down, either by contradicting itself, by treating two situations that were
> effectively the same as very different (or vice versa), or by concluding that what is obviously an immense
> generation of resources is 'card disadvantage.' More complex theories are then introduced to plug those
> holes, until the point where someone points out correctly that common sense must be applied.**

His diagnosis of the root cause is the thesis of the whole series:

> **These theories of card advantage treat cards as worth one unit and they begin when the game begins.**

But any useful theory is describing **the process by which one player ends up with more useful stuff than
his opponent, and then uses that stuff to win the game.** And:

> **This process doesn't begin over the board, it begins during deck construction, and the contents of both
> decks are a strong influence on how you should be calculating any type of advantage even before those
> cards have been drawn.**

Following that thread "quickly ballooned into the biggest question in Magic: **What wins games?**"

## Three warnings

Zvi front-loads his caveats, and they are the reason the series aged better than the theories it critiques.

**1. It is long.** "A fleshed out version of this series could be a book, and in fact it might become one."
Denser than an article about a deck or a format.

**2. Do not over-apply the math.** This is the important one:

> **Don't try to sit down and do the math that these principles would imply without thinking long and hard
> about the way you've set things up. Make sure that you're choosing the right tools for the current problem
> whether you're working on a deck, game, matchup or anything else, and even when you're confident of that
> you should first look to use the relationships to build up and understand your intuitions and make sense
> of what is going on rather than figuring out which play yields an extra 0.09 cards in value. Magic will
> never be that precise.**

**3. The examples are deliberately old.** Earlier cards and decks "were often simpler and more direct as
well as better understood than many modern ones, and that can make them better choices."

He also nods at a contemporary writing in parallel, referring readers to the introduction of what that
author "calls his Magic University," and says he would be happy to cover duplicated material twice.

## The Rule of Reflection

This is the payload of part one, and Zvi flags that he only found the right words for it **after finishing
all five parts**, and that **Justin Gary had to tell him he had it wrong**:

> **Rule of Reflection: Everything in Magic has value equal to the amount it increases your chance to win
> the game.**

His gloss:

> **If it doesn't make you more likely to win the game, it has no value. If it does, it has value equal to
> the probability it will make the difference. Once you hear it that's an easy thing to say, but a difficult
> thing to properly apply.**

**This is the standard against which he then measures every existing theory.** Everything that follows in
the series is an attempt to build tools that approximate the Rule of Reflection well enough to use at a
table, while never mistaking the tool for the rule.

## The review: four systems, graded

Part one is a critical survey. Zvi is explicit that he is not going in chronological order but in what he
considers logical order.

### 1. Cardboard Advantage (Tait's "Pure Card Advantage")

**The definition:** count who has more cardboard. Cards in play or in hand count. Nothing else does.
**Roar of the Wurm in your graveyard does not count. A Squire with a -1/-1 counter counts, and so does the
Arrest on it, but a 7/7 Demon token is out of luck.**

He renames it deliberately: **"That's why I call this Cardboard Advantage rather than Pure Card Advantage.
It makes it much easier to understand what this system is, and what it is not."**

**Five strengths:**

1. **Well defined.** Give him a game state and he will tell you who has it and by how much.
2. **Simple.** Definable in one line.
3. **Complete.** It always gives a straight answer, never "it depends."
4. **Useful for most situations.** When neither player is dying to an early rush, the player with more
   permanents and cards in hand usually is ahead, and ahead *because of* those cards.
5. **A good foundation** for beginners and for more complex theories.

**Eight failures:**

1. **Counts dead cards** — Sarcomancy after the token dies.
2. **Counts nullified cards** — your four Black Knights under Light of Day.
3. **Doesn't count tokens** — that Sarcomancy token.
4. **Counts uncastable spells** — a blue card in a deck with no blue sources still counts in hand.
5. **Doesn't count flashback in the graveyard** — Roar of the Wurm.
6. **Ignores Card Impact** — Mon's Goblin Raiders equals Rorix Bladewing.
7. **Gets game states badly wrong** — Mirari's Wake plus Decree of Justice for **37 4/4 soldier tokens**
   registers as nothing.
8. **It gets misapplied** by people who don't understand the limits. **"It is not a way of measuring your
   chance of winning the game."**

Zvi is careful to be fair here. Tait knows all of this, and his critics know it too. He quotes Tait: **"It
is the places that this system breaks down that cause us to create more refined theories."** And he defends
Tait against Flores:

> **Flores claimed "The definition is wrong" because of these situations, but that's silly: It's not meant
> to be a theory of advantage, or an evaluation of all the resources in the game that create those
> advantages, just a way to count Cardboard Advantage.**

**"Advanced Cardboard Advantage"** gets one paragraph of pure mockery: go buy double-sided tokens from Your
Move Games, and now the tokens are literally cardboard so they count. **"Good for you. Now remember that the
whole exercise is rather silly if taken too seriously and move on to something more useful."**

### 2. Investment (Flores)

Included for two reasons. First, **Tait misappropriates the term** and Zvi "won't stand for that sort of
thing, so here it is properly nailed to a tree." Second, the strategic content still holds. His update:

> **Most modern decks do almost no investing, because they don't have that kind of time. Compulsion is a
> good way of seeing how good cards requiring investment have to be before they're worth playing.**

The article he is defending is in this collection at
[block-3-manabase-reference/00-investment-1998-03-20.md](../block-3-manabase-reference/00-investment-1998-03-20.md).

### 3. More Advanced Card Advantage: the threat

This piece introduces the idea that **all cards are not created equal**, via the concept of the *threat*: if
a card is not a threat, your opponent doesn't have to answer it, so you are down a card.

Zvi's objection is that "threat" is not well defined. **Howling Mine and Sylvan Library** are excluded, but
why? In the right context they absolutely must be answered; in others they can be ignored, in which case
they have to earn back the initial card loss. **Cursed Scroll** is treated as obviously a threat because it
will recoup its card over time.

His conclusion is more interesting than the critique:

> **You choose which of your opponents' cards you will view as threats and which you will not... you are
> forced to construct a model of the game in which some cards are threats and some cards are not, and
> arrange your view of Advantage in general and card advantage in particular on that basis.**

**Threat status is a modelling decision, not a property of the card.**

### 4. Eric Taylor on Virtual Card Advantage

Called **"the first source of virtual card advantage theory."** Zvi walks Taylor's four examples:

1. **A lone creature holds off an army.** All creatures are not equal, especially when some cannot attack
   without dying pointlessly. **"If you can't attack, you can't block and you're not doing anything else,
   what good are you?"**
2. **Token generation.** Tokens are creatures, they have power, they kill people. **"They're not full cards,
   at least 1/1 tokens are not, but counting them as zero is even worse."**
3. **Preventing cards from being used.** Zvi groups this with #4 under his own term, **Blanking**: play a
   Masticore and **"you blank all the cards you have enough spare mana to kill with the Masticore and they
   stay blank until Masticore leaves play."**
4. **Dead cards** — blanked before the game even begins, **during deck construction.**

**His definition of VCA:**

> **Whenever you render one of your opponents' cards dead or useless, or you deal with all its effects, you
> gain +1 VCA, and for each card you spend doing this you lose -1 VCA, and your opponent does the same.**

Or more intuitively: **treat useless or dead cards, along with the cards spent making them useless, as if
they weren't there at all.**

**Seven strengths:** still simple (two lines); doesn't count dead, nullified or useless cards; very good for
long-term-advantage situations; a strong intermediate system; flexible in ways simpler systems aren't
("although that is obviously a double edged sword"); gets right nearly everything Cardboard Advantage got
right; and gets right a lot that Cardboard Advantage got wrong.

**Eight remaining holes:** still unclear on tokens; **not well defined** and context dependent; not complete;
widely misunderstood; **still treats all live cards as identical**, only registering Card Impact when it hits
zero, so **Mountain Goat and Rorix Bladewing are the same card**; concerned with who wins but far from a
complete story of it; it triggers on cards being drawn **without considering how they got into the deck**;
and finally:

> **By posing a series of situations, I can force anyone trying to nail down VCA into either contradicting
> himself or getting a situation very, very wrong.**

### 5. The Deck: card advantage in pure form

Weissman's *The Deck* is the case study for card advantage taken to its limit. Zvi is blunt that the
accompanying claims were overstated: the assertion that **card advantage wins games and tempo and other
forms of virtual card advantage do not** was "simply untrue even at the time, and it is much farther from
the truth now."

But the deck itself is the exception that proves the frame:

> **This is a deck designed to win on card advantage and nothing but card advantage. Almost all of its games
> can be thought of purely as a card war, no matter what its opponent is trying to do, because the failure
> to deal with one of its opponents' cards will normally kill it.**

Which licenses Weissman's vocabulary. Counting only the one card per turn, **Ancestral Recall becomes three
times as valuable as Time Walk**, and **Swords to Plowshares on a Giant Growth'd creature is like taking an
extra turn.** He can talk that way **"because that's how this deck allows him to think when it is working
properly. The game will last long enough to render other calculations irrelevant"** — though you still have
to count the VCA from cards like Moat.

Weissman's article is in this collection inside
[block-2-core-theory-paired/00-schools-of-magic-1996-04-10.md](../block-2-core-theory-paired/00-schools-of-magic-1996-04-10.md),
where Hahn documents the Weissman school.

## Where part one leaves off

The closing paragraph sets up the rest of the series and states the unification goal:

> **This is as far as the system can come without taking into account other types of advantage than just
> cards, even strictly virtual cards. In particular tempo (and the related idea of speed) and fixed
> resources like life must be separated from cards.**

The goal:

> **One major goal is to establish a relationship between them that will be useful in figuring out what is a
> good or bad trade between them, and in some situations these three things which together with information
> form the vast majority of all Magic resources can be thought of as only two things, or in rare cases one
> thing. If only one resource will determine the outcome of the game, then everything else can be thought of
> only in terms of it.**

**Four resources: cards, tempo/speed, life, and information.** Reducible to fewer when the game state allows.
That reduction is what the series calls the Grand Unified Theory, and it is precisely the move Chapin makes
six years later.

Next up, he says, is tempo — its separate development, the theories relating it to cards, and **"Tait's
attempt to invalidate tempo completely."**

## Notes for use

- **Read the five parts in order**, then the coda at
  [06](04-grand-unified-theory-philosophy-of-fire-2004-05-03.md). Part one is the survey and the standard;
  parts two through five build the machinery.
- **The Rule of Reflection is the takeaway if you read nothing else.** "Everything in Magic has value equal
  to the amount it increases your chance to win the game." It dissolves most card-advantage arguments by
  reminding you what the count was ever supposed to approximate. Note that Zvi credits **Justin Gary** with
  the correct phrasing, and that he only arrived at it after writing all five parts.
- **The strengths-and-weaknesses format is the method worth stealing.** Zvi never says a model is wrong. He
  says what it is for, where it holds, and exactly where it breaks. That is why he can defend Tait against
  Flores while also demolishing the system Tait built.
- **"Threat status is a modelling decision"** is the most under-quoted idea here, and it generalises. Which
  of your opponent's cards you decide to respect determines your whole read of the position.
- **Connects backward:** Flores's [Investment](../block-3-manabase-reference/00-investment-1998-03-20.md) is
  reviewed here by name, and Eric Taylor's
  [it's about TIME](../block-2-core-theory-paired/04-its-about-time-1997-08-11.md) is the tempo work part two
  takes up. **Connects forward:** Chapin's
  [Theory of Everything](07-innovations-the-theory-of-everything-2010-01-12.md) explicitly takes "Zvi's Grand
  Unified Theory to what I believe to be the inevitable extreme."
- **Recovery note:** the series was Brainburst **Premium**. Every 2004 Brainburst capture of these articles
  is a paywall teaser with the promotional blurb and nothing else. The full text exists only because
  TCGplayer later republished the Brainburst back catalogue free, and the Internet Archive crawled it. That
