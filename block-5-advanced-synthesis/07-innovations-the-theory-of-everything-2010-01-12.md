# The Theory of Everything

**Patrick Chapin — StarCityGames, January 12, 2010**

> Why it's in the list: Chapin's attempt to unify card advantage, tempo, and Philosophy of Fire into a
> single account of resources, pushing Zvi's work further toward marginal mana. Summarizes Zvi's Grand
> Unified Theory along the way, which is your only free access to it.

---

## The framing conversation

**The article opens on a conversation with Adrian Sullivan**, who had remarked that Rome was "the height
of my renown as a writer and theorist."

**Chapin's reply:**

> **"Adrian, I truly believe that the height of your renown as a writer and theorist may be in 2025."**

**What he meant:**

> **"I think in the future, most people will probably not be familiar with the majority of the decks,
> articles, or ideas that you have ever come up with. In the future, your tournament accomplishments will
> be mostly forgotten by the community as a whole…**
>
> **I believe that you will be best remembered for your contributions to the true Grand Unified Theory
> that is to come. Zvi, Flores, you, I, and others — we haven't done it yet, though we have a general
> sense of what it is we are developing, discovering, and putting words to."**

**Chapin's own warning about structure:** the first half is heavier and more abstract; **the second half
is basic material on Card Advantage, Tempo, and the Philosophy of Fire that may be particularly useful to
mid-level and less experienced players.** He explicitly invites skipping sections.

---

## The physics analogy

**Borrowed deliberately.** Physicists describe four forces: **Electromagnetism**, **the Weak Nuclear
Force** (atomic decay, radiation), **the Strong Nuclear Force** (holds atoms together), and **Gravity**.

> **Something that has troubled physicists to no end is how awkward it feels that we need several totally
> different systems of physics to describe these seemingly fundamental aspects of reality.**

> **There is a certain aesthetic beauty to a unified theory that can describe everything. After all, why
> should some things obey a certain set of laws and other things follow completely different laws?**

**In 1979 Electromagnetism and the Weak Force were proven connected** — the Electroweak Theory, which won
Nobel Prizes. **Unifying that with the Strong Nuclear Force is the Grand Unified Theory; adding Gravity
would be the Theory of Everything.**

**The mapping Chapin uses for Magic:**

| Physics | Magic |
|---|---|
| Electromagnetic + Weak → **Electroweak** | **Card Advantage + Virtual Card Advantage** |
| **Strong Nuclear Force** | **The Philosophy of Fire** |
| **Gravity** — omnipresent, hardest to unify | **Tempo** |

## A history of the fundamental forces in Magic

**Chapin's potted intellectual history, which is a useful map of the rest of this library:**

> **Back in the mid-nineties just about everyone was stone cold awful at the game. Even the people who
> were good did not really know how to put into words why the things that caused them to win worked the
> way they did. These were the dark ages of Magic theory.**

**The named lineage:** **Brian Weissman, Adrian Sullivan, Eric Taylor** and others built the foundation
that **Zvi Mowshowitz and Michael Flores** brought to the masses — along with **Brian Hacker, Robert Hahn,
John Shuler, Jamie Wakefield, George Baxter, and Frank Kusumoto.**

### 1. Card Advantage — Weissman

> **Brian Weissman coined the term "Card Advantage" and described Magic theory in terms of the struggle to
> acquire an advantage when it came to this sort of resource. This fundamentally changed the game
> forever**, and **The Deck** was born.

> **Overnight, thousands of players rose to greatness by taking advantage of the original "net deck."**

**And then it broke.** People started seeing the limits with Fastbond and Dark Ritual, **but it wasn't
brought to the forefront until Mirage and the beginning of the first modern Limited era.**

### 2. Tempo — Eric Taylor

> **Eric Taylor is the first writer I know of to really begin to describe "Tempo" in Magic** — followed by
> **Brian Hacker, Scott Johns, Alan Comer, Gary Wise**, and others.

**Cards that pointed at a world where card advantage wasn't the center:** Winter Orb, Force of Will,
Contagion, Man-o'-War, Gemstone Mine, Arcane Denial, Unsummon, Propaganda.

**And the honest admission that nobody could define it:**

> **We could talk about tempo in certain contexts, but what was it really? Was it mana? Was it land
> advantage? Was it time? Is it tempo to Memory Lapse a Dark Ritual? What about attacking?**

> **Many could feel what was being described; the concept has been among the most elusive in all of Magic
> theory.**

### 3. The Philosophy of Fire — Sullivan

> **Adrian was trying to find the words to describe why it was that spending mana and a card to Lightning
> Bolt an opponent could be good. You were losing Card Advantage. You were losing Tempo. Why then was
> Lightning Bolting an opponent so ruthlessly effective?**

**And the crucial note on how the concept grew past its author:**

> **Michael Flores actually took the concept that Sullivan had uncovered and ran with it, taking it to a
> level that was far beyond what Adrian had ever meant the term to describe. One cannot fault Flores for
> this, though, as the direction he took it is much larger and more useful.**

> **Adrian talked about burn and life total as a resource, but Flores pointed out that cards in your
> library and poison counters you could afford to take were really the same sort of thing.**

> **The Philosophy of Fire was evolving from the measure of how close you are to losing the game, as Adrian
> originally designed it, and towards a measure of actual resources that you had to work with.**

*(Note the naming irony Chapin records: the theory is called the Philosophy of Fire **"much to Sullivan's
chagrin"**, after Flores's article rather than anything Sullivan called it.)*

## Zvi's Grand Unified Theory — summarized

**This section is the reason the reading list flags this article as "your only free access" to Zvi's
work.**

> **Building on Flores and Sullivan, Zvi wrote The Grand Unified Theory, which began to talk about the link
> between Card Advantage and the Philosophy of Fire.**

> **While the article touches on burn spells being used for tempo, it is really about the fundamental link
> between how much cards are worth with regards to "Card Advantage" versus your opponent's life total
> reaching zero.**

> **The article is primarily about Shock, the at-the-time baseline burn spell, and how a card was worth two
> life and a mana — and that Shock was at the time the center of Magic physics.**

**Chapin's stated ambition beyond it:**

> **I want to take Zvi's Grand Unified Theory to what I believe to be the inevitable extreme. I want to
> describe a system for describing every sort of resource (force) in the game completely independent of
> context.**

> **In addition, I want to link not only Card Advantage and the Philosophy of Fire — I want to link Tempo
> and demonstrate a clear and elegant connection between the three.**

# The Theory of Everything

## First: what it's for

**Chapin puts the utility question before the theory, deliberately:**

> **It is no good to sit around and pat ourselves on the back at clever definitions if they do not produce
> real results. The Theory of Everything is meant to be a useful framework that can actually be applied to
> win games, not just fascinate like so much String Theory.**

**The Zvi lesson he builds on:**

> **One of the most important lessons I ever learned from Zvi is that the whole point of Magic strategy,
> the core of Magic theory, is about having more and better options than your opponent. This means gaining
> more and better options and denying your opponent many or quality options.**

## The object of the game

> **Magic is a game of resources. You begin with some, you gain some over time, but all are just means to
> an end.**

> **The most important of these options is the option to continue to play the game. The real object of a
> game of Magic, as far as winning is concerned, is to deny your opponent this option.**

> **At its core, Magic is a game of submission, where every move you make should be leading to eventually
> taking from your opponent the option to continue the game.**

**He generalizes across win conditions:** reducing to zero from burn, decking them with Jace, ten poison
counters, Door to Nothingness — **"these are all just ways of denying your opponent the option to continue
to play the game."**

### The hard consequence about survival

**This is the most bracing passage in the article:**

> **You don't get any extra credit for staying alive longer. None.**

> **If you are trying to survive until time runs out, your goal is essentially to remove your opponent's
> option to play the game by denying him the resource of time, which — while you are restricted in how much
> you can manipulate — is a resource that only exists in a finite amount, and to pretend otherwise is to rob
> yourself of a more clear understanding.**

> **The only reason it is so often right to make decisions to stay alive is because if you can no longer
> play the game, you cannot take away your opponent's option to continue playing the game.**

**The clean demonstration:**

> **If you cast Final Fortune, you will die at the end of the turn. If you remove your opponent's option to
> continue playing the game long enough to see the end of the turn, then it doesn't matter. You win.**

## The three resources — the actual theory

**The organizing insight, stated as a trichotomy that is exhaustive by construction:**

> **There are resources you begin the game with but do not gain naturally over time. There are resources
> you gain naturally over time but do not begin the game with. Finally there are resources you both begin
> the game with and gain naturally over time.**

> **Every resource in Magic can be described in these terms.**

| Resource type | Availability | Maps to the turn |
|---|---|---|
| **The Philosophy of Fire** | **Start with it, gain no more** | The option to continue playing (life, library, poison) |
| **Tempo** | **Don't start with it, gain it each turn** | Land drop, untap, attack phase |
| **Card Economy** | **Both** | Opening hand **and** the draw step |

> **These three concepts deal with the basic manipulation of resources in Magic. The reason there are three
> is because there are three ways for a resource to be available to you in a game.**

**On each:**

**Philosophy of Fire** — *"resources that you start with and get no more of, unless you pay for them in
some way with the use of specific cards. The most well understood example is your life total, but this also
includes the cards in your library, how many poison counters you can endure without dying, and so on. This
is really just a way of describing the option to continue playing the game."*

**Tempo** — *"resources that you have the opportunity to utilize every turn. The most important of these
to understand is, surprisingly, the land drop — but the untap phase and attack phase are also important."*
Also upkeep, discard, and once-per-turn activations like planeswalkers or Icy Manipulator. **"You start the
game with absolutely no Tempo."**

**Card Economy** — *"a unique resource because you start the game with seven cards (so it is sort of like
the Philosophy of Fire), but you also draw a card each turn (so it is sort of like Tempo)."*

> **Card Advantage is the only fundamental resource that occupies a large amount of territory in both areas
> — so much so that sometimes it is useful to view it as one, sometimes the other, sometimes both, and
> sometimes neither.**

# Part two: the three forces in practice

## Card Advantage vs. Card Economy

> **Card Advantage is really just the positive side of the spectrum of a concept known as Card Economy.**

> **Card Advantage is just how many you have. Card Economy is how good they are.**

**The money analogy:** three Spectral Procession tokens versus a Broodmate Dragon token **"is a lot like
someone with three one-dollar bills versus someone with a single five-dollar bill."**

**The test he gives:**

> **When evaluating if something is a matter of card economy, the only question you need to ask is "Does
> this relate to the resource that I start with and gain over time?"**

> **When evaluating how important this is, ask yourself how it relates to your ability to get more options
> that you want.**

**The example that shows card advantage can be negative:**

> **If you have exiled all of your opponent's victory conditions and the only way you can lose is by running
> out of cards before your opponent, drawing two cards will give you two options that are irrelevant at the
> cost of a resource that is vital — cards in your library.**

**And the full accounting of a Divination:**

> **When you cast Divination, you spend a card, three mana (that you gained from part of your untap step),
> and two cards from your library** — which mean, among other things, **the option to keep playing the game
> for two more turns.**

> **The most important factor is not what gives you the most cards, but rather what gives you the best
> options.** It might be much better to have **one Cruel Ultimatum than two Terminates and a Lightning
> Bolt.**

## Tempo

**Chapin's stated reason for a new framework:** he wasn't satisfied with existing writing on the subject
when writing *Next Level Magic*.

> **Tempo deals with the resources that you gain every turn, but do not possess initially.**

**Why the land drop is the foundation:**

> **You start the game with no land in play and have the ability to play one land a turn. This basic
> arrangement dictates the pace of the game, and consequently most of the conversations about tempo.**

> **You can play one land a turn, so something that breaks that rule is worth some amount. This is why
> Signets cost two mana, whereas Graven Cairns costs no mana.**

**The Dark Ritual reframe, which is the cleanest statement of his tempo model:**

> **Dark Ritual is a high-tempo play not because it produces mana, but because it gives you more of a
> resource that you would normally have to wait for — namely, the amount of mana you could have in a turn.**

> **Who cares if you are down a card if you resolve Necropotence on turn 1 instead of turn 3?**

**Remand, priced precisely:**

> **Remand lets you trade two mana for however much mana your opponent spent. In general, if you are
> consistently Remanding spells that cost three or more, you are gaining tempo. If you are Remanding spells
> that cost two or less, you are losing it.**

> **Why is two a loss? Because your card is reactive and requires you to leave the two lands untapped
> before your opponent ever tries to cast a spell.**

### The rule that governs all of it

> **Tempo, like Card Advantage, is only worth what you can do with it.**

> **Who cares if you Stone Rain your opponent if you don't take advantage of the fact that he is temporarily
> behind on mana?**

> **If you draw seven extra cards and they are all land, they may be worthless; if you set your opponent back
> seven turns and do nothing with it, did it really matter?**

**The Lightning Bolt example makes the accounting precise:**

> Bolt a Scion of Oona and **you have gained tempo because essentially you are ahead by two mana. This is not
> the end of the tale, however, since that two mana is for naught if you just leave two land untapped for the
> rest of the turn.**

> **On the other hand, if you played a Sower of Temptation and your opponent plays a Scion in response, which
> you answer with a Bolt, you are still way ahead.** Your opponent spent seven mana and two cards; you
> answered with five mana and two cards. **It doesn't matter if you tapped the lands or not.**

### Faeries as the worked archetype

> **Every Remove Soul, every Broken Ambitions, every Agony Warp, every Mistbind Clique — they are all just
> "Time Walk," plays that try to prevent the opponent from advancing the game.**

**And the deckbuilding lesson:**

> **When you are building a tempo-based deck, the key is to figure out what advantage you are gaining as a
> result of the tempo you are producing.**

> **This is why good Faeries players mulligan so much. They know that if they don't have a card like
> Bitterblossom, Ancestral Visions, Jace, or Mistbind Clique, then what good is all the time they are buying
> themselves?**

**The Winter Orb illustration is the best demonstration of tempo being contextual:**

> **Memory Lapse normally gains you only two mana on a Wrath of God — but if there is a Winter Orb in play, it
> may be worth four turns. And if you have a Black Knight and a Fallen Askari out, those four turns may be
> worth sixteen damage.**

**And the diagnostic for playing against it:**

> **If your opponent is beating you with tempo, ask yourself what he is specifically doing that is gaining
> the tempo. Then address that.**

## The Philosophy of Fire

> **Your life total is really a resource that you start the game with, but do not gain over time — which is,
> essentially, the opposite of tempo.**

> **Many players make the mistake of thinking their life total matters beyond the fact that when it is zero
> you are dead. Your life total is just a number. That is not what is important. It is a resource. That is
> all it is.**

**Two worked decisions about spending life for options:**

**1.** You have Tidehollow Strix and Agony Warp; opponent attacks with Kathari Screecher. **Take two, waiting
for a second creature so the Agony Warp kills two.**

**2.** Opponent attacks with Anathemancer; you hold Terminate and suspect Demigod of Revenge.

> **You are paying two life for the option to kill a Demigod. If you can beat him if he has no Demigod, maybe
> you can beat him with no Demigod and two less life — but if you Terminate the Anathemancer, you may have
> two more life, but so what? If you can't beat Demigod, you can't beat Demigod.**

> **The life matters… but does it matter as much as the option? That is what being good at Magic is all
> about.**

### The threshold effect

> **When your opponent is at twenty, an Incinerate is hardly threatening — the difference between twenty and
> seventeen isn't typically going to impact their game. However, if you Incinerate them and they are now at
> four, suddenly they can no longer safely tap out.**

**And the practical consequence for red decks:**

> **This is a big reason why you should generally not play out all of your lands late in the game with red
> decks — you want them to think they're in danger, so keep the lands in your hand and the information
> hidden.**

> **If the opponent can't afford to tap out, that's going to make it much harder for them to play Mistbind
> Clique. Every turn they don't Mistbind Clique, you have saved four life.**

### Blocking as countering

> Let's say you block a Boggart Ram-Gang with a Mulldrifter. **On the surface this appears to violate our
> policy about chump blocking. However, against a red deck, it's like you are "countering" an Incinerate.**

**The general question:**

> **Is the resource you are trading worth less than the life points?**

> **There is nothing wrong with throwing away creatures and other resources to deal extra damage or to
> prevent damage — but bad players do this too much. Decent players avoid it. Good players look for when it
> is right to do.**

### Lifegain, correctly valued

> **Many players make the mistake of thinking that lifegain is bad. The truth is that lifegain can be a very
> powerful weapon against someone who cares about life totals.**

> **If you play Kitchen Finks against a red deck, it is not just two bodies that they have to get through, it
> is also two Shocks that have been undone by the lifegain. That is a lot of card advantage for a green/white
> card that draws you no cards.**

### Counters as Philosophy of Fire

**The most elegant small argument in the article:**

> You are at seven; opponent has three cards. **If you let a Flame Javelin resolve, now you must counter
> Incinerate or die. On the other hand, if you counter Flame Javelin, you could in theory let two Incinerates
> resolve without losing.**

> **In a way, countering Flame Javelin is actually a form of card advantage, as it means you can save two
> Counterspells that would have had to be used, at the cost of just one.**

**And the habit he recommends:**

> **Get in the habit of asking yourself what life points are going to be worth to you and to your opponent as
> the game progresses.**

**A closing note on planeswalkers** — then new to the game:

> **These powerful cards are primarily vulnerable to attacking creatures and spells that can deal damage
> directly. As such, the Philosophy of Fire has taken on a new dimension, as it now has overlap with your
> defense against planeswalkers.**

## The conclusion, in Chapin's own words

> **In a game of Magic, resources are options and can be viewed in three ways: that which you begin with but
> do not gain naturally (The Philosophy of Fire), that which you gain naturally but do not begin with
> (Tempo), and those that are both (Card Economy).**

> **The most important of these, like a King on the chess board, is the option to continue playing the game.
> The object of a game of Magic is to take away this option from your opponent.**

> **The way you do this is by manipulating your resources to get more and better options while denying your
> opponent the same, in an effort to eventually take away their option to continue to play.**

> **This is at the core of every element of Magic strategy, and when faced with a situation, asking oneself
> which of these three types of resources is in question can provide the player with the best framework for
> evaluating both strategy and tactics.**

> **Each of these three fundamental types of resources has properties that can be understood and exploited,
> studied and improved upon, but they are all just parts of the same thing: the option to play the game.**

## Notes for use

- **The taxonomy is the contribution, and it's genuinely elegant.** Three resource types, distinguished not
  by what they are but by **how they become available**: start-only, gain-only, or both. That's exhaustive by
  construction, which is why Chapin can claim it covers everything. It also explains *why* card advantage is
  the hardest of the three to reason about — it's the one that sits in both categories.
- **The practical question to carry away:** faced with any decision, **ask which of the three resources is
  actually in question.** That alone reframes most confusing spots.
- **"You don't get extra credit for staying alive" is the line that changes play.** Survival is instrumental,
  never terminal. The Final Fortune example proves it cleanly. Compare Reid Duke's playing-to-your-outs
  argument in `block-1-the-spine/24-playing-from-ahead-playing-from-behind-2015-03-30.md`, which is the same
  principle applied to a losing position.
- **"Tempo is only worth what you can do with it"** is the single most useful corrective in the article, and
  it applies symmetrically: a Stone Rain you don't exploit and seven cards that are all lands are the same
  failure. The Winter Orb example shows the flip side — the *same* Memory Lapse is worth two mana or sixteen
  damage depending on context.
- **The Dark Ritual reframe is the best short definition of tempo anywhere in this library:** it doesn't
  produce mana, it produces *mana you would otherwise have had to wait for*. That connects tempo directly to
  the land-drop rule and to Eric Taylor's original mana/time identity.
- **On Zvi's Grand Unified Theory:** the summary here is genuinely all you get without hunting the original
  down. Zvi's contribution was linking **card advantage to the Philosophy of Fire** with Shock as the fixed
  exchange rate — one card ≈ two life ≈ one mana. **Chapin's addition is folding tempo into the same
  system.** If you want the original, the Library of Leng is the place to look.
- **Read directly against Flores's Breakdown of Theory** (`06-the-breakdown-of-theory-2007-12-17.md`), which
  is the other half of Block 5 and a different kind of unification. **Flores divides the game
  *chronologically* into three phases; Chapin divides the *resources* into three types.** They're
  complementary, not competing — and Chapin cites Flores's phases explicitly in his tempo section. Flores in
  turn quotes Chapin's three-resource passage at the top of
  `block-2-core-theory-paired/07-2-3-4-the-philosophy-of-fire-and-the-high-water-mark-2012-07-27.md`, which
  makes this article the hinge between them.
- **This article is also the best intellectual history in the library.** Weissman → Taylor → Sullivan →
  Flores → Zvi → Chapin, with credit carefully assigned at each step, including Chapin's honest note that
  Flores took Sullivan's idea well past what Sullivan intended. Every name in that chain has a file in this
  repo or an entry in `block-0-orientation/04-shoulders-of-giants-2013-08-06.md`.
- **Reading caution:** the card examples are Standard and Extended circa 2009–2010 (Faeries, Jund, Zendikar
  block). The theory is entirely portable; the examples require period familiarity. The second half is
  deliberately written for less experienced players and holds up better than the first half's physics
  analogy, which is decorative.
