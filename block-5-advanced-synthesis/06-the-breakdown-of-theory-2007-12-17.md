# The Breakdown of Theory

**Mike Flores — StarCityGames, December 17, 2007**

> Why it's in the list: a framework dividing games into three phases — the phase where mana decides
> things, the phase where errors decide things, and the phase where one player has a trump and the rest is
> formality. Reframes "how did I lose" into "which phase lost it."

---

## What it claims to be

> **This is a framework for Magic theory that I have been working on for a little over a year. It isn't
> perfect — meaning that it isn't exhaustive at present — but I believe it to be better at describing the
> progression of duels than any other working theory at this point.**

**Three broad, progressive phases**, each with its own traits and rules:

| | Name | Defined by |
|---|---|---|
| **Phase I** | *Basically Manascrewed* | **not having enough mana** |
| **Phase II** | *Mostly Errors* | **mistakes** |
| **Phase III** | *Trump Mode* | **one player dictating the field of battle** |

> **Most if not all decks begin a game in Phase I, and many — though enlighteningly, not all — would
> progress to Phase III if they were not pre-empted and defeated by their opponents, given sufficient draw
> steps and land drops.**

**Flores's own postscript on the terminology:**

> **Only now do I realize that "Phase" was a terrible word to use for this theoretical framework given that
> we already have "phases" in Magic. Sorry about that.**

---

# Phase I — Basically Manascrewed

## The Minimum Game Threshold

> **Phase I is defined by any points in the game that exist before crossing the Minimum Game Threshold.**

> **The Minimum Game Threshold is a mana point where a deck can reasonably operate** — interact, make
> plays, "play Magic" — **though not necessarily dictate the field of battle.**

**The two canonical numbers:**

| Deck type | Threshold |
|---|---|
| **Most beatdown decks** | **two mana** |
| **Most control decks** | **four mana** |

> **You will generally see bigger mana requirements the smaller the format.**

**His evidence for two mana on the beatdown side:** even in big diverse formats, **Boros-Zoo needs two
lands to use its best cards** (Tarmogoyf, Lightning Helix), and even a twelve-to-sixteen-one-drop deck
can't get by on one — **Kird Ape is quite unplayable without a Forest, and if you choose Stomping Ground
you can't play Isamaru or Savannah Lions.** Such decks benefit from four (Jitte plus equip, Flametongue
Kavu), **but two is where they can spend most of their hands.**

**And for four on the control side:**

> **Four mana has been the universal mana flashpoint for Standard control decks for as long as I've been
> playing.** Below four, a control deck can counter more than once per turn **only against a sloppy,
> blind, or wildly inexperienced pressure player.** It is conveniently **also the mana cost of Wrath of
> God, Mutilate, Damnation, Nevinyrral's Disk.**

## The consequence for control decks

> If both decks are slow on drops early, **it is easy to see why beatdown should be advantaged. Beatdown
> only needs two mana to do most of what it has to do, whereas control is a scatterbrained wreck before
> four.**

**So control lowers its curve** — Force Spike, Spell Snare, and assorted two-mana responses **joined only
by the fact that they are cheap and sometimes effective.**

**And the cost of that fix, which is the sharp observation:**

> **Allotting for this part of the game necessarily makes participating control decks more draw dependent
> and less consistent in the early game, and in fact throughout the game.**

> **Because some cards exist for no other reason than that the control deck needs to be able to play
> something before it can cast Wrath of God, many of those pulls are dead later on. Consider topdecking
> Force Spike on turn 15.**

# Phase III — Trump Mode

*(Flores presents Phase III before Phase II, because Phase II is defined by what the other two aren't.)*

> **Phase III is a special point that exists for some decks where that deck is actively dictating the field
> of battle and only a small subset of the opponent's cards still matter.**

> **For those decks, getting to Phase III is really what they are all about; if left unchecked for a turn or
> so, they basically win.**

## Where he found it: Masques Block, summer 2000

**The story is the clearest illustration in the article.**

He played **Rebel Informer** — *"kind of a false trump, initially effective but not best."* It was good
enough for most opponents **but was itself trumped by Mageta the Lion.**

**And then the key sentence:**

> **"Card advantage" as I understood it at that point broke down. All the raw cards I had stockpiled with
> Rebel Informer washed away under the potential / ostensible "card disadvantage" of Mageta. Mageta would
> sometimes drop two cards to deal with nothing more than a Chimeric Idol.**

> **It took me a while to understand it, but card advantage and most card interactions simply ceased in the
> Mageta-defined Trump Mode.**

**Why:** Mageta was dictating the field of battle. **Its limit was simply the number of cards in hand.**

> **Until Mageta was removed, the opponent White deck simply could not win; therefore only cards that
> removed Mageta — or, theoretically against a sloppy player, out-lasting Mageta — were the only viable
> paths.**

**The format's response, in stages:** first four Mageta to outnumber the 0–2 Mageta builds, then
**maximizing the number of cards that mattered in the last zone of the game** — Story Circle to buy time,
Afterlife to kill your Mageta and drop my own, then cards to supplement Mageta in Trump Mode (Blinding
Angel) or protect one or both (Cho-Manno's Blessing).

## Trump Mode is not "the end game"

> **The Trump Mode differs from the imprecise "end game" specifically in that the trumping player is
> actively defining the field of battle and only a few cards matter on the opponent's part.**

> **If you want to succeed in a particular format, special attention must be paid to specifically those
> cards.**

**Examples of cards that function *after* the opponent has apparently already won:**

- **Masashiro Kuroda won a Pro Tour** specifically because his deck could win **after his opponent
  functionally won** — Nassif resolving Tooth and Nail. *"Not easy."*
- **Kami of Ancient Law** in Extended: functional even after an Enduring Ideal opponent has "gotten
  there," **and it works even when locked under Dovescape.**

## The worked case: Dragonstorm

**Heezy Red Fever** (Herberholz / Chapin / Nassif) is his modern example of a deck whose Trump Mode is
hard to interact with:

> **Dragonstorm is a powerful multi-pronged assault that attacks the opponent from several disparate angles
> simultaneously.** The method for activating **Spinerock Knoll** is both mana efficient and proactive — **a
> red deck wants to deal seven to the face, the cheaper the better** — and if all else fails, the opponent
> faces **a multiple must-counter scenario against the burn.**

**And the point of the exercise: the answers are extremely specific.**

> **Numerous good players have brainstormed about what kinds of cards, specifically, would function even as
> Dragonstorm approaches its unique Trump Mode. Note that they are very specific: that's how this point in
> the game works.**

1. **Pestermite** — tap Spinerock Knoll just before the seventh damage resolves. **Usually the opponent
   won't have enough mana to make up the lost damage and activate the Knoll. A subtly card advantageous
   play.**
2. **Mistbind Clique** — as above.
3. **Trickbind** — **you can Trickbind the Knoll, pocketing the good will of the critical mass the opponent
   spent to get there.** You can also Trickbind the spell under the Knoll, on the assumption it's a
   Dragonstorm, **but it might be a Hellkite or something other than a Storm spell, so be careful.**

## Trump Mode as a state of mind: the burn argument

**Josh Ravitz's rule:** the more burn a deck played, the better against control (and worse against
creatures). Half the reason is dodging Wrath. **The Phase III half is the interesting one:**

> **The beatdown decks could narrow the number of relevant cards in the opposing deck with each play.**

**Why burn narrows and creatures don't:**

> Superficially, it is easier to deal with a creature in the short term than a burn spell: **you can
> counter it; you can Wrath it dead; you can Remand or Repeal it for velocity and tempo and decide to deal
> with it later; you can profit from it with a Faith's Fetters; you can put a Dragon in front of it and
> laugh it off.**

> **However, a burn spell — you can only counter it.**

> **At some point every burn spell becomes a must-counter because it is going to kill you. You can only
> stop these cards with counters, so if you run out of counters, you lose.**

**And counters degrade:** **Remand is a poor answer to Shock or Lightning Helix, and Rewind is a dangerous
response if the opponent has any additional cards in hand.**

**The blue decks' response** was to refuse the exchange entirely: **use counters in Phase II for tempo and
try to go Trump Mode themselves** — Niv-Mizzet plus untap (Wafo-Tapa), Keiga/Meloku plus untap into Blaze
(Lebedowicz), Heartbeat combo (Bracht).

**And the counter-counter:** beatdown played **Flames of the Blood Hand**, specifically against Loxodon
Hierarch, Faith's Fetters, and similar cards **designed to delay the beatdown opponent's Trump Mode.**

# Phase II — Mostly Errors

**The framing question:**

> **If Phase I is largely draw-dependent and defined by not having sufficient mana, and Phase III is a
> special point where the player in Trump Mode is increasingly if not automatically likely to win — where
> and when are most of the decisions in a game made? Where is skill?**

> **The answer is Phase II.**

> **Phase II is typically the longest part of the game. It encompasses everything in between first not
> being manascrewed, then actually winning — that is, a lot.**

**Where your mistakes live:**

> **Have you ever mis-tapped your mana? You probably did that in Phase II.** In Phase I you might not have
> had a better option; in Phase III you have so much mana that erring probably didn't cost you much
> flexibility. **Have you ever made a bad attack or block? Phase II.**

## What defines a good player

**Three characteristics:**

> **1. They preserve their options as the game progresses.**
> **2. They make better short-term decisions** — they tend not to toss away games in hand, so as to
> consistently "win the games they are supposed to."
> **3. Historically, they have better mental games and can steal value or games with bluffs, intimidation,
> and pre-emptively dictating the field of battle.**

**And the crucial structural point about how skill works in Magic:**

> **Because you can't actually play any better than the natural limit dictated by the boundaries of the
> game, the scale goes down, not up.**

> **Out-playing an opponent is mostly making fewer errors than he does, and secondarily not falling for
> traps. There aren't any outstanding, superhuman plays in Magic. There are only the right plays — and not
> falling for the bait.**

**Why the errors concentrate in Phase II:**

> **In Phase I we are bound by inherent scarcity; we don't have a lot of room to wiggle around, to err. In
> Phase III our plays are largely dictated to us by circumstance** — and from the other side of the table,
> **we're just as much on autopilot.**

> **Therefore the majority of our bad reads, bad taps, and bad tutor decisions occur in Phase II.**

**And why it's the good part:**

> **Phase II is also the best part of the game. Card advantage matters most in Phase II. Everything we've
> learned about trading for value, stealing cards, and forcing down key permanents against resistance only
> matters in Phase II. It is where we win all of our attrition fights.**

## The Eternal-formats argument

**Flores uses the framework to explain a common complaint:**

> **I would guess that most of the disdain non-Eternal players have for, say, Vintage is that so little of
> the format exists in Phase II** — whereas the vast majority of important decisions in Magic elsewhere
> occur there.

> **"Degenerate" formats can jump from a minimum resource base directly to the end game. For many of these,
> the entirety of Phase II sometimes occurs over half of one turn.**

> **With fewer opportunities to meaningfully err, the window to out-play the opponent can become
> essentially meaningless.**

### The redefinition of interaction

**This is the article's most quotable structural claim:**

> **The essential core of interaction is not turn suppression as it has been historically characterized, but
> Phase suppression.**

> **There are two basic goals in Magic: interactively to push the opponent into a lower Phase, where he has
> fewer if any relevant interactions — or to rocket to later Phases.**

### The Bob Maher illustration

<summary>Bob Maher — Two-Land Belcher, Legacy, December 2007</summary>

**Creature (15)** — 4 Elvish Spirit Guide · 4 Simian Spirit Guide · 3 Street Wraith · 4 Tinder Wall

**Artifact (16)** — 4 Chrome Mox · 4 Goblin Charbelcher · 4 Lion's Eye Diamond · 4 Lotus Petal

**Sorcery (15)** — 4 Burning Wish · 3 Empty the Warrens · 4 Land Grant · 4 Rite of Flame

**Instant (12)** — 4 Dark Ritual · 4 Desperate Ritual · 4 Seething Song

**Land (2)** — 1 Bayou · 1 Taiga

**Sideboard** — 1 Cave-In · 1 Channel the Suns · 1 Duress · 1 Empty the Warrens · 1 Gamble ·
4 Pyroblast · 1 Tendrils of Agony · 4 Tormod's Crypt · 1 Unmask
</details>

> **Bob would just mulligan until he had a hand that could win on the first turn. Maher won eight games, not
> one of which consisted of action beyond the second turn. Bob's less impressive wins were all 14 Goblins on
> the first turn.**

**The analysis in Phase terms:**

> **As early as the first turn, Bob goes from being completely manascrewed to an explosive sequence
> culminating in Empty the Warrens or a lethal Goblin Charbelcher.**

> **The extent of The Great One's skill was counting whether or not he had it.** The extent of the
> opponent's interaction — **remember the opponent would almost always be stuck in Phase I in games this
> short** — **would be whether or not he had Force of Will**, less than 50% raw provided he was meaningfully
> blue. **And even then it would have to be correctly placed, and might be meaningless if the finisher was
> Empty the Warrens.**

> **To my knowledge there was only one game where the opponent had a relevant choice… and he blew it.** A
> B/G/W Pikula-Junk player **had the option of second-turn Gaddock Teeg, which would have been a soft lock
> against Bob's critical fours. But he instead went for Dark Confidant.**

### Flores's answer to the "different test of skill" defense

He states the counter-argument fairly — that one high-stakes decision may be worth more than many small
ones — and then answers it:

> **I would counter that there is a richness to being able to play out of mistakes, and that a consistent
> finality to having only one decision can cut off a whole, and largely unexplored, branch of execution.**

**His example is Gabriel Nassif**, whom he compares to poker's Daniel Negreanu:

> **Nassif has a rare ability to play out of errors that would crack the frames and sink the games of
> mentally less talented pros. While pundits with a little knowledge are quick to point out Nassif's
> high-profile errors, few remember that unlike most people in his position, Papa Hat almost uniquely pulls
> out to win from these mistakes, more often than not.**

# Applying the framework to existing concepts

**The three broad applications Flores states:**

> **1. Successful interaction is largely based in suppressing the opponent's ability to reach the next
> Phase.**
> **2. Most "broken" strategies are based on accelerating "prematurely" to a supernormal Phase.**
> **3. Most interactive cards are effective only during one or two Phases.**

## Consistency = crossing the Minimum Game Threshold

> **Consistency, specifically mana consistency, is about one thing: breaching the Minimum Game Threshold.**

**The five-color Domain example:** very powerful in Phase II, can dictate Phase III with Global Ruin or
armored Bringers. **But its failing is getting out of Phase I.**

> **You can look at an ostensibly "good" two- or three-land hand — with a Sakura-Tribe Elder — and still be
> completely unable to make a meaningful play inside of ten cards.**

> **This axis of Magic design is largely a balance between Phase I and Phase III.**

**And his stated personal bias, with its cost:**

> **The reason I prefer to err on Phase I is that at the level where I usually play, given room to choose,
> bluff, trade, and interact, I tend to have the tools to out-play the competition. However… this can cost
> me in the later stages of Phase II going into the last, if I come up short on power.**

He gives a concrete failure: an Extended Gold deck that beat the top and bottom of the field but went 0-2
at a PTQ against slower attrition decks. **"It is important to note that my deck had no natural Phase III
capability."**

## Life gain, reframed

**Flores revises his own earlier position** — he had been "at the forefront of calling life gain a kind of
card advantage," a theory he credits to **Mike Donais** and folded into the Philosophy of Fire.

**The corrected version:**

> **Life gain is largely irrelevant** — the same as taking lots of damage from your lands is largely
> irrelevant — **because most matchups are strategic blowouts. If you win, you can win by 50 given
> sufficient turns.**

**It matters in exactly two situations:**

1. **In a thin-margin matchup, probably midrange versus midrange, that threatens to be decided in the
   latter stages of Phase II rather than proceeding to Phase III.**
2. **As a measure to keep a beatdown or burn deck out of Phase III.**

**The mechanism, stated precisely:**

> **The concrete value is how the Firemane Angel keeps the opponent stuck in Phase II while the control can
> advance to a superior Phase II where it can define the field of battle, or even move to Phase III.**

> **The whole "deal ten with creatures" goes to 12 to 15 to 30 against a Firemane Angel and some Lightning
> Helixes, until the burn damage horizon threatens to disappear altogether.**

*(He notes a period aphorism from Dan Bridy: casting Gerrard's Wisdom against Deadguy Red was "Time Walk
your graveyard.")*

## Discard — where theory breaks down

**Flores opens with a loss:** at Pro Tour Chicago, playing PT Junk against Jay Elarar's Replenish. Stuck on
one black source, he had to choose between **Duress** and holding **Ebony Charm** in case of a topdecked
Replenish. He Duressed. **Elarar topdecked the Replenish and won.**

> **Discard — and the ill-defined "disruption" in general — is the most important place where theory can
> break down when not examined with a Phase-specific lens.**

**He names his own bad training:** Chris Pikula taught him that aggression and discard was the best strategy
against combo. **"There are numerous problems with this blanket concept that I didn't understand in
1999–2000."**

### The two corrections

**1. Discard is only worthwhile in Phases I and II.**

> Our typical play is Duress on turn 1. **This is the autopilot play that can dictate the remainder of the
> match, but it isn't actually correct, strategically.** We do it **because Duress costs one, which is how
> much mana we have on turn 1.**

> Discard **actually gets better as the game progresses** — the opponent has a better chance of drawing the
> card you want to pull — **but it also has a steep dropoff. It is completely useless in Phase III. While it
> gets progressively better throughout Phase II, if you let the opponent actually get to Phase III you'll
> probably just lose.**

**2. Discard can't stop the opponent from topdecking.**

Alan Comer's point after the Elarar loss, which Flores initially misread: **not that the topdeck was
likely, but that Duress could not have saved him from it, whereas Ebony Charm might have.**

> **Discard is only valuable insofar as it can keep the opponent in Phase II or lower while you actually set
> up to win the game.**

> **It's not discard that is great against combo, it's discard *and aggression*. You need to actually kill
> the opponent, not just set him back a card.**

**And the general warning:**

> **Attrition, in and of itself, is a poor strategy for dealing with broken cards.**

> **Trading at value only works while you are not being trumped. You can be ahead on all metrics — cards,
> mana, life, board position — and it can all fall apart with one topdeck.**

His example: the 2004 G/W deck that could be burying a Goblins deck, **but the worse it seemed for the
Goblins, the worse the resulting Patriarch's Bidding was going to be.** *"You gotta keep those bastards out
of Phase III."*

## The three disruptive strategies that work

> **There tend to be three sweeping schools of disruptive interactive strategies that are any good.**

**1. Land destruction (accelerator into turn-two Stone Rain).**

> **A double whammy.** The active deck **moves directly into deep Phase II play from the second and third
> turns while firebombing the opponent below Phase I.** A follow-up of Ohran Viper or Giant Solifuge is often
> just it. **The control is hopeless to reach Phase II competition whereas the active deck might get all the
> way to Phase III Demonfire.**

**2. Silver bullet plus threat of force (Napster / the Gold deck).**

> **Gaddock Teeg keeps Dread Return, Balancing Act, or Enduring Ideal from resolving.** The active deck
> **rules Phase II with 5/5s while continually hassling with Duress, Unmask, Gerrard's Verdict, manipulation,
> and a little card drawing.**

> **The real bullet is the fact that the opponent can't get to a Phase of the game that matters.**

**3. Aggression and disruption (Trix).**

> **Probably the Suicide Black strategy taken to its furthest possible extreme, Trix was the best deck
> because it could spend Phase II shelling the opponent into the fetal position and then win in a 1-2 turn
> window.**

> **Full-on Trix was the complete and utter master of Phase-based Magic.** It advanced its own Phase
> prematurely with false land drops via Mana Vault, Dark Ritual, and Mox Diamond; **it fought other decks at
> any Phase with Duress, Unmask, and Force of Will; and its life gain kept beatdown decks hopelessly out of
> their planned end games.**

**And the failure case, for contrast:** Suicide Black against Sligh. **"Can Black firebomb Red into the
stone age of Phase I? Duress ends up being terrible because Red is aces when both decks are in Phase II.
Black historically gets its ass kicked."**

**Plus the Zvi anecdote paid off:** when Zvi told a fellow Slide player he didn't have the cards to beat
Beasts-Bidding, **the sideboard option he meant was Stoic Champion — which didn't suppress Beasts-Bidding's
Phase III, but sought to end the game before it got there.**

## Phase-specific takeaways

**Phase I — Defensive Deck Speed.**

> **I try to make control decks faster, early, so they can get to the difficult work of defining the field
> of battle against beatdown in Phase II rather than being overrun in the early parts of Phase II, before
> they've even gotten there.** Example: **I put Volcanic Hammer in Pat Chapin's Korlash sideboard.**

**Phase II — Relentless Card Advantage.**

> **A special case of Phase II Magic that acts like Phase III Magic in some matchups.** A resolved **Jushi
> Apprentice** in the mirror **isn't killing you** — but **your ability to define much of anything erodes as
> it continues to draw cards.**

He cites the **Mannequin deck** as the archetype: never really reaching Phase III on threat quality, but
**outnumbering the opponent in Phase II to an oppressive degree.**

**Phase III — on "card power."**

> **I used to roll my eyes when someone detracted a deck based on "card power." Card power is such a
> meaningless thing to talk about.**

> Decks like The Rock can be right **because they are powerful enough while keeping the opponent out of
> Phase III** with a Spike Feeder and a Duress. **Decks like the Gold deck are not themselves broken — some
> would say under-powered — but they spit all over broken decks with their four mana spells.**

**And his metagame claim:**

> **I believe that there is a best deck to play for every tournament, and that it is almost never the deck
> that most people, even people "in the know," think is the best to play.**

**Flores's closing self-assessment**, which is the useful part:

> Most of my Green Extended decks have something in common: **even when they have solid Phase III
> suppression, basically none of them have real Phase III power.**

> By contrast, when I was one of the more successful Standard deck designers, **my decks had both rich
> Defensive Deck Speed and legitimate Phase III play.**

> **There is still a balance to be had, but this last part is homework for me.**

## Notes for use

- **The reframe is the value: "which phase lost it?"** A loss in Phase I is a mana-base or curve problem. A
  loss in Phase II is a play problem. A loss in Phase III is a deck-construction problem — you had no trump
  and no way to deny theirs. Those three diagnoses call for completely different fixes, and most post-game
  analysis conflates them.
- **"Interaction is Phase suppression, not turn suppression"** is the article's central original claim and
  the one that generalizes best. You are always doing one of two things: pushing them down a phase, or
  rocketing yourself up one.
- **The claim about skill is worth sitting with:** *"the scale goes down, not up."* There are no superhuman
  plays, only correct ones, so out-playing someone is making fewer errors. That reframes improvement as
  error-elimination rather than brilliance-acquisition — the same conclusion Reid Duke reaches by a
  different route in `block-1-the-spine/45-becoming-a-better-player-2015-09-28.md`.
- **Phase-specific card evaluation is the most immediately usable output.** Discard is good in I and II and
  dead in III. Force Spike is live in I and a dead topdeck by turn 15. Life gain matters only in thin-margin
  Phase II or to deny Phase III. **Ask of every reactive card: which phases is this live in?**
- **"Discard and aggression, not discard" is the specific correction most players still need.** Stripping a
  card without a clock only delays; it doesn't stop a topdeck. Attrition alone loses to a trump.
- **The Minimum Game Threshold is the concept that connects this to Block 3.** Two mana for beatdown, four
  for control, is precisely what Karsten's tables compute the probability of reaching — see
  `block-3-manabase-reference/02-how-many-lands-do-you-need-2017-05-30.md`, whose entire first table is
  "P(n lands by turn n)."
- **Relationship to the rest of Block 2.** Phase III is a generalization of **inevitability** — Zvi's
  question of who wins a long game — but stated as an active condition (dictating the board) rather than a
  passive one (surviving). Phase I maps to Zvi's **fundamental turn** from the defending side. And Flores
  explicitly revises his own **Philosophy of Fire** position on life gain here, which is why this belongs
  after Block 2 rather than inside it.
- **Reading caution:** the card and format references are 2007 Extended and Standard, and dense. The
  framework survives entirely; the examples require period knowledge. The Bob Maher Belcher section and the
  Masques Mageta story are the two that carry the argument without needing it.
