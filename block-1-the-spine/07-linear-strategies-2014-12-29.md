# Linear Strategies

**Reid Duke — Level One, December 29, 2014**

---

## What a linear strategy is

Think about Magic and you probably picture attacking, blocking, creatures, removal spells, and the
other hallmarks of a close, back-and-forth game. **This installment is about decks that aren't
interested in playing close, back-and-forth games.**

> **To employ a linear strategy means you're entirely focused on one goal or theme. Every card
> contributes to that goal, and you have little interest in deviating. Worrying about what your
> opponent is doing is largely a distraction.**

**Linear strategies follow a straight line from point A to point B.**

### Duke's own example

In his more casual days he saw **Elvish Champion** and thought he liked the card and liked Elves.
Nothing came of it until **Imperious Perfect** piqued his interest. When **Elvish Archdruid** and
**Joraga Warcaller** were printed, he knew what he had to do.

**The tipping point is the important part.** Not all green creature decks are linear, and you could
build a deck with Imperious Perfect and a lot of Elves without being linear. But he added so many
cards that rewarded playing Elves that **he no longer wanted any card in his deck that wasn't an
Elf.**

> I didn't want to draw my Giant Growths anymore, I didn't want to Doom Blade my opponents'
> creatures, I just wanted to draw more and more and more Elves.

He cut the miscellaneous cards and played all lands and Elves. **That's a linear strategy.**

## The strength

**Linear strategies have the potential to be extremely explosive and powerful. Their goal is not to
play a fair game of Magic**, and they frequently succeed at that — running over opponents whose decks
are slower, less powerful, and certainly less focused.

**The comparison that makes it click.** In that era, one of the format's best creatures was **Kitchen
Finks**. One on one, Kitchen Finks greatly outclasses Elvish Champion. **But how do four Kitchen Finks
match up against four Elvish Champions?** And what if you then spend five mana to add Joraga Warcaller
to the party?

> **Linear strategies are often designed to spiral out of control. If my linear deck is allowed to
> "do its thing," and your normal deck is allowed to "do its thing," the linear deck will come out on
> top, often in impressive fashion.**

### Combo decks as the purest case

**Lee Shi Tian's Jeskai Ascendancy combo, Pro Tour Khans of Tarkir.** Duke points out what the
decklist lacks: combat-ready creatures, removal spells. **Basically a lack of anything that does
anything — other than contribute to the combo.**

**The combo, as Duke recaps it:** assemble **Jeskai Ascendancy**, **Retraction Helix**, and
**Briber's Purse**. Cast Retraction Helix on a creature, say **Rattleclaw Mystic**. Tap it to return
Briber's Purse to hand. Cast Briber's Purse for zero mana. Untap the Mystic with Jeskai Ascendancy.
Repeat until the Rattleclaw Mystic has +1,000/+1,000, then attack for the win.

**Why he needed no removal or defense:** he was simply faster and more powerful than his opponents.
**He'd win on turn four, and in many cases there was nothing the opponent could do about it.**

## The weaknesses

### 1. Individual card quality suffers

**When everything must contribute to the theme, the quality of individual cards suffers.** Duke named
Kitchen Finks one of the best creatures in the format, and his own deck's single-mindedness forced
him to pass on it. **When things went well he never missed it. When things went poorly he sometimes
regretted it.**

### 2. Critical mass, and being denied it

> One on one, Kitchen Finks beats Elvish Champion. Four on four, Elvish Champions beat Kitchen Finks.

**So the normal deck's goal is to keep things as close to a one-on-one fight as possible. The linear
deck's goal is to expand things out so its cards are at their most powerful. This is called achieving
critical mass.**

**One way to combat a linear strategy is to keep it from achieving critical mass.** Duke liked playing
against Kitchen Finks. What he didn't like: **Lightning Bolt. Shock. Doom Blade.**

When the opponent killed enough Elves to keep him at one or two creatures, **his Elves were simply
weaker than whatever the normal deck was playing. Without critical mass, the deck didn't function.**

### 3. No ability to interact

**Linear strategies give up flexibility in exchange for power**, and two further weaknesses follow.

**First: very little ability to interact with the opponent.** They're unprepared to shut down the
opponent's plan, banking instead on their own being more powerful.

**The illustration:** Elves versus Jeskai Ascendancy combo. No way to kill a creature, destroy an
enchantment, or make the opponent discard — because **every non-Elf card that could have been added
would have weakened the strategy.** Now those decisions come back to haunt him against a faster deck.

> All I can do is play out some Elves and wait until my opponent kills me on turn four. I hardly get
> to play a game of Magic at all.

### 4. Easy to attack from the sideboard

Duke quotes his own earlier sideboarding installment: **"the more extreme a strategy is, the easier
it will be to sideboard against."**

> **Linear strategies are as extreme as it gets.**

**The problem compounds:** the strategy is extreme and exploitable, so the opponent easily boards in
cards that devastate it — **and the lack of flexibility makes counter-measures or damage control
hard.**

## The verdict

> **It's all a game of weighing pros and cons.** Linear strategies can be attackable and can lack
> flexibility. **However, often their raw power is worth making these sacrifices. If you're a very
> big favorite before sideboarding, maybe you're willing to accept being a small underdog after
> sideboarding.**

## The second case study: Tom Ross's W/U Heroic

**W/U Heroic seeks to quickly and efficiently create a single, giant creature**, typically unblockable
and sometimes with lifelink. **The whole deck is designed to support and protect that one threat.**

**As is typical of linear strategies, there are no removal spells** — except one **Singing Bell
Strike** that **Heliod's Pilgrim** can search up in a pinch.

> **To play with more removal than that would be a mistake, because every card that does not
> contribute to the primary game plan will only serve to weaken the deck. You're going from point A
> to point B, and you cannot afford to take detours.**

## The preview: Valorous Stance, and why versatility breaks the rule

The article doubles as a **Fate Reforged** preview, and Duke uses the card to make a real theoretical
point.

**The problem it solves.** Much of W/U Heroic must be dedicated to protecting its creature, since a
single removal spell shuts the strategy down completely. **Valorous Stance protects a creature in a
direct, reliable, efficient way** — it contributes directly to the plan, doing a job that needs doing.

**And it smuggles flexibility in.** W/U Heroic can't afford **Suspension Field**, **Reprisal**, or any
dedicated removal, because they don't advance the linear plan. **That doesn't mean it never comes up
that you wish you had a removal spell.**

Valorous Stance kills **Courser of Kruphix**, **Siege Rhino**, **Butcher of the Horde**, **Savage
Knuckleblade**, and a dozen other Standard hallmarks. For Heroic that means getting through an
annoying blocker, swinging a race, or buying time.

**The key theoretical claim:**

> It's all possible because of the versatility of a card that can play two different roles. **A
> removal spell alone would not make the cut**, and **Feat of Resistance** might be preferable to the
> indestructible effect. **However, putting both abilities together makes for a card that will be
> tremendously valuable in a large number of games.**

**Other applications Duke notes:**

- Not restricted to Heroic; good in any white-based creature deck.
- **Saving your best creature from End Hostilities.**
- Combat: letting your **Mantis Rider** beat their Mantis Rider.

**And the metagame consequence:** even if you don't play it, you have to know it's out there. **Think
twice before making your Rakshasa Deathdealer or Seeker of the Way into a 4/4. Consider using that
Hero's Downfall on your own turn, before your opponent untaps their white mana.**

## Notes for use

- **The core tradeoff to carry forward:** linear decks trade flexibility for raw power, and buy a
  pre-board advantage at the cost of post-board vulnerability. Duke's framing — big favorite in game
  one, small underdog after sideboarding — is a genuinely good way to evaluate whether the trade is
  worth it.
- **"Critical mass" is the term to keep.** It reframes the matchup as a fight over the *number* of
  relevant permanents rather than their individual quality, which is exactly why cheap removal, not
  bigger creatures, is the answer to a linear deck.
- **The Valorous Stance argument is the most transferable idea here** and generalizes well past
  Magic: a card that does two jobs can enter a deck where neither job alone would justify a slot.
  That's the mechanism by which focused strategies buy back flexibility without taking detours.
- **Cross-reference to Block 2.** A linear deck has, in Flores's terms, an *undermined role* problem
  waiting to happen — it can only be the beatdown, so an opponent who blanks that role leaves it with
  no position at all. See principle 7 in
  `block-2-core-theory-paired/02-eight-core-principles-of-whos-the-beatdown-2012-03-16.md`.
- **Also compare Zvi's fundamental turn** (Block 2 file 04). Lee Shi Tian's deck wins on turn four,
  which is precisely the number that determines whether a linear deck is viable in a given room.
- **Reading caution:** this installment is dated by design, since half of it is a card preview for a
  set from 2015. The theory in the first two thirds is durable; the Standard metagame commentary is
  not.
