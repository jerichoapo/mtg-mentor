# Role Assignment

**Reid Duke — Level One, January 5, 2015**

---

## The trap

**Being adaptable is key.** Knowing how to defend yourself is useful, as is knowing how to be
aggressive and close a game. **But you can't make full use of your skills if you don't know when to
employ each one.**

> **Once you know the basics, you can decide whether to play offense or defense. The problem is that
> many players make this decision far too early.**

**It's a natural trap**, because from the moment you lay out cards to build a deck, you do so with a
plan of being either aggressive or controlling.

> **It's wise to construct your deck with a game plan in mind. That said, it's essential to be able to
> alter and reevaluate that game plan.** Going into a particular matchup you might realize your normal
> plan won't work. Other times you'll have to make the same evaluation **as the texture of the game
> begins to change.**

## The question

Duke credits it directly, and notes the lineage:

> **In 1999, Mike Flores, my predecessor as Level One columnist, posed perhaps the most poignant
> question in all of Magic theory: "Who's the Beatdown?"**

**In other words: which player ought to be attacking and which ought to be defending?**

> **Answering correctly won't guarantee victory. However, answering wrong — or worse, failing to ask at
> all — will most certainly lead to a quick and convincing loss.**

**The two prohibitions:**

- **Do not try to race an opponent who can output damage faster than you.**
- **Do not try to outlast an opponent with a stronger late game.**

> **Identify whether you ought to be forcing the action or trying to extend the game, and act
> accordingly.**

### The two things it's tied to

**1. Inevitability.** If one player has it, **the opponent most certainly ought to be the beatdown**,
because otherwise the game steadily moves toward a state where they can no longer win.

**2. Speed.** Which player is capable of winning faster. That player **has an edge in the early game
and virtually forces the opponent into playing defensively.**

> **Identify the point in the game where your advantage is greatest, and center your strategy around
> it.**

## Blocking with Stormbreath Dragons

**The case study: Jeremy Frye's Temur deck, Top 8 at Grand Prix San Antonio.**

**What the deck does.** Uses early turns to develop mana via **Sylvan Caryatid** or **Rattleclaw
Mystic**, then deploys single creatures capable of unloading lots of damage very quickly.

**What Frye built it to do:** attack with **Stormbreath Dragon** on turn four, knock away life points in
huge chunks, and win many games around turn six. **And that is what the deck does — most of the time.**

**The problem:** some Standard decks can win before turn six. **Plan A works like a charm against
slower decks**, and cards like **Stubborn Denial** and **Temur Charm** shut down whatever defensive
measures the opponent hoped to use. **Against faster decks the strategy must be reevaluated.**

**Duke's description of how the error actually happens is the most valuable part:**

> Round after round you get used to making the same plays. **Turn-two Sylvan Caryatid, turn-three Savage
> Knuckleblade and attack with haste, turn-four Stormbreath Dragon and attack with both.** The first
> five times it works exactly as planned, **but in that sixth match you might never untap for your
> fifth turn. Why not? Because your opponent dealt you 20 damage and you lost.**

**Decks like Mono-Red and W/U Heroic are simply faster than Temur, and racing them is a losing
battle.**

> **What you have to do instead is go off-plan and do something your deck wasn't built for, which is to
> defend yourself. You have to be willing to cast your Stormbreath Dragon and not attack, as strange as
> that may feel.**

**Duke's realistic concession:** Temur isn't great at defense, **but having even a small chance is much
better than dying without a fight.**

> **Try to identify quickly when a plan is not going to work and look around for alternatives, even if
> they aren't particularly appealing.**

### The sideboard as a role-switching tool

Frye's sideboard had **Anger of the Gods** and **Magma Spray**.

> **These are not merely "good cards" against Mono-Red; they contribute to a complete reversal of
> strategy, changing you into a defensive deck designed to handle a fast creature onslaught.**

**The resulting game plan:** use a single big blocker to hold the ground, **and as soon as the opponent
develops enough to attack past it, Anger of the Gods cleans up the mess.**

## Mirror matches and Limited

**Often the question is easy** — one deck is clearly faster. **It gets complicated when the decks look
similar, and when role assignment is fluid as the game progresses.**

**The most common case is Limited**, where both decks likely have offense and defense, creatures on a
balanced curve, removal and support spells.

> **In such cases you decide your role not only on the matchup between the decks, but also on what
> cards are drawn and how the board develops. You may have to adjust as the game progresses.**

**Also:** decks of comparable speed forced to play each other. **Mono-Red and W/U Heroic are both fast
racing decks — at what point does one player rein in the aggression and start leaving back blockers?**

**And the cleanest case, the mirror match.** Both players on the same archetype. **Mono-Red players
aren't used to blocking or voluntarily trading creatures, but in a mirror they might be forced to.**

### The three factors in borderline situations

**Factor 1: who goes first.** Duke quotes his own earlier installment:

> If the decks are of comparable speed, the player who goes first has the early-stage advantage.
> **Mirroring your opponent's plays when you're on the draw is typically a losing battle.** You start
> behind and remain behind. **To "break serve," the player on the draw has to trade resources, slow the
> game down, and try to reach the late stage on even footing.**

**Concretely:** Johnny's **Foundry Street Denizen** on the play will usually unload damage faster than
Jenny's on the draw. **Jenny should identify herself as needing to play defense and trade creatures
right away instead of trying to race.**

**Factor 2: what cards each player draws.** Whose opening hand is better? Who missed a land drop? Who
drew creatures and who drew removal?

> **Don't be afraid to press an early advantage.** Sometimes your opponent's draft deck is faster and
> more aggressive than yours, **but that doesn't eliminate the chance of you having the faster draw.**
> With a one-drop, two-drop, and three-drop against an opponent who plays nothing early, **it might be
> best to take the aggressor role and keep them on the back foot.**

**And the reversal.** Once they start playing creatures it might be right to switch back to defense.
Three reasons it might be:

- **Their deck can still beat you in a race despite the slow start.**
- **You have a bomb in hand that wins if you survive to cast it.**
- **Minor differences between the decks mean you have inevitability, or at least a noticeable long-game
  advantage.**

**Factor 3: a perceived advantage in the late game.** In an exact mirror this might not exist. **But if
you sideboarded in three Chandra, Pyromaster for the Mono-Red mirror and feel she'll pull you ahead
late, you're slightly more inclined to play defense.**

**This comes up more in Limited**, where a bomb rare makes you want to drag the game out. **And it works
in reverse:** if you lost game one to their **Duneblast**, you might decide to close out games two and
three before they draw it, **in which case you have to play the beatdown.**

### The summary on fluidity

> **The way your two decks match up matters a lot, but once in a while it can be outweighed by the
> cards drawn in a particular game.** Your deck might be aggressive, but if your opponent has a fast
> start you simply have to play defense.

> **You never know what might happen in a game of Magic, and the longer you keep yourself alive, the
> more unexpected opportunities for victory might arise.**

## Building your deck to be flexible

**The major strength of midrange decks is flexibility** — a control game against fast decks, the
aggressor role against slow ones. That comes from a balanced game plan and a good sideboard, **but a
lot of it comes down to individual card choices.**

**What to look for:**

- **Creatures that gain life or excel at blocking** are great on defense. **If they also pack a punch
  when the opponent fails to mount a defense, they're perfect for midrange.**
- **Versatile answer cards** — remove an attacker, or create an opening for a threat of your own.
- **Removal spells generally add flexibility.** Control over the board means being able to slow their
  offense **or take out a blocker to keep attacking.**

**The problem with removal, and the fix:**

> Dedicated control decks might not have many targets. **This is why it's particularly valuable to
> choose burn spells as your removal. When there's no creature you need to answer, you can simply point
> them at your opponent.**

> **Burn spells are great for aggressive and midrange decks alike, and they're at their best when you
> master role assignment.** They can stop an attacker, take out a blocker, or go straight at the life
> total. **When you know which choice to make, burn spells help you win the maximum possible number of
> games.**

## The preview: Wild Slash (Fate Reforged)

**One-mana burn spells are as old as the game**, and a new one is always exciting because they're
almost invariably important for both Constructed and Limited.

**The benchmark question:** where does it fall relative to the tried-and-true **Shock**? **Shock tends
to be a staple, and Duke has played burn spells far worse.**

> **Wild Slash is noticeably better. It always has at least the same effect as Shock, with the
> potential for much more.**

### The damage-prevention clause

Duke works through three applications:

**1. Against Kiora, the Crashing Wave.** Your **Stormbreath Dragon** has been targeted by Kiora's +1 and
is slated to have its damage prevented. **Wild Slash the opponent or one of their creatures, and
because the Dragon gives you ferocious, you can now successfully attack and kill Kiora.**

**2. Against protection.** Protection reduces damage from a source to 0, **which is another way of
saying it prevents the damage.** So while **Wild Slash cannot target a creature with protection from
red**, its ability **can allow a Slagstorm to kill it, or a red creature to deal damage in combat.**

> Are you sure you want to block my Ember Swallower with your Master of Waves? **I'll Wild Slash you
> for 2 damage, and I'll kill your Master and all of your Elemental tokens.**

He notes this also applies against **God's Willing** and **Feat of Resistance** out of W/U Heroic.

**3. Against Turbo-Fog.** Decks that cast **Fog** or **Defend the Hearth** every turn, either by drawing
lots of extra cards or recurring them from the graveyard. **The best way to foil the strategy is to
stop damage prevention, and Wild Slash is an easy way to do it.**

**Duke's forecast:** a big card in Standard and Limited simply by virtue of its similarity to Shock.

> **It's a good, efficient card that provides flexibility to decks that might not know ahead of time
> what role they'll need to take in a game of Magic.**

## Notes for use

- **This is the installment to read directly against Block 2.** Duke names Flores and the question
  explicitly, and identifies himself as Flores's successor in the Level One column. Read
  `block-2-core-theory-paired/01-whos-the-beatdown-1999-04-13.md` first, then this.
- **What Duke adds to Flores.** Flores's three questions are about *deck composition*. Duke adds two
  more inputs that Flores's original underweights: **who is on the play**, and **what was actually
  drawn this game**. That makes role assignment fluid within a single game rather than fixed at
  deck-registration time. Flores himself moved toward this in 2012 — see principle 1, "positions are
  fluid," in `block-2-core-theory-paired/02-eight-core-principles-of-whos-the-beatdown-2012-03-16.md`.
- **The Temur autopsy is the best description of *how* misassignment happens** anywhere in this
  library. It isn't ignorance, it's habit: five rounds of the same correct sequence trains the sixth,
  wrong one. That's a practice problem, not a knowledge problem.
- **"Cast your Stormbreath Dragon and not attack"** is the single most useful image here. The physical
  reluctance to make that play is the thing to notice in yourself.
- **The sideboard point is important and underrated:** good sideboard cards don't merely improve a
  matchup, they let you *change roles*. Anger of the Gods converts Temur into a defensive deck. That's
  Flores's principle 8 (sideboarding to acquire the other role) arrived at independently.
- **Burn as flexible removal** is the concrete deckbuilding output: a card that's good in both roles
  costs you nothing when you guess the role wrong.
