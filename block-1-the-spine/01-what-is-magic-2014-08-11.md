# What is Magic?

**Reid Duke — Level One, August 11, 2014**

---

## The question

> **"So what is Magic?" They ask me.**

**Duke's "they":** parents, grandparents, aunts, uncles, nieces, nephews, girlfriends, boyfriends,
coworkers, strangers, everyone.

**Why it's a universal question:**

> **Magic: The Gathering is so much different from, quite frankly, everything else.** They know chess,
> but Magic isn't quite chess. They know playing-card games like bridge, **but again, Magic isn't quite
> one of those either.** Even for gamers, the small details about Magic — **and more importantly, their
> consequences** — are difficult to grasp.

## The claim, and the audience

> **Magic is the greatest game on Earth.**

**Why he's comfortable making the claim:** because Magic **can suit itself to the needs of so many
different people.** It's for the collector, the deckbuilder, the lover of the lore or art, and of course
the player. **Just as well-suited to players like himself, who play every day, as to friends who play
once every year or two.**

**His introduction of himself:** over the last nineteen years, since he was five years old, he's been
all of those people — player, deckbuilder, collector, lover of the Magic world. **Today a full-time
professional Magic player and writer.**

**The scope of the column, stated up front:**

> This column won't feature much about Magic storyline, art, or collecting. **Nonetheless, my writing
> will be for absolutely anyone, at any level of expertise, who might one day aspire to play competitive
> Magic.**

> **The goal is to take you from the most basic concepts of Magic all the way up to playing at a
> competitive, Pro Tour Qualifier (PTQ) level.**

## What makes Magic, Magic

**Duke's cocktail-party one-liner:**

> **"You can think of Magic as a cross between chess and playing-card games."**

**The similarities to chess.** Both are one-on-one strategy games. At the simplest level, **your
creatures are your chess pieces and your spells are one extra layer of support for them.** Both require
**strategic thinking, long-term planning, and an eye for advantageous openings.**

**The key difference:**

> **In chess, the whole game is laid out clearly on the table. In Magic there is uncertainty.**

### Variance

> **Far and away the most prominent variable is simply that you can't know what card will be coming off
> the top of your library — or your opponent's — next.**

**Chess is about thinking several moves ahead. The same is true of Magic, but with the extra challenge
of considering possibilities:** What if you draw a land next turn? What if it's a creature? What if the
opponent draws a way to kill the creature you left back as a blocker?

**The consequence Duke draws out is the interesting one — you cannot memorize openings:**

> Every game will be different, **even between the same two players using the same two decks.**

> Let's say in chess I always move my knight first. **Well, in Magic I might not even draw my "knight"
> at all.** What's more, my decision to move my knight first is based on how it matches up against my
> opponent's pieces. **In Magic, you also don't know for sure what "pieces" your opponent will have.**

### Hidden information

**Beyond variance, you don't know what cards your opponent holds or has in their library.**

> Often you'll face situations where **one choice is better if your opponent has a removal spell, and
> another is better if they do not.** You might have a powerful spell to cast, **but you'd rather wait
> if your opponent has Dissipate.**

> **Cases like these can feel largely like a guessing game, but there are, in fact, a number of
> techniques you can employ to help you work toward making the best possible decision.**

## What skills do you need?

### The "playing-card game" skills, and why they're overrated

**Duke's myth-busting is the most useful passage in the installment.**

> In the movies, **Wild Bill Hickok might see the tiniest twitch of another cowboy's eyelash and
> immediately know, with mortal certainty, that he's holding exactly the Ace and Eight of Spades.
> Moments like this, at least to my knowledge, are the things of fiction.**

> While a variety of factors can combine to give you an educated guess about your opponent's hand,
> **you do not need any mystical powers to be great at Magic or at other games of hidden information.**

**Both glamorous skills get demoted:**

> **In my experience, it's rare for two Magic players to engage in a deadly staredown in the wake of a
> game-changing bluff.** Similarly, **the times when you want to pull out your calculator and pinpoint
> your odds of drawing a certain card are also few and far between.**

> If you're a wizard at reading opponents, great. If you're a lightning-fast mathematician, bring it.
> **These things will come up from time to time, and they may accelerate your growth as a player.
> However, for my money, they aren't the most important skills in Magic.**

**What handling uncertainty actually is:**

> **Less of an exact science and more a matter of simply being aware that these possibilities exist.**
> Some games you won't draw as many lands as you like. Other games you'll draw far more than you can
> use. Sometimes your opponent will have Flesh to Dust for your powerhouse creature and other times
> they won't.

> **These uncertainties are in the background of every game, and that's the point. All you can do is be
> aware that not everything is under your control.**

### The "chess" skills, and the priority order

> **The first and foremost skill in Magic's gameplay is simply the ability to play your own cards
> well.**

> To truly master the game it can be helpful to learn to read opponents and to brush up on
> probabilities. **However, these things should come after — long after — learning how to best deploy
> the resources your deck gives you.**

> **Focus most on the "chess" aspects (the board, the information you have), and simply be aware of the
> "playing-card game" aspects (variance and hidden information) that underlie everything.**

## Focus: the number one skill

**Duke ends the installment by naming the single most important skill, and it isn't a Magic skill at
all.**

**Other names for it:** thoughtfulness, or presence in the game.

> **This is not a natural talent that some people are born with and some are not.** Every single person
> reading this can force themselves, the next time they play, **to slow down a little; to minimize
> distractions; to keep thinking about the game during the opponent's turn instead of letting their
> mind wander.**

**What focus buys you — four things:**

1. **Lowers your chances of making silly mistakes.**
2. **Raises your chances of seeing winning plays.**
3. **Provides more hints about what cards your opponent might be holding.**
4. **Perhaps most importantly, accelerates your learning**, because you'll understand what's going on
   and **be better able to pinpoint where things are going right or going wrong.**

## Duke's summary list of cultivable skills

### "Chess skills"

- **General strategic thinking** — How can you win? How can you stop your opponent from winning?
- **Looking for opportunities** — Is your opponent vulnerable to an attack? Is this a good time to get
  in damage?
- **Patience** — Is this *not* the time to attack? **Sometimes in Magic, waiting is best. Don't feel
  like you need to make a big play just for the sake of making a big play.**
- **Thinking a move or two ahead** — Is the game going to come down to a damage race? **If so, how can
  you make sure you win that race?**

### "Playing-card game skills"

- **Factoring in variance** — **You don't need to be a math wiz; if you can count, you can be good at
  Magic.** Just remember you don't have total control of what comes off the top, and make the most of
  any situation.
- **Considering hidden information** — **You don't have to be a mind reader either. You're not meant to
  know every card in your opponent's hand at all times.** But sometimes you get a hint. **What are the
  likely possibilities? What is your best play for each?**
- **Focus** — **Be present in the game, be aware of everything on the board, take it slow, and don't
  jump in without thinking.**

**His sign-off:** take it a little slower, think things through a little more, and remember to focus.

## Notes for use

- **This is the mission statement for the whole course.** Duke names the destination explicitly —
  from the basics to PTQ-competitive play — which is worth keeping in mind as you read the other 45
  installments.
- **The demotion of bluffing and math is the most important thing here, and it's contrarian.** New
  players assume the skill ceiling is in reading opponents and computing odds. Duke says both are real
  but marginal, and that playing your own cards well is where nearly all the available improvement
  lives. Installment 04 makes the same point about bluffing ("people bluff less often than you'd
  expect").
- **"Focus is not a natural talent" is the claim to take seriously.** It's the only skill in the course
  that costs nothing to acquire and improves everything else, including the rate at which you learn the
  rest. Note that its fourth benefit is meta: focus makes your practice more valuable, which compounds.
- **The chess/cards split is the organizing metaphor for the entire column.** Perfect information and
  planning on one side, variance and hidden information on the other. Nearly every later installment
  sits on one side or the other — sequencing and combat are chess, mulligans and permission are cards.
- **Cross-reference:** the patience item foreshadows installment 16 (Inevitability); the damage-race
  item foreshadows installment 18; "considering hidden information" is developed into technique in
  installment 20 (Sequencing) and installment 12 (Permission Spells). Duke's closing installment,
  45 (Becoming a Better Player), returns to these themes.
