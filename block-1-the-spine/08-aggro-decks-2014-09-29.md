# Aggro Decks

**Reid Duke — Level One, September 29, 2014**

> The best defense is a good offense.

---

## Where this sits in the course

This is the point where Level One starts assembling the fundamentals into concrete applications, which
means looking at the complete picture: not only gameplay but **deckbuilding**.

Deckbuilding matters in Limited too, but **the greater challenges and rewards come from Constructed**.
There the possibilities are virtually endless, since you can build from any legal card you can get
your hands on. **The challenge becomes honing in on a particular strategy and making sure every card
contributes to it.**

**The simplest and most common strategy is to exploit tempo.** Build a fast deck geared toward playing
and attacking with creatures quickly, put opponents on the back foot immediately, and win lots of
games with minimal resistance. **These are aggro decks.**

## The importance of tempo in aggro decks

**Your goal is to get ahead early and stay ahead** until you either win or create an advantage large
enough to leverage into a win. **Tempo is your most important resource and ought to overshadow other
concerns.**

Duke then reframes each earlier concept through the aggro lens:

**Card advantage.** A bigger concern for players aiming at a long game. **With aggro you want to
decide the game with your opening seven plus your first small handful of draw steps.** You wouldn't
turn down free extra cards, **but you cannot afford to sacrifice tempo to get them.**

**Mana curve.** Carefully built and **as low to the ground as possible.** If you can't start putting
creatures into play in the first couple of turns, **you risk losing the window where you could have
built your biggest advantage.**

**Mana efficiency.** For every job that needs doing, choose the cheapest option available.

> **Ideally, you should spend less mana to deploy threats than your opponent spends to answer them.
> You should spend less mana answering threats than your opponent spends to deploy them.**

**Don't invest tempo in cards like Sandsteppe Citadel and Abzan Banner**, since hopefully the games
won't run long enough for such cards to repay their initial investment.

**Threats over answers.** Focus on your own threats rather than answering whatever the opponent might
be doing. **The small number of answers you play should be geared to counter the cards that shut down
your own game plan**, like blocking creatures.

**Life total.** Be concerned with attacking your opponent's, not protecting your own.

> **In short, you should thrive in the early turns instead of planning for a long game.**

## Example 1: W/B Aggro

### The curve

**A full 22 spells cost one mana.** There were five white and black one-mana creatures with 2 power
legal in Standard at the time; **this deck plays four of them, four copies each.** That means the
highest possible chance of a good aggressive turn-one play, and a high chance of casting two
additional one-mana spells on turn two. **Deploying threats as quickly as possible is central to a
tempo strategy.**

**No spell costs more than three mana.** All your weapons are available in the early turns, and it
allows a **relatively low land count of only 21**, making more room for threats.

### Herald of Torment, and why flexibility matters

**Herald of Torment is a perfect aggro card.** Three mana, so it has a great chance of coming down
early and applying pressure. **And if you draw too many lands or the game runs longer than you'd
like, bestow provides a backup plan.**

**The tempo point Duke draws out:** bestow can create a big swing by letting an existing creature fly
over a blocker **right away**. Most creatures require an initial tempo investment because they can't
tap or attack the turn they arrive — what used to be called summoning sickness. **Bestowing Herald has
an immediate, and large, impact on the board.** The flexibility of having both options is what makes
the card appealing.

### Getting over the life-loss phobia

A number of cards here cost you life: **Herald of Torment**, **Pain Seer**, **Thoughtseize**,
**Ulcerate**. Duke's observation from experience: **new players tend to instinctively avoid cards like
these like the plague, and all players need to get over that phobia.**

**Why aggro can afford it:**

- **In the early game you'll be so far ahead on tempo that your opponent can't attack you back.**
- **If things go awry and they swing it back, a couple of life points are unlikely to make the
  difference.**

> **Your goal is to win before your opponents get their more expensive and powerful cards online.
> Your goal should never be to beat those cards in a fair fight.**

**The same logic applies to lands.** Slower W/B decks might prefer **Scoured Barrens** to **Caves of
Koilos** and **Mana Confluence**. But an aggro deck **should be happy to sacrifice life total to
preserve tempo. Lands that enter tapped slow you down and throw off your curve**, which is very costly
for an aggro strategy.

### The answer cards

- **Hero's Downfall** — a reliable way to remove virtually any blocker and keep attacking.
- **Ulcerate** and **Murderous Cut** — more situational, requiring the right circumstances, **but they
  make up for it in mana efficiency.** Kill a creature with an Ulcerate or a delved Murderous Cut
  *and* play your own creature the same turn and **you've created a huge tempo swing.**
- **Thoughtseize** — an answer card, but in this deck **you'll most often use it to answer your
  opponent's answer.**

**Duke's framing of Thoughtseize is the sharpest idea in the installment.** The two biggest threats to
an aggro strategy are **blocking creatures and removal spells**. You cannot Hero's Downfall a removal
spell, **but you can Thoughtseize it away before they cast it.** Especially valuable when the opponent
has planned the whole game around a high-impact card like **End Hostilities** and you strip it at the
last moment.

## Attacking your opponent's life total

**The key to Constructed is building with a concerted strategy.** The beauty of this deck is that
**every card contributes to attacking the opponent's life total.**

> **No single card need win the game on its own. If your Tormented Hero deals 4 or 6 damage before
> dying, it's done its job.** The rest of the deck continues working toward getting your opponent from
> 20 to 0.

### Pressure limits their options

Just as the aggro player uses cards that cost life, **opponents naturally want to do the same. But
facing a horde of cheap creatures, your opponent regrets every Thoughtseize they cast and every Mana
Confluence they tap.**

**The Athreos illustration.** With **Athreos, God of Passage**, a slow deck's opponent might gladly pay
3 life to exile your creature permanently. **When you're aggressively attacking the life total, you
take that option away.** You present the choice of giving your creature back or dying immediately.

> **With Athreos, you can leverage an advantage of tempo or life total into card advantage** by
> returning your dying creatures to your hand.

## Reach

**The saying:** no point of damage matters except the last point. Meaning there's no difference
between winning at 20 life and winning at 1.

**There's some wisdom in it, but it's incomplete.** It can be quite valuable to get an opponent low
even if you don't finish them right away — as Athreos demonstrates, decimating a life total limits
their in-game options.

**And there's a psychological component Duke states plainly:**

> When players get low on life, panic inevitably creeps into their gameplay, as it rightly should.
> **The lower their life totals get, the more likely they are to make unfavorable blocks and the less
> likely they are to make bold, risky plays.**

**The definition:**

> **Reach refers to the ability to finish off a wounded opponent even once you've lost your initial
> tempo advantage.**

Aggro decks get ahead early, stay ahead as long as possible, and leverage tempo into a win or an
advantage of another form — **often a life-total advantage.** Reach is what converts that.

**In the W/B deck, reach comes primarily from Herald of Torment and Mogis's Marauder.** Even if the
opponent takes over the ground with bigger creatures, at a low enough life total **it's frighteningly
easy to simply draw a Herald** (a flier) **or a Mogis's Marauder** (intimidate) **and close out the
game.**

## Example 2: Rabble Red

**Traditionally red is the color best suited to aggro.** Beyond lots of cheap, aggressively slanted
creatures, **red's removal comes in the form of burn.**

**Burn, or direct damage**, can kill a creature such as a blocker *and* deal damage straight to the
opponent. **It's one of the simplest and most effective forms of reach.**

> Facing Rabble Red, a player must do everything possible to stay at a high life total. **If they go
> down to 7, the game is in a way no longer in their hands.** No matter how well things are going, no
> matter how far ahead on board they get, **a Stoke the Flames and a Lightning Strike can appear at
> any time to take the game away.**

## Proactive strategies

Duke closes on being proactive.

**The tournament problem:** you're paired against a series of unfamiliar players using unfamiliar
strategies with unfamiliar card combinations. **It's difficult or impossible to be fully prepared for
all of them.**

> **In a tournament, the slower your deck, the more risks you open yourself up to.** When games run
> long, opponents can surprise you with any powerful creature, planeswalker, sorcery, instant, or
> combination imaginable, and you need an answer, or at least a game plan, for beating them all.

**Being proactive** — fast, aggressive, powerful, or all three — **means focusing on executing your own
game plan rather than counteracting theirs.** You're likely an expert at your own plan; **it's much
harder to prepare for every eventuality of a drawn-out game.**

> **It's simple: win every game by turn five and you won't have to worry about any card that costs
> more than five mana. Burn your opponents to death and you won't have to worry about any card that
> doesn't gain them life.**

## Notes for use

- **The efficiency rule is the one to memorize:** spend less mana deploying threats than they spend
  answering them, and less answering than they spend deploying. That single line is the whole aggro
  deckbuilding philosophy.
- **"Answer their answer" is the non-obvious idea.** Discard in an aggro deck isn't disruption for its
  own sake, it's protection for the clock. That reframes Thoughtseize from a control card into a
  tempo card.
- **Reach is defined here more fully than in installment 06**, including the psychological half: a low
  life total makes opponents block badly and play timidly. Worth pairing with **The Fear**, the
  concept Flores credits to Dan Paskins in `block-0-orientation/04-shoulders-of-giants-2013-08-06.md`.
- **This installment is the practical companion to the Philosophy of Fire** (Block 2 file 05). Duke's
  "if your Tormented Hero deals 4 or 6 damage before dying, it's done its job" is exactly Flores's
  argument that an unblocked threat trading down is fine if it moved the opponent toward zero.
- **The proactivity argument doubles as tournament-selection advice** and connects forward to
  installment 41, Choosing Your Deck. The claim is that a proactive deck reduces your exposure to
  unknown opponents, which is a variance argument rather than a power argument.
- **Reading caution:** both decklists are Khans-era Standard and the specific cards are long rotated.
  The curve logic, the life-total reasoning, and the reach framework are what transfer.
