# Threats and Answers

**Reid Duke — Level One, September 8, 2014**

---

## The frame

> **Magic is a game of threats and answers.** Mana, card advantage, and other fundamental concepts are
> in the background of every game, **but threats — like creatures and planeswalkers — are what win
> games.** A well-placed answer card, like a removal spell, **can swing a game in your favor, and the
> lack of one can mean watching things slip away from you.**

**The task Duke sets:** it can be difficult to compare the values of these very different types of
cards, **but the key is to strike a sensible balance.**

## Being proactive

**The definition, repeated from installment 05:** at any moment you're either **proactive** (deploying
threats, attacking, progressing your own plan) **or reactive** (defending, answering their threats).

> **In the Magic world, the word "proactive" has a positive connotation — it's good to be proactive.**

### Dave Price's law

Duke credits **Dave Price**, a pro from the early days of Magic known to this day as **"the King of
Beatdown,"** with the famous saying:

> **"While there are wrong answers, there are no wrong threats."**

**The asymmetry it names, spelled out:**

**When you draw a threat**, one of two things happens:

1. **Your opponent doesn't have the right answer** → your threat sets you on course to win.
2. **Your opponent does have an answer** → a usually neutral exchange.

> **There's little risk when you fill your deck with lots of potent creatures.**

**When you draw an answer**, there are three cases:

1. **Your opponent may not even have a threat** → your answer isn't particularly useful.
2. **Best case: you answer a threat** and undo one of their actions → again, usually neutral.
3. **The disastrous third case: your answer doesn't match up against their threat** — Duke's example is
   **Pillar of Light against a 3/3 creature.**

**The conclusion, carefully hedged:**

> This is not to say answer cards are useless; there are plenty of situations where you need them to
> survive. **However, while filling your deck with answers can buy you time, it will not actually
> contribute to winning the game.**

> **Generally speaking, the player proactively deploying threats has an easier route to victory than the
> player reactively trying to answer them.**

### The tournament argument

> **Another advantage of being proactive is that you're not at the mercy of your opponent's strategy.**

Over a long tournament you face ten different opponents with ten different decks. **You might not know
what all ten are playing, and you certainly cannot tailor your deck to be perfect against all ten
simultaneously.**

> **If you're employing a proactive strategy, focusing on executing your own game plan, you have a
> fighting chance even against a strategy you weren't specifically prepared for.**

## The four kinds of threat

> **Threats are what win games.** You can win easily by catching your opponent unprepared for a
> particular threat. You can win quickly by deploying more threats than they can answer in time.
> **Alternatively, you can take your time and win a longer game with a resilient threat.** No matter
> what form a game takes, **threats play a central role.**

### 1. Quick threats

**The tried-and-true strategy:** deploy a lot of cheap creatures and try to win before your opponent
can answer them all or cast more powerful spells.

**Why it works — the cost asymmetry:**

> While plenty of creatures cost only one or two mana, **the vast majority of answer cards are more
> expensive than that.**

- **End Hostilities costs five mana.** A one-mana creature has a good chance of **attacking four or more
  times** before it can be removed.
- **Common one-for-one removal like Hero's Downfall costs three.** On the play, casting a creature on
  turns one, two, and three means **your opponent has a tough battle ahead** if they want back in using
  expensive answers. **As they battle back, you play more creatures as fast as they're answered, and
  attack all the while.**

**The catch:** if your creatures are all 1/1s and 2/2s, **it takes either a lot of time or a lot of
creatures to kill your opponent from 20.**

### 2. Potent single threats

**More powerful creatures**, such as the monstrosity creatures of Theros block.

> **You give up the option of outpacing your opponent's answers, but in exchange, any single one of your
> threats, if unanswered, will win the game in short order.** High-impact cards like these **have the
> potential to provide virtual card advantage and run away with a game.**

**And this is where Price's law pays off most:**

> By playing potent single threats you put yourself in the best possible position to take advantage of
> the concept. **You put your opponent through maximum punishment if their answer comes at the wrong
> time or in the wrong form.**

### 3. Resilient threats

**Threats that dodge common removal**, maximizing the chance your opponent has a wrong answer.

**Examples:** **Prognostic Sphinx** or any hexproof creature. **The best example from recent times is
Ætherling** — careful to leave blue mana available at all times, **there's virtually nothing that can
permanently remove it from the battlefield.**

> **Resilient threats are especially good choices if your deck only has a small number of threats**,
> since in that case you can't easily afford to have them killed.

### 4. Threats that provide guaranteed value

**Sometimes you get something out of your threat even if your opponent does have the right answer.**

Examples: **enters-the-battlefield triggers** and **haste creatures**. **The best examples are
planeswalkers**, since you can activate them immediately, as soon as you cast them.

**The illustration:** cast **Nissa, Worldwaker** and make your Forest a 4/4 Elemental. **Even if they
destroy Nissa with Hero's Downfall, you're left with a 4/4 permanently.** Either they need an additional
answer for the Elemental — **which essentially means card advantage for you** — or it sticks around and
contributes to your win.

## Answers

**Duke turns the argument around:**

> **Despite the truth and wisdom behind Mr. Price's words, answers are, in fact, also tremendously
> important.** Your opponents are going to have threats as well, and being caught without an answer at
> the wrong time can mean defeat.

> **If your deck is all threats and no answers, you're likely to have a very hard time coming back from
> behind.**

**And a correction to the connotation:**

> **While "proactive" tends to have a positive connotation, "reactive" is not necessarily negative.**
> Some reactive strategies are very effective. **More importantly, situations come up where any player,
> with any deck, will be forced to be reactive in order to have a chance.**

**Potent single threats like Polukranos, World Eater dominate if unanswered**, so it's very valuable to
have **at least a small number of answers** to have a fighting chance.

### Even fast decks need answers

**The key expansion of what counts as a "threat":**

> Even employing a fast strategy trying to win before expensive creatures arrive, **you're still at risk
> of being shut down by a blocking creature or other defensive card.** In this case **your opponent's
> "threats" might not be attacking you directly, but they are neutralizing your game plan and will
> eventually cause you to lose.**

> **Having access to some sort of answer can actually be a valuable part of your proactive game plan.**

**A very proactive, threat-based deck** focused on quick threats, intending to deploy a lot of small
creatures as fast as possible. **With so many small creatures, it's at risk of being shut down by a
blocking creature.**

**Its answers are tailored to that specific problem.** Since the most dangerous threats to the deck are
*blocking creatures*, every answer removes them, and Duke classifies each:

| Card | Type of answer |
|---|---|
| **Stoke the Flames** — burn the blocker, then attack | **a direct answer** |
| **Rubblebelt Maaka** — bloodrush to kill it in combat | **a situational answer** |
| **Firefist Striker** — take away its ability to block | **a temporary answer** |

**The elegant part:**

> **The beauty of Rabble Red's answer cards is that they can never really be "wrong answers." Even in
> the worst-case scenario, you can always cash them in for damage on your opponent.**

**The rule Duke extracts:**

> **So even one of the fastest strategies possible employs its own form of answer cards. Generally
> speaking, the slower your strategy, the more answer cards you're going to need, and the more directly
> they should answer your opponent's threats.**

## The preview: Murderous Cut

**Duke calls it exciting in its simplicity and directness — a clean and reliable answer card.**

**Why it's what you want in an answer:**

> **If you need to answer a creature, Murderous Cut will rarely fail you. It can kill anything from a
> Firedrinker Satyr all the way to a Polukranos, World Eater.** It can take out an attacker threatening
> your life total, **or a blocker trying to shut you down.**

**On delve.** On paper it costs five mana, and **you're unlikely to cast it in the first couple of
turns** before you have cards in the graveyard. **But later, delve lets you cast it on the cheap, giving
you the crucial ability to cast it and another spell in the same turn.**

**Building around it:** a deck with **Satyr Wayfinder** and **Necromancer's Assistant** ensures there are
always plenty of extra cards in the graveyard, and **Murderous Cut will often cost only one mana once
you're ready to cast it.**

**But you don't need to:**

> **Even in an ordinary deck, creatures, lands, and spells will wind up in the graveyard over the course
> of a long game**, and the ability to use delve to make Murderous Cut cheaper **will come up often, and
> be quite valuable.**

## Notes for use

- **Price's law is the takeaway line of the installment**, and one of the most quoted lines in Magic
  strategy: while there are wrong answers, there are no wrong threats. The asymmetry is real and it's
  the reason threat-dense decks are more forgiving to build and to pilot.
- **The four threat types are a genuinely useful taxonomy** — quick, potent, resilient, and
  guaranteed-value — and they aren't interchangeable. Which one you want depends on how many threats
  your deck runs: few threats argues for resilient, many threats argues for quick.
- **The cost asymmetry is the underrated fact.** Creatures cost one or two; answers cost three or five.
  That structural gap is why aggression is the default-favored posture and why control decks need card
  advantage to compensate.
- **The Rabble Red analysis is the best part.** "Answers that can never be wrong answers" is the design
  goal for removal in an aggressive deck: burn doubles as reach, so a dead removal spell is still
  damage. Compare Duke's identical argument for burn in installment 17.
- **The redefinition of "threat" matters:** a blocker is a threat to an aggro deck even though it never
  attacks. Threat means *whatever beats your plan*, not *whatever deals damage*. That reframing is what
  makes the concept portable.
- **Cross-reference:** proactivity is argued at length in installment 08 (Aggro Decks); the
  ten-unknown-opponents argument appears in both. Installment 11 (Board Sweepers) is the case where an
  answer is so strong it becomes the game plan. Flores's Philosophy of Fire, Block 2 file 05, is the
  theoretical basis for "a threat that trades down still did its job."
