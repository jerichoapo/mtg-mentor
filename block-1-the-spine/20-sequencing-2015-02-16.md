# Sequencing

**Reid Duke — Level One, February 16, 2015**

---

## What sequencing is

> **Just as important as what you do is how you do it. Sequencing refers to the order and the manner in
> which you take your actions in a game.**

> **Proper sequencing will make the game as easy as possible for you and as difficult as possible for
> your opponent.**

**Duke pre-empts the objection** that this seems like nitpicking, an aspect unlikely to make a big
difference:

> **Trust me when I say that this is not the case.** You never really know when something that seems
> small might be important to the outcome. **It's best to develop good habits and give yourself the best
> possible chance to take the advantages that come from proper sequencing.**

## The golden rule

Duke introduces this as **the single most valuable piece of concrete advice he can offer another Magic
player.**

> **Think through your entire turn before you do anything.**

**The procedure.** Untap, address any relevant upkeep effects, draw your card, **and stop.**

> **At this point in each and every one of your turns you should take ten or twenty seconds to think
> through everything you intend to do this turn.**

**The checklist:** Will you play a land? Which one? Will you attack? With what? Will you play a spell?
What spell, and what will you do with it?

> **You'll need a list in your head of what you're going to do before you can consider the best way to
> do it.**

### Include your opponent's predictable responses

**Example:** you have a 2/2 and **Awaken the Bear** in hand. Your opponent has an untapped 3/3. **If you
attack, there are two predictable responses: block or don't block.**

- **Will you cast Awaken the Bear if they block?**
- **Will you cast it if they don't?**

> **The answer to these questions will help you decide whether you really want to attack in the first
> place.** The whole thought process should take place at the start of your turn, before you do anything
> rash.

> **There's no reason to find yourself in the middle of a hairy combat phase only to realize you'd have
> really preferred to use your mana to cast a creature instead of Awaken the Bear.**

> **You can avoid unfavorable situations by thinking everything through at once instead of facing your
> decisions one at a time.**

### The four benefits

Duke lists what the habit buys you:

1. **You'll make fewer mistakes.**
2. **You'll have a better grasp of what's going on in the game.**
3. **You'll sequence your plays better.**
4. **You'll give away less information**, because your opponent won't know what you're considering when
   you pause.

**That last one is the subtle one:**

> If you attack and then stop to think when your opponent blocks, **they're going to think, "I wonder if
> my opponent has Awaken the Bear?"** If you think everything through at the start of the turn, **your
> opponent is far less likely to guess what you have.**

He quotes Sun Tzu: let your plans be dark and impenetrable as night, and when you move, fall like a
thunderbolt.

### The caveat on pace

> **Be careful not to play too slowly.** Slow-play rules and timed rounds are important aspects of
> tournament Magic. **Taking twenty seconds to think through each turn is a great idea, but taking
> several minutes on each turn is too much in a tournament.**

**And the resolution:** thinking at the beginning of your turn **lets you take your actions quickly and
efficiently once you've decided.**

## The four types of action

**Every play you make fits into one or more of these categories.**

### 1. Actions that give YOU more information

Anything that lets you see cards in normally hidden zones — your opponent's hand, the top of either
library. **Anything that draws cards also gives information. Anytime your opponent acts, it gives you
information.**

> **You have the least information on turn one**, because you haven't drawn many cards and don't know
> every tool you'll have later, **and you might not know much about your opponent's deck.** You have
> more information as things drag on and the important aspects of the game reveal themselves.

### 2. Actions that give YOUR OPPONENT more information

> **Technically, everything you do falls into this category.** When you cast a spell you've revealed it,
> shown when and how you intend to cast it, and shown that you now have one fewer card.

**The key is gauging how important the information is.** Playing your land on turn three is very
unlikely to affect their decisions. **Playing your bomb creature might change them dramatically.**

> **Once you show your Brutal Hordechief, you can no longer trick your opponent into using Reach of
> Shadows on a weaker creature.**

### 3. Actions that force YOU to make decisions

Attack the 2/2 into the untapped 3/3 and **you'll soon have to decide whether to use Awaken the Bear.**
Cast **Bitter Revelation** and **you don't know which four cards you'll see, and must choose on the fly
which two to take.**

### 4. Actions that force YOUR OPPONENT to make decisions

Attack the 2/2 into the 3/3 and **you force them to decide whether to block.** Cast **Rakshasa's
Secret** and **you force them to decide which two cards to discard.**

## The rule that follows

> **You want the most possible information when you make your decisions, and you want your opponent to
> have the least possible information when they make theirs.**

| Do EARLY in the turn | Do LATE in the turn |
|---|---|
| Actions that give **you** more information | Actions that give **your opponent** more information |
| Actions that force **your opponent** to decide | Actions that force **you** to decide |

**The complication:** many cards fit multiple categories. **Bitter Revelation** and **Diplomacy of the
Wastes** both give you information *and* force you to decide.

> Should you cast Diplomacy of the Wastes sooner in order to gain information for another decision, **or
> take other actions first, allowing you to make the most informed decision possible when you finally
> see your opponent's hand? It all depends on the circumstances.**

## Example 1

**The board.** Your hand is **Swamp**, **Awaken the Bear**, and **Bitter Revelation**. **Your opponent
has no cards in hand.**

**Step 1 — the plan.** Attack. Cast Awaken the Bear **if** they block, not if they don't. If you don't
cast Awaken the Bear, cast Bitter Revelation. Play a land.

**Step 2 — identify the decisions.** Your only remaining decision is **which cards to take off Bitter
Revelation.** Your opponent's decision is **whether to block with Longshot Squad**, and you want them
making it with as little information as possible.

**Step 3 — sequence it.**

**Attack before Bitter Revelation.** In this case it's forced, since you need the mana for Awaken the
Bear. **But even with seven mana available you'd attack first**, for two reasons:

- **To conceal information from your opponent before they decide.**
- **To gather information before your own decision** — you'll know whether they blocked and whether you
  cast Awaken the Bear before choosing cards off Bitter Revelation.

**Play the land last.** Duke gives three reasons:

1. **It gives your opponent information**, so it shouldn't come before you force the block decision.
2. **Bitter Revelation gives you information that could change the decision.** What if you take a land
   that enters tapped? What if you take **Dark Deal** and decide not to play a land at all?
3. **It conceals information.** Casting Bitter Revelation before playing a land **keeps open, from your
   opponent's perspective, the possibility that you chose a land off Bitter Revelation.**

**The final sequence:**

1. **Attack.**
2. **If the opponent blocks, cast Awaken the Bear.**
3. **If the opponent doesn't block, cast Bitter Revelation.**
4. **Play a land.**

## Example 2

**The board.** Your hand is **Forest**, **Rakshasa's Secret**, **Awaken the Bear**, **Empty the Pits**.
**Your opponent has three cards in hand.**

**Step 1 — the plan.** Play a land, cast Rakshasa's Secret, attack. Cast Awaken the Bear if they block.

**Step 2 — identify the decisions.** **This turn you won't have any new decisions based on new
information.** Your goal is entirely to force your opponent to decide with minimum information. **Their
biggest decision is what to discard to Rakshasa's Secret**, since they hold three cards.

**The final sequence:**

1. **Cast Rakshasa's Secret.**
2. **Play Forest.**
3. **Attack.**
4. **If the opponent blocks, cast Awaken the Bear. If not, end your turn.**

## Whether to act before or after combat

> **Under ordinary circumstances, attacking should be the first thing you do on a turn.**

**Why:** if a creature doesn't have haste or a relevant ability, **there's no reason to cast it before
you attack.** If your opponent is deciding whether to block or use removal, **all you've done is give
them more information.**

### The land exception, and its clever inversion

**Sometimes you need to play your land to enable a combat trick**, in which case obviously play it
first. **If you have no trick, your goal becomes concealing information for as long as possible.**

**But here's the twist:**

> **Since players sometimes play a land precombat to enable a combat trick, it follows that playing your
> land precombat is sometimes actually the best way to conceal information.**

- **Attack with your Ainok Guide before playing your third land** → **you're demonstrating you cannot
  cast Awaken the Bear.**
- **Attack into a 3/3 before playing your fourth land** → **you're hinting you might have specifically
  Awaken the Bear, but not Dragonscale Boon.**

**The best example is morphs.** With four lands in play, attacking a morph into another 2/2 **hints you
either have a spell to cast or want to trade.** But **play your fifth land before attacking and the
possibilities multiply** — from their perspective your morph could be a **Snowhorn Rider**, a **Glacial
Stalker**, or any of a dozen things.

> **By increasing the possibilities of what might happen during combat, you've concealed information
> from your opponent.**

**And a practical safety argument:** playing a land before combat **helps you prepare for the
unexpected.** You might hold a land to conceal information **but open yourself to disaster if your
opponent casts Stubborn Denial on your combat trick.**

### Duke's general habit

> **Play your lands before you attack**, particularly in Khans of Tarkir Limited. **Typically play most
> creatures and sorceries after you attack.** But be willing to deviate when circumstances call for it —
> in Example 1, where you might cast Bitter Revelation after combat, **it's certainly best to wait
> before playing your land.**

## The close

> **Proper sequencing is all about managing information.** Play so that you have the maximum information
> before your important decisions. **Conceal information from your opponent for as long as possible,
> particularly when you can present them with a difficult decision.**

> In many of these examples the sequencing might seem small and unimportant, **but in Magic, the small
> things add up. If even one game out of a hundred you lead your opponent into making a wrong,
> uninformed decision, you'll be a better player for it.**

## Notes for use

- **"Think through your entire turn before you do anything" is presented by Duke as the single most
  valuable concrete piece of advice in the course.** It's also nearly free — twenty seconds after your
  draw step. If you take one habit from Level One, take this one.
- **The four-category table is the whole technique.** Information-gathering and opponent-decision-forcing
  go early; information-leaking and self-decision-forcing go late. Everything else in the installment is
  an application.
- **The tell about pausing is the underrated benefit.** Thinking at a decision point *announces* the
  decision point. Front-loading all your thinking hides which branch you're on. That's an information
  leak most players never consider they're producing.
- **The land-timing inversion is the most sophisticated idea here** and generalizes well: because a
  known convention exists (play land precombat to represent a trick), *following* the convention becomes
  concealment and *deviating* becomes a tell. Any repeated pattern in your play can be read; adding
  possibilities is what protects you.
- **Cross-reference:** installment 04 covers the mechanical order of the combat phase, which is the
  rules substrate this installment sits on. Installment 28 (When to Cast Your Spells) extends the timing
  question. The information-concealment theme is the practical version of Flores's principle 5, "all war
  is based on deception," in
