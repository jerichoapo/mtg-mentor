# "Creature" Lands

**Reid Duke — Level One, September 7, 2015**

---

## Why nonbasics matter

**Most players learn using basic lands.** They're simple, reliable, and the one tool we always have
access to. **But nonbasic lands are tremendously important too, and as players get more competitive in
Constructed they encounter them more and more.**

The installment is framed around Magic's return to Zendikar, home to adventure, treasure hunting, and
hostile landscapes — **and Duke notes that for anyone who played the original block, the landscapes
are quite literally hostile.**

## What creature lands are

**Lavaclaw Reaches** is the example he opens with. **Creature lands are an incredibly powerful and
important category, played at the highest level of competition in every format from Vintage through
Standard and Booster Draft.**

**They vary considerably:**

- Some **produce only colorless mana.**
- Some **enter the battlefield tapped.**
- **Foundry of the Consuls** is a different type entirely, since it **must be sacrificed before it can
  actually produce creatures.**

**What they all share:**

> **All creature lands increase the midgame and late-game power of the decks they're in. They can be
> used for mana in the early game and later become deadly threats. Importantly, they provide insurance
> against mana flood, and can thereby increase the consistency of a deck.**

## The preview: Lumbering Falls

**Templated just like its ancestors**, two of which are **Lavaclaw Reaches** and **Celestial
Colonnade**. It **enters the battlefield tapped** but **produces two different colors of mana**, and
**for an affordable though not cheap cost it becomes a relevant and hard-to-kill creature.**

## Investment

**When you play a land that enters tapped, you're making an investment.**

**The tradeoff, stated cleanly:** it's clearly more powerful to have Lumbering Falls in play than an
Island or a Forest. **The catch is that an Island or Forest can be used right away, whereas with
Lumbering Falls you're forced to wait a turn.**

> **You spend a turn "investing" in Lumbering Falls, and in return you find yourself with a powerful
> creature land in play later.**

**Who can't afford it:** a blisteringly fast deck, since it can prevent you playing out creatures on
time.

**Who can:** an average deck, especially in Standard. **Duke's argument is about wasted mana rather
than about power:**

> Many Standard decks only play a small number of one-mana creatures, if any. **If you play a Forest on
> the first turn but have no creature to cast, your mana is going to waste anyway. You might as well
> use that opportunity to lay down a Lumbering Falls.**

## Mana curve

**Even with an Elvish Mystic for turn one**, you can lead with a Forest and find a convenient time for
Lumbering Falls later.

> **You can consider lands that enter the battlefield tapped as giving you more flexibility in how you
> build your mana curve.**

**The concrete version:** play Lumbering Falls on turn one, **or** play a two-drop creature and a
Lumbering Falls on turn three.

> **If your goal is to waste as little mana as possible, laying a tapped land is effectively a way to
> spend (or invest) one mana on whatever turn is most convenient.**

## Insurance against mana flood

**In many games creature lands are relatively simple to play.** Treat them as a normal land early until
you've played out your hand. **Then, once you reach the late stages and no longer need to spend all
your mana every turn, you start attacking.**

> Think of every game where you've drawn too many lands and had nothing to do with them. **Wouldn't
> you have loved to cash one in for a 3/3 hexproof Elemental?**

**The indirect benefit, which is the smarter half of the argument:**

> **Creature lands also help mitigate being mana hosed.** When you don't have to worry as much about
> mana flood, **you're free to play more lands than you normally would** — you might feel comfortable
> playing 24 or 25 instead of 23.

**All told, a fantastic way to improve consistency and have fewer awkward hands.**

## Turning the corner

**Creature lands give their controller a remarkable ability to switch gears at a moment's notice.**
Defend and develop early, **and then start firing up your creature lands and attacking much faster than
your opponent was prepared for.**

Duke explicitly links back to earlier material: **turning the corner is an important part of damage
racing**, and **creature lands help you turn the corner in a big way.**

> **Turning the corner means switching quickly between playing defense and going for the win.** Once
> you've survived the early onslaught, there comes a turn when you fire up your creature lands, make a
> big attack, and suddenly put your opponent on their heels.

## What's special about Lumbering Falls

**Two reasons beyond the generic case.**

**1. It produces two colors.** So beyond reducing flood and hosed risk, **it's a dual land that helps
you have all your colors on time.** Duke expected it to be **a four-of auto-include for any blue-green
deck in Standard**, and **a huge incentive for players to choose that color combination.**

**2. Hexproof.** Creature lands already have **built-in resilience to board sweepers like Languish.**
Since Lumbering Falls **cannot be targeted by spot removal like Abzan Charm**, it makes for a
**nightmare threat against control decks.**

**And the subtler consequence of hexproof, which is the best analysis in the installment:**

> **A four-mana activation would ordinarily be somewhat pricy, opening you to an unfavorable tempo
> swing when you plan to attack or block with your creature land only to see it get killed.** Hexproof
> means **you can comfortably plan on it doing exactly what you need, when you need it.**

**The defensive application:** leave Lumbering Falls and four other lands untapped, and **it's
difficult for weenie creatures to attack into you, whether or not the opponent has removal in hand.**

## Notes for use

- **The strongest idea here is the second-order one:** creature lands don't only fix flood, they
  change your *land count*. Because flood is less punishing, you can run 24–25 instead of 23, which
  reduces screw. One card class improving both tails of the mana distribution is unusual and worth
  internalizing.
- **"A tapped land is a way to spend one mana on whatever turn is most convenient"** is the reframe
  that makes tapped lands feel less bad. It converts a fixed cost into a schedulable one.
- **The wasted-mana argument is the real justification for turn-one tapped lands.** Not "it's worth
  the cost" but "on turn one there was frequently no cost to pay." Check the premise before applying
  it: it holds only if your deck genuinely lacks one-drops.
- **The hexproof analysis generalizes past this card.** An expensive activated ability carries hidden
  risk, since the opponent can respond and blow you out. Protection converts an unreliable ability into
  a plannable one, which is worth more than the raw stats suggest.
- **Cross-reference:** "turning the corner" is defined in installment 10 (Midrange Decks) and recurs in
  installment 18 (Damage Racing). Mana flood and mana hosed are from installment 02. The
  land-count-versus-consistency question is treated rigorously in
  `block-3-manabase-reference/02-how-many-lands-do-you-need-2017-05-30.md`.
- **Reading caution:** the back half is a Battle for Zendikar preview from 2015. The category analysis
  in the first half is durable and still describes how creature lands are evaluated today.
