# Signals in Booster Draft

**Reid Duke — Level One, January 19, 2015**

---

## The problem being solved

> **One of the most important and challenging questions in a booster draft is how and when to choose your
> colors.**

> **It's hard to know when to stick to the colors of your first pick, when to change colors based on a
> perceived signal, and when to move in on a bomb card you open in pack two or three.**

**Duke's aim: a relatively simple framework for those decisions.** That framework is the tier system.

---

## The tier system

> **The tier system is a way of gauging the relative strengths of the cards you see in a draft.**

**Why tiers rather than a ranked list:**

> **Instead of creating an absolute power ranking of all the cards in the set — which is difficult and
> can unnecessarily constrain your options — you simply slot each card into a tier along with cards of
> comparable strength.**

**The decision rule that falls out of it:**

> **When deciding between cards of the same tier, you're free to choose the one that fits better into
> your deck. When deciding between cards of different tiers, you'll think more deeply and begin to
> consider signals.**

### The five tiers

**1. Bombs.** Powerful enough to **frequently determine the outcome of a game all on their own.**

> **Opening a bomb in your first pack is like winning the lottery.** They're the highest tier because
> **you should pick them over anything early in the draft, and later in the draft they can make you
> consider a dramatic shift in the direction you're going.**

**2. Premium removal.** By premium he means **high in quality — versatile, reliable, mana efficient —
and hard to come by.**

> Unlike bombs these won't win the game alone, **but they give you a great amount of control and can
> sometimes answer opposing bombs. You should be happy to spend your first couple of picks on premium
> removal.**

**3. Strong filler.** **Filler is the cards that make up the bulk of your deck.**

> **In most drafts you'll be lucky to get one bomb and two or three pieces of premium removal. The rest
> of your deck will be your run-of-the-mill, bread-and-butter cards.**

**Strong filler** = cards you're happy to play and **will very rarely cut.** Includes **the better
portion of common and uncommon creatures; removal that might be a little expensive but is still
reliable; and a small handful of the absolute best noncreature, non-removal cards** like **Force Away**.

**4. Weak filler and sideboard cards.** The **take-'em-or-leave-'em cards you need to round out the
deck.**

> **It's normal and totally acceptable to play with a couple of unexciting morphs, overpriced removal
> spells, or average-quality combat tricks.**

> **Typically you'll end the draft with more weak filler than you need.** Since some won't make your
> maindeck anyway, **there's value in leaving yourself options for sideboarding.**

**5. Mostly useless.**

> **You can save yourself some trouble by pretending these cards don't exist; if you do, you'll only be
> making a mistake a very, very small portion of the time.**

---

## The first pack: finding direction

**P1P1 is easy:** absent strong preferences about color or archetype, **pick the best card.**

**Pick two is where it gets interesting.** In a perfect world it matches your first pick's color, **but
does that mean passing up a stronger card to stay on-color?**

### The procedure

> **First identify the best overall card in the pack, then identify the best on-color card.** Assuming
> they're different, **how large is the gap in power level?**

> **If they're in different tiers, take the best card. If they're in the same tier, take the on-color
> card**, using the fit with your first pick as a tiebreaker.

### Worked both ways

**Same tier → stay on-color.** First pick **Wingmate Roc**. Pack two has **Feat of Resistance** and
**Heir of the Wilds**.

> Heir of the Wilds is almost certainly better, **but the gap is not huge — they're both strong filler.
> Take Feat of Resistance, delaying your choice of a second color and leaving you more flexible and open
> to opportunities for the rest of the draft.**

**Different tier → take the better card.** **Feat of Resistance** versus **Murderous Cut**.

> Murderous Cut is a tier above, being premium removal, **and the difference is large enough to be worth
> dipping into a new color.**

> **It's good to stay open in a draft and wait for opportunities, but what are you waiting for if not
> something like a Murderous Cut?**

**The rule, generalized:**

> **When two cards are in the same tier, take the one that fits best with your deck. When one card
> stands out in a class of its own, consider taking it** — either moving into a new color, **or simply
> speculating on a powerful off-color card in case you switch later.**

## Signals

**Duke's reassurance, and it's the structural insight of the installment:**

> **The concept of signals is daunting, even for experienced drafters. But not to worry — if you employ
> the tier system, you'll be naturally acting on signals even without conscious effort.**

> When you're passed a very strong card that stands out on a tier of its own, **the tier system would
> have you pick that card.** And from the signals perspective, **seeing a card that stands on a tier of
> its own gives you a hint toward an open color.**

> **The tier system and the concept of signals lead you to the same decision. So you can benefit from
> signals without even being conscious of it.**

### The concepts behind signals

> **Your goal is to draft different colors from the people around you; if you succeed, you will
> naturally be passed more good cards simply by virtue of your neighbors not wanting them.**

**The layered version:**

> **You don't want to draft colors that are generally overdrafted at your table** — if six of eight
> players are drafting black, the good black cards dry up quickly. **You don't want to share colors with
> the player on your right, or two to your right, or three to your right. You don't want to share with
> the player to your left, or two to your left**, and so on.

> **Of course you can't achieve all of these at once; it's simply not reasonable to expect you can be the
> only drafter interested in your colors.** He raises it only to note **there are slight differences
> between all these situations.**

### Pack one: receiving from the right

**Being passed a standout card like Murderous Cut hints the players passing to you aren't in black.**

**Duke is careful about the epistemics, which is what makes this section good:**

> **I emphasize the word hint because a signal will never prove anything beyond a doubt.**
>
> - **Perhaps your neighbor is drafting black but took a Sorin, Solemn Visitor over Murderous Cut.**
> - **Perhaps your neighbor passed it but will decide to start drafting black afterwards.**
> - **Perhaps they simply don't value Murderous Cut the way you do.**

> **However, being passed a Murderous Cut is one piece of evidence you can note, and if you continue to
> see more signals that black is open, this evidence begins to add up and contribute toward a rough
> picture of what might be going on.**

### The absence signal

> **You can also take a signal from the absence of strong cards.**

**The example:** first-pick Wingmate Roc, second-pick Feat of Resistance. **Pack three's best white card
is Sage-Eye Harrier. Pack four has no white card. Pack five has only an Erase.**

> **You're seeing an abnormally small number of white cards with a notable absence of anything exciting.
> This is a signal that one or more of the players passing to you might be drafting white.**

### Pack one: sending to the left

> **If you pass along a Murderous Cut, it's very likely someone downstream will wind up drafting black.
> If you snap up every good white card you see, it's a little less likely the player to your left will be
> drafting white — but you can't be sure. You don't know all the variables in that player's draft and you
> can never be sure of another individual's thought process.**

## Acting on signals: the conundrum

> **Reading signals is about accumulating information over the course of the draft. Acting on signals
> therefore presents a bit of a conundrum.**

> **The longer you wait to act, the more certain you become about what signals you're seeing. However,
> the longer you wait, the less time remains for you to reap the benefits of finding the open color.**

> **Knowing how to balance these things is one of the more challenging aspects of booster drafts and will
> depend more on experience than on anything I can write in an article.**

### Case A: follow the signal

**Your picks are weak and uncommitted.** P1 **Heir of the Wilds** (no bomb or premium removal in the
pack). P2 **Force Away** from a weak pack. P3 has **Suspension Field** (premium removal) and **Savage
Punch**.

> This is a close pick where the tier system can only inform your decision. **Savage Punch is noticeably
> weaker than Suspension Field but could reasonably be assigned to either premium removal or solid
> filler.** Moreover **you already have two colors, so jumping into a third this early comes at a cost.**

**Say you take Savage Punch — but note the Suspension Field as a signal that white might be open.**

**P4** has **Mardu Hordechief** as a standout, with **Wetland Sambar** the best in your colors.

> **You've now seen two signals in a row that white might be open, and it's a good time to act. Your
> green-blue deck isn't shaping up to be particularly stellar, and you won't desperately miss that
> Wetland Sambar.**

**And the reasoning for the pick is precise:**

> **Take Mardu Hordechief — but not because it alone is a strong enough card to make you change gears.
> What you're really doing is speculating on the chance of getting more strong white cards later, which
> reading signals has given you reason to believe might happen.**

### Case B: stick to your guns

**Back to the Wingmate Roc draft.** By pick seven or eight **white has dried up and you're pretty
confident one or more players on your right are drafting white. Should you jump ship?**

> **Here the answer is no.**

**Two reasons:**

1. **Wingmate Roc is such a strong card that you're loath to give up on it.**
2. **It's late enough that you're unlikely to reap many rewards from finding an open color. By pick
   eight the premium cards in pack one are already gone.**

**And the insight that resolves it — the direction of passing reverses:**

> **Granted, it's a problem that you're unlikely to be passed good white in pack three. However, what
> about pack two? It's the players on your left passing to you in pack two, and you've sent them a
> reasonably strong signal that they should not be drafting white** — by your failure to pass white
> cards downstream. **You're likely to be passed very good white in pack two.**

**The two options laid side by side:**

| | Option A: stick with white | Option B: jump ship |
|---|---|---|
| **Wingmate Roc** | **you get to play it** | **give it up**, plus four or five other white picks |
| **Rest of pack one** | as-is | **marginally better filler** |
| **Pack two** | **likely very good white** | **uncertain** |
| **Pack three** | **risk a weak pack** | **hope for a good pack** |

> **Between those two, I like Option A better.**

**Duke's summary of the tension:**

> **Don't get me wrong, it's never ideal to draft the same colors as the player passing to you. However,
> the key factor is that by the time you've gathered enough information via signals to make an educated
> decision, the time to switch colors has already passed.**

## How should you be drafting?

> **Signals, and Booster Draft in general, are deep and complicated subjects that can take years to fully
> master.**

**His advice for newer drafters:**

> **Start thinking a bit more about signals, and taking note of what you're seeing and what it might
> mean. Being aware will accelerate your learning process. But for the most part your primary focus
> should still be on putting together your own deck, rather than trying to take on too many challenges
> at once.**

**And the closing restatement of the framework:**

> **When two cards are of comparable strength, take the one that's more valuable to you in the moment —
> matching your colors, filling a hole in your curve. When cards are vastly different in power level, you
> can consider changing colors, or at least speculating on what might come later.**

> **In this way, you'll allow yourself to naturally go to, and benefit from, where the signals are taking
> you.**

---

## Notes for use

- **The tier system is the deliverable, and its best property is that it makes signal-reading automatic.**
  You don't need to consciously track seven other drafters; picking the standout card when one exists
  *is* following the signal. That's an unusually elegant piece of teaching.
- **The same-tier / different-tier rule is the whole decision procedure**, and it's simple enough to
  actually use under time pressure: same tier → take what fits, different tier → take the better card and
  consider what it means.
- **Duke's epistemic care about signals is worth imitating.** Three separate innocent explanations for
  any single passed card. A signal is *one piece of evidence*, and only accumulation makes a picture.
  Overreacting to a single card is the standard beginner error.
- **The absence signal is easy to miss and just as informative** as a passed bomb: an abnormally thin
  color with nothing exciting means someone upstream is in it.
- **Case B is the most sophisticated argument here.** The reason to stay in a contested color isn't
  stubbornness — it's that **pack two reverses the direction of passing**, and your own picks have already
  signalled that color away from the neighbors who will feed you next. That's a genuinely non-obvious
  structural point.
- **The timing conundrum is the honest core:** certainty and opportunity move in opposite directions, and
  Duke declines to pretend there's a formula. Experience is the actual answer.
