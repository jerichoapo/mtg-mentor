# Booster Draft, Part 3

**Reid Duke — Level One, February 2, 2015**

---

## Where this sits

Having covered the basics and signals, **Part 3 offers tips and techniques that might not occur
naturally to beginning drafters.**

---

## Prioritizing the key pieces of your deck

**The corrective that opens the installment:**

> Through everything you learn about Booster Draft — all the fancy terms and concepts and techniques —
> **the basic principles of Magic will remain the most important weapons at your disposal.**

> **Signals, archetypes, counterdrafting, and just about everything else should take a back seat to card
> advantage, removal spells, and mana base.**

### The skeleton of a well-built draft deck

**Duke's framework for a triple-Khans draft**, stressed as a rough guideline that changes with colors
and strategy:

| Component | Count |
|---|---|
| **Total cards** | **40** |
| **Total lands** | **17–18** |
| — two-color deck | **0–2 dual lands** |
| — three-color deck | **4+ dual lands** |
| **Total noncreature spells** | **5–8** |
| — combat tricks | **0–2** |
| — removal spells | **4–8** |
| **Total creatures** | **14–17** |

**The creature curve:**

| Cost | Count |
|---|---|
| **One-drops** | **0–2** |
| **Two-drops** | **2–4** |
| **Three-drops and morphs** | **5–8** |
| **Four-drops** | **2–3** |
| **Five-drops** | **2** |
| **Six-drops** | **0–1** |

**Duke's format-specific notes:** Khans **causes a small glut at three because of morphs** (he counts a
creature like **Snowhorn Rider** as a morph in the three-drop slot), and **there's less need for
expensive non-delve creatures because you can spend later turns turning morphs face up or outlasting.**

> **These are only guidelines, not a master formula. It would be a mistake to shoehorn every draft deck
> into this exact framework. Take note if your decks look dramatically different in one or more ways, but
> for the most part this is merely a tool for further discussion.**

### Scarcity, not just quality

**The key economic point:**

> **The quality, or power level, of a card is not the only factor that determines its value in a Booster
> Draft. The supply and demand of the particular card type also plays a big role.**

**Which cards you need in healthy numbers:** **cheap creatures and removal spells.**
**Which are substantially more dispensable:** **expensive creatures, combat tricks, and other
noncreature non-removal spells.**

> **Tusked Colossodon is a completely respectable creature, but given that you only want zero or one
> six-mana creature in your deck at all, it would be a mistake to spend a high pick on an unspectacular,
> expensive creature.**

**His analogy:**

> It's like watching those infomercials on TV. **It might be a great item — of the finest quality and
> craftsmanship, and somebody out there might really love it. It's just that I really don't need to own
> a ten-inch, glass Teddy Roosevelt figurine.**

**The rule:**

> **Put a premium on the cards that are absolutely crucial to your deck. Pick them early and often, and
> let the fringe cards fall into place later.** In pack three, when you already have enough two- and
> three-drops and want a big guy to cap your curve, **that's the time to pick Tusked Colossodon.**

### How this maps onto the tier system

> **Bombs and premium removal represent the highest tiers because of their quality *and* their scarcity.
> If you pass up a bomb or a premium removal spell, you cannot count on getting another one later.**

> Beyond that, **efficient cheap creatures should be slotted into "strong filler," while unexciting
> expensive creatures and the bulk of noncreature, non-removal cards should be slotted into "weak filler"
> or "mostly useless."**

### The tiebreaker rule

> **Early in the draft, any time you're in doubt of which card to take, err on the side of taking the
> cheap creature.**

> **You can't really have too many creatures, but you can most definitely have too few.**

**Both outcomes:**

- **Lots of cheap creatures early** → great. **Spend the second half of the draft picking up spells and
  expensive creatures as you see fit.**
- **Not enough cheap creatures late** → **a disaster. Now you have to scramble and pick cheap creatures
  over stronger cards just to plug the hole in your curve.**

## Choosing your colors: knowing the archetypes

> **Sometimes we get an extra layer of complication thrown into our drafts, and it can change from set to
> set. It's the question of good color combinations and bad color combinations.**

> **If there are colors that clash and cannot easily combine into a good deck, you might be better off
> steering your ship a little against the tides instead of going completely with the flow.**

### The simplified thought experiment

> Imagine a fictional, very simplified format. **White is very defensive — the only white card is Salt
> Road Patrol. Red is very aggressive — the only red card is Valley Dasher.**

> **In this format it makes no sense at all to draft red and white together.** If your plan is to win a
> long game outlasting giant Salt Road Patrols, **Valley Dashers don't contribute.** If your plan is a
> quick rush, **Salt Road Patrol is far too slow.**

> Naturally, real formats are never this simple and color combinations are never this extreme.
> **Nevertheless, there are formats where particular combinations are better or worse than others.**

### Gold cards, and the Khans structure

**Khans is a perfect example because it features powerful multicolored cards.**

**The clans:** five three-color combinations **more desirable than other color combinations.**

> If you drafted green-white-blue instead of Abzan (white-black-green), **you'd miss out on Abzan Guide,
> Armament Corps, Abzan Charm, and a ton of other premium gold cards. What a waste.**

**The two-color gold cards are all in enemy colors** — for example white's enemies are red and black,
its allies green and blue. Cards like **Chief of the Edge** and **Icefeather Aven** are **strong
incentives to draft enemy-color combinations.**

**And Duke's resulting opening heuristic, which is the sharpest practical tip in the installment:**

> **I find it convenient to start a Khans draft with an enemy-color combination. This way you have three
> options: build a two-color deck enjoying powerful two-color gold cards, move into a clan featuring the
> two enemy colors, or move into the *other* clan featuring the two enemy colors.**

> **Starting in white-black can lead you into Abzan, Mardu, or leave you with a two-color deck.**

> **So in Khans, enemy-color combinations have the advantage. If my first pick is a blue card, I'll hope
> to also second-pick a blue card. If I can't, I'd much prefer to pick a green or red card rather than a
> white or black card.**

*(The general lesson beneath the format specifics: prefer early pairs that keep the most viable
finishing archetypes open.)*

### Knowing which cards need which shell

> **As you learn a format you'll begin to discover particular strategies that come together more nicely
> than others. You should have these archetypes in mind as you draft.**

**Two examples of a card whose power depends entirely on its shell:**

- **Raiders' Spoils** is mono-black, leaving many combinations open. **But black and white have the most
  Warriors, and W/B Warriors is a very strong and reliable archetype.** You might sometimes play it in
  Sultai, **but the card will only be at a fraction of the power level it would have in W/B Warriors.**
- **Treasure Cruise** is **a bit overpriced in an average green-blue aggressive deck, but extremely good
  in a Sultai Delve deck with lots of ways to fill its graveyard.**

## When is it too late to switch colors?

> **The deeper you get into a draft, the more you should solidify yourself in your colors.** This usually
> won't be a huge challenge, **since you aren't likely to see top-tier cards very late into pack one.**

> **The problem is that if you continue switching colors throughout the draft, you might wind up without
> enough playable cards or with some other problem that leads to a weak deck.**

**Duke re-quotes the conundrum from the previous installment:**

> **The longer you wait to act, the more certain you become about what signals you're seeing. However,
> the longer you wait, the less time remains to reap the benefits of finding the open color.**

### The timing windows

> **There's a sweet spot for following signals. You should be gathering information throughout the whole
> draft, but the best time to find an open color is between approximately pick two and approximately
> pick seven of pack one.** At this point **there's still sufficient time to reap the rewards.**

| Milestone | Timing |
|---|---|
| **Best window to find an open color** | **P1p2 – P1p7** |
| **Should have a good idea of your colors** | **around P1p8–p9** |
| **Latest you can safely switch** | **around P2p2–p3** |

## The pack-two question

**The most valuable analysis in the installment.**

> **What happens when you open a bomb in pack two?** Say you're drafting Abzan and open **Sarkhan, the
> Dragonspeaker**.

> **The question of whether to stick with Abzan or change into red depends not only on the strength of
> Sarkhan, but on what you can expect to happen in the rest of the draft.**

**The structural fact that decides it:**

> **Since it's the players on your left passing to you in pack two, the question is what colors are they
> drafting? You can try to answer this based on the signals that *you* sent in pack one.**

**The questions to ask yourself:**

- **What strong red cards did you pass?**
- **What strong Mardu cards did you pass?**
- **Did you pass anything so good in other colors that your neighbors would be strongly incentivized to
  draft something else?**

**And the trap, stated precisely:**

> **If red was very open in pack one, an understandable mistake would be to take the Sarkhan hoping red
> will remain open. However, if you received a signal in pack one that red was open — meaning you were
> passed a lot of strong red cards — and you were drafting Abzan, then you probably passed those cards
> along to the players on your left, signaling them that *they* ought to be drafting red.**

**The consequence:**

> **If you took the Sarkhan you might hope to get passed good red in pack three, but in pack two the
> players on your left are likely to gobble up all the red cards and cut you off.**

> **You could switch into red banking on pack three to reward you, but you'd really be playing with
> fire. It's incredibly dangerous to speculate so far in advance that you start pack three with only a
> couple of cards of one of your main colors. There's a lot that could go wrong.**

**The rule:**

> **When you open a bomb in pack two, whether to take it depends on the signals you sent and received in
> pack one** — in addition to what other cards are in the pack alongside it.

> **Generally speaking, the earlier you can choose your colors the better off you'll be.**

## Notes for use

- **The deck skeleton is the most immediately usable artifact in the Limited section.** 17–18 lands,
  14–17 creatures, 4–8 removal, 0–2 tricks, and a curve weighted at three. Check every draft deck against
  it and investigate any large deviation.
- **Scarcity as a pick criterion is the conceptual advance here.** A card's draft value = quality ×
  how badly your deck needs that *slot*. A fine six-drop is a bad pick because you want at most one. This
  is why "take the cheap creature when in doubt" is correct: the failure mode is asymmetric.
- **The enemy-color opening is a beautiful piece of practical reasoning** and generalizes past Khans:
  open with the pair that leaves the most finishing archetypes available. Optionality has value in a
  process where information arrives over time.
- **The pack-two question is the deepest idea in the whole draft sequence.** The signals you *received*
  in pack one become the signals you *sent*, and pack two reverses the direction of passing — so an open
  color in pack one is likely to be contested in pack two precisely because you fed it. That's a real
  second-order effect and almost nobody reasons through it.
- **Note the pairing with installment 33's Case B**, which uses the same reversal in your favour: staying
  in a cut color works because you signalled it away from the neighbors who feed you next. Same mechanism,
  opposite conclusion, depending on which direction you were passing.
- **Cross-reference:** installment 31 (basics, counterdrafting), 33 (tier system, signals), 35
  (walkthrough), 32 (drafting sideboard cards). The curve reasoning traces back to installment 02.
