# Becoming a Better Player

**Reid Duke — Level One, September 28, 2015**

---

## The farewell framing

> **This will be the final article I write for Level One.** Over fourteen months I've tried my best to
> **build a complete, self-contained course that offers the tools one needs to become a competitive Magic
> tournament player.**

**And the purpose of this last piece:**

> **Before we part ways, it's my duty to prepare you for "Level Two." For better or for worse, a complete
> step-by-step manual for becoming a truly world-class Magic player may never exist.**

> **If your goals extend beyond the scope of Level One, the ball is going to be in your court. Let me tell
> you everything I know about becoming a better player.**

---

# Part I: Process

## Practice makes perfect

> **If your goal is to improve at Magic, the best thing to do, by far, is play a lot of Magic. The most
> dominant winning streaks in the game's history have been put together by players who were simply
> eating, sleeping, and breathing Magic.**

> **When you're playing a lot, you see winning lines more easily, you make fewer mistakes, and you
> understand what's going on in the games on a deeper level.**

**The fundamentals argument:**

> **If you're not "in shape" — if the fundamentals aren't coming quickly and effortlessly — then it's
> going to be that much more difficult to improve at the more challenging facets of the game.**

**But hours aren't the metric:**

> **It's not just about putting in the hours, and having a full-time job or other commitments does not
> preclude you from being a world-class player. More important than the number of hours you play is how
> much you're actually taking away from those hours. Quality over quantity.**

**And the specific warning, which is sharper than the usual version:**

> **Deep focus on a single game, from start to finish, is a world apart from playing while distracted. Is
> the TV on in the background? Are you multi-queuing on Magic Online? Are you playing while frustrated,
> tired, or otherwise not in your best frame of mind?**

> **Not only is this an inefficient use of time, but if you do it too often it can also train you into bad
> habits.**

---

## Asking why

**The named method, and the most valuable artifact in the installment.**

> **When you finish a game, you should reflect on it, and ask yourself why the winning player won.**

> **The exercise is more challenging than it sounds, particularly when you've just lost. It requires
> patience, honesty, and a level head. Asking why is about taking responsibility for your own
> decisions.**

### The five levels, in order

**1. Technical mistakes and judgment calls.**
> **Did you miss a potential point of damage early in the game that might've changed things?**

**2. The mental game.**
> **If you'd known your opponent had a permission spell, could you have played around it? Were there
> hints you could've picked up on to learn what was in your opponent's hand?**

**3. Sideboarding and deckbuilding.**
> **Did you get mana flooded? If so, was it a fluke, or did your post-sideboard configuration lack
> late-game staying power?**

**4. Deck choice.**
> **Have you lost enough games in this same way that you've revealed a fatal flaw in your deck?**

**5. Bad luck — and only last.**
> **Many times your losses will largely be due to bad luck. However, be careful not to hide behind that
> excuse. Look to it only after you've left no stone unturned with regard to the aspects of the game that
> you did have control over.**

*(The ordering is the whole method: each level is only reached after exhausting the one above it, and luck
is the last resort rather than the first explanation.)*

---

## Research

> **Improving at Magic is going to be a largely internal process. However, trying to figure out everything
> by yourself will be difficult. Sometimes you won't realize you have a misconception that's causing you
> to persistently make the same mistake.**

> **Incorporating outside ideas into your study will help prevent this, and will accelerate your
> learning.**

**What's available:** **new decklists and tournament coverage every weekend, strategy articles from dozens
of sites, videos of the masters at the Pro Tour or World Championship.**

> **Each week, more Magic strategy content comes out than one individual could possibly keep up with, and
> that's not even to mention the twenty years of backlogged information you can still find on the
> internet. There's no reason to ever feel stuck.**

**The caveat:**

> **Research should be supplementing your own practice, not replacing it. Use the resources at your
> fingertips to take in new ideas, and then incorporate them into your own gameplay, in your own way.**

## Other players

> **The most valuable resources available to you are other players. There's no faster way to grow than to
> put yourself out there and be ready to learn from others. Listen to them, ask questions of them, watch
> them play, and play against them.**

**And the point that it isn't only about stronger players:**

> **You'll learn fastest by playing against players who are better or more experienced than you. However,
> there's plenty to learn from everyone, even players who might not have enjoyed as much Magic success as
> you have.**

> **Everyone has had different experiences and cultivated different opinions. Listen to what people have
> to say, put it through the filter of your own mind, and figure out how you can use it to improve.**

# Part II: Attitude

## Mastering luck

> **The concept of luck is challenging, but a proper understanding of it is essential for any serious
> Magic player.**

**The danger of over-blaming luck:**

> **Blaming bad luck can make you blind to mistakes you might be making. Worse yet, it can make you
> complacent, and can diminish your desire to win and to improve.**

**The reality, stated plainly:**

> **Many of the losses you take will be the result of bad luck. Sometimes you will get a hand with no
> lands and be forced to mulligan. Sometimes you will get paired against your worst matchup in the crucial
> round. Sometimes the cards will simply come in an order that leads to you losing. There are many
> uncertain elements in Magic, and not everything is in your hands.**

**The danger of under-blaming luck:**

> **Blaming bad luck all the time is unhealthy, but the opposite is dangerous as well. Losing a game
> cannot be the end of the world for you. You cannot beat yourself up, scrap your deck idea, or quit
> Magic every time you mulligan down to five.**

**The balance:**

> **You must not hide behind luck and let it excuse your own less-than-perfect decisions. However, you
> should also understand and accept that not everything is under your control.**

## When you're losing

**Duke's observation about strong players:**

> **One trait that many of the best players I know have in common is that they take losses very hard.
> Great players tend to take responsibility for their own mistakes and misjudgments, and blame themselves
> for a high portion of the games they lose.** (**This probably has something to do with how they became
> great in the first place.**)

> **It's painful to put everything you have into something and come up short. Unfortunately it's a common
> experience in Magic, since only one player can be the winner of any given tournament.**

**The prescription:**

> **It's okay to feel disappointed when you lose. It's a sign that you care. The key, though, is to be
> disappointed, and then to move on.**

> **Dwelling too long on a loss will make you miserable and stunt your growth. You can't accomplish
> anything when you're down in the dumps, and your gameplay will suffer if you allow yourself to become
> frustrated easily.**

> **The best thing to do is honestly reflect on the loss — not with the goal of making yourself feel bad,
> but with an eye toward avoiding the same problem in the future.**

## When you're winning

**The counterintuitive opening:**

> **If you're winning a lot at Magic, you're probably getting lucky. It's a strange idea to stomach, but
> it absolutely doesn't take away from anyone's accomplishments.**

> **Tournament Magic is about putting yourself in the best possible position to take advantage of good
> luck, should it come to you. It's only when you prepare a lot, you play well, and the cards come your
> way that you'll be successful.**

**The trap:**

> **Don't assume you're not making mistakes just because you're winning. The vast majority of mistakes do
> not swing the outcome of the game. However, if you allow that fact to make you complacent, then how will
> you ever prevent the mistakes that do turn out to be game-losing?**

> **When you are on a high swing, keep working at improving. Your highs will last longer, and your lows
> won't swing as low.**

---

## A healthy state of mind

> **A healthy state of mind is the crux of a successful Magic career.**

**Duke's five requirements:**

**1. Confidence.**
> **You must have the confidence to trust yourself and believe victory is possible. If you don't believe,
> deep down, that you can win, then you won't search your hardest for those subtle plays that define a
> great player.**

**2. Humility.**
> **You must have the humility to see how much you have yet to learn. If you become overconfident and
> disdainful toward other players, you'll destroy the most valuable resources for your own growth.**

**3. Striving for perfection.**
> **Even though perfection may not be possible, you should still reach for it. Some of the topics I've
> covered in Level One may have seemed nitpicky at the time. However, these are the skills you need to
> cultivate in order to become an elite player. "Good enough" should never really be good enough.**

**4. Dedication.**
> **If you enter Magic tournaments, you're going to lose, and you're going to lose a lot. If you allow
> yourself to give up when things aren't going your way, then you'll have no chance of ever achieving your
> goals.**

**5. Love of the game.**
> **If traveling to tournaments, firing up Magic Online, or spending your lunch break studying a strategy
> website are not truly joyous things for you, then becoming a great player is going to be impossible.**

**And the permission to step back:**

> **Life is a long road, and your dedication to Magic will inevitably wax and wane, and that's okay. If
> you're starting to burn out, or if other aspects of life come calling, then it's good to re-evaluate
> your approach to Magic, at least temporarily.**

> **The worst thing you can do is force yourself to keep playing until the game you once loved becomes a
> chore.**

## Farewell

> **I've been playing Magic for the bulk of my life, and offering what I've learned over the years to a
> new generation of players means a lot to me.**

> **Level One covered everything you need to know in order to become a rock-solid, winning tournament
> player. It even touched on a few advanced concepts that more serious players might love to sink their
> teeth into.**

> **With this final article, I hope I've given you the tools you need to continue your study, for as long
> and as far as competitive Magic can hold your interest. If the game can offer you any fraction of the
> joy it's given me over the years, then your time will be well spent.**

> **With that, I leave the rest to you.**

## Notes for use

- **"Asking why," with its five levels in strict order, is the single most portable technique in the
  entire course.** Technical error → mental game → sideboard/deckbuilding → deck choice → luck. The
  ordering is the discipline: luck is admissible only after the four things you controlled have been
  examined. Run it after every loss.
- **Note that it's symmetric in principle but asymmetric in practice.** Duke tells you to ask why *the
  winner won* — which means running the same audit after wins, where the "if you're winning you're
  probably getting lucky" warning applies. Most players only review losses, and that's precisely when
  honest review is hardest.
- **The two-sided luck argument is the mature position** and worth holding onto: over-attributing to luck
  makes you complacent; under-attributing makes you miserable and makes you scrap good decks. Both errors
  are common and they look like opposites of each other.
- **"Quality over quantity" is backed by a specific mechanism** — distracted play doesn't merely waste
  time, it *trains bad habits*. That's a real argument against multi-queuing and background play, not a
  platitude.
- **The confidence/humility pair is the most interesting item in the mental list**, because they pull
  against each other and you need both: confidence to look hard for the winning line, humility to keep
  learning from everyone at the table.
- **The permission to burn out and step back** is unusual for a competitive column and, in Duke's framing,
  strategically correct rather than merely kind: forcing play past the point of enjoyment destroys the
  thing that generates the practice.
- **Cross-reference:** installment 01 named **focus** as the foundational skill and this closes the loop
  on it; installment 44's scheduled reflection is the logistical form of "asking why"; installment 27's
  bias-calibration (Zvi's instinct advice) is the same self-knowledge theme; installment 41's
  stick-with-one-deck argument is the practice-quality argument applied to deck selection.
