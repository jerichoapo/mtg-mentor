# An Introduction to the Popular Constructed Formats

**Reid Duke — Level One, April 27, 2015**

---

## The five variables

**Before the formats themselves, Duke defines what actually distinguishes them.**

**1. Speed.** *On what turn can the fastest decks typically win? How much and what type of early defense
will you need to survive? How often do games drag into the very late stages?*

**2. Power level.** *Where is the bar for what you ought to be doing at each point in the game? How
powerful are the creatures and spells of varying mana costs?*

**3. Balance.** *Do any decks, strategies, or cards stand out from the rest? If so, what does it take to
compete against the top dogs?*

**4. Diversity.** *Can you predict what you're likely to face? How many competitive decks are there? How
much variation within each archetype?*

**5. Complexity.** *How challenging is the gameplay?* **This can affect your deck choice and other
aspects of your tournament preparation.**

### The variable underneath all five

> **The size of the format: how many cards and sets are legal for play?**

> **With very few exceptions, the larger the format, the more powerful and the faster it will be. It will
> usually also be more diverse and more challenging, simply by virtue of allowing for more
> possibilities.**

---

## Standard

> **By far the most played Constructed format, and the easiest starting point for a new player.** As
> such, **the vast majority of examples in Level One come from Standard.**

**A rotating format** — as of writing, **cards released in the last two years are usually legal.**

**What the small card pool produces:**

- **A relatively modest power level.**
- **Not terribly fast** — **it takes extreme circumstances for a game to end before turn 5.**
- **Balance and diversity are not guaranteed.** **There are sometimes a small handful of cards that
  stand out in power level, and it's often in your best interest to build your deck with these cards in
  mind.**

**And a consequence Duke draws out that connects to the mulligan installments:**

> **The best cards are not always replaceable, and some decks will function much differently depending on
> the presence or absence of their key cards.** Consider a Green Devotion deck that performs much better
> when it starts with **Elvish Mystic**, or a Sultai deck that really needs **Satyr Wayfinder** to fuel
> its graveyard.

> **Another hallmark of Standard is how often it changes, so it's important to stay open-minded and be
> ready to adapt.**

*(Example deck: Martin Dang's Red Aggro from Pro Tour Dragons of Tarkir — deploying many creatures
quickly, then unloading damage via **Atarka's Command** and **Stoke the Flames**.)*

---

## Block Constructed

> **The only commonly-played Constructed format with a smaller card pool than Standard.** The chance of
> certain cards standing out in power level **and being difficult to replace becomes even more likely.**

**His example:** in Theros Block Constructed, **Sylvan Caryatid**, **Courser of Kruphix**, and **Elspeth,
Sun's Champion** were staggeringly powerful.

> **You would've wanted a very special reason to do anything other than play with these cards, or play a
> strategy that was effective at beating these cards.**

---

## Modern

**A non-rotating format.** New sets become legal but **do not push older sets out.** At the time of
writing, **Mirrodin and Eighth Edition (2003) to the present.**

**The banned list:** cards from legal sets **not legal to play with**, usually chosen because they're too
powerful, **but a card can be banned for other reasons as well.**

> **Historically, the Modern banned list has been adjusted about once per year, and has caused some of
> the most impactful changes to the format.**

**What the large pool produces:**

- **Much more powerful, and much faster.** **There are many strategies that can win on turn four against
  an unprepared opponent.**
- **Enormous diversity.** **Modern enthusiasts could probably produce a list of 50 or more competitive
  decks, with plenty of variation within each** — though **not necessarily all equally strong.**

### The strategic conclusion Duke draws

**This is the most valuable paragraph in the installment:**

> **In a format as fast, powerful, and unpredictable as Modern, it becomes valuable to focus on a
> proactive strategy.**

> **A reactive strategy would struggle because there are too many decks attacking from too many different
> angles — you can't be prepared to shut them all down at the same time.**

> **Instead, it's better to focus on enacting a powerful game plan of your own, and know that if you can
> succeed in doing so, you can win in spite of most things your opponent might throw at you.**

*(Example deck: Antonio Del Moral León's Blue-Red Splinter Twin. Duke walks the combo: cast **Deceiver
Exarch** at end of turn, untap, enchant it with **Splinter Twin**. The Exarch taps to make a copy of
itself, which untaps the original and repeats — **an unlimited number of hasty tokens, killing from any
life total at a moment's notice.**)*

---

## Legacy

**An Eternal format — all sets from the entire history of Magic are legal.** Like Modern, **a somewhat
extensive banned list.**

### The card pool, illustrated

> **Consider that in Standard, if you wanted a one-mana spell that dealt damage directly to your
> opponent, you wouldn't have many options beyond Wild Slash, Spark Jolt, and Collateral Damage.**

> **In Legacy, just to name a few, you'd have: Burst Lightning, Chain Lightning, Firebolt, Galvanic
> Blast, Lava Dart, Lava Spike, Lightning Bolt, Pillar of Flame, Rift Bolt, Seal of Fire, Shard Volley,
> Shock, Tarfire, and Assault // Battery** — in addition to everything legal in Standard.

> **When I play Legacy, even as a Magic player of 20 years, I still encounter cards I've never seen
> before.**

### The two defining cards

**1. Library manipulation — Brainstorm, Ponder, Preordain.**

> Much of what's possible in Legacy is also possible in Modern, **but one main difference is the excellent
> library manipulation available in Legacy.**

**Two effects:**

- **(A) Increases the consistency of blue decks** — particularly important for combination decks.
- **(B) Multiplies your in-game options exponentially.** **When you play with Brainstorms you see a
  larger portion of your deck than you otherwise would, and you have more flexibility and control over
  your draws.**

**2. Force of Will — and free spells generally.**

> **Where library manipulation multiplies your own in-game options, free spells multiply the number of
> actions your opponent might take against you at any given time.**

> **The combination of these two factors makes Legacy's gameplay much more complicated and challenging
> than smaller formats.**

**And the deckbuilding consequence:**

> **Free spells, and free counterspells especially, increase the efficiency and speed of the format. They
> make it difficult to play with expensive spells, and force Legacy decks to be sleek, simple, and
> low-to-the-ground.**

*(Example deck: Yuta Takahashi's White-Blue Miracles — heavy library manipulation via **Brainstorm**,
**Ponder**, and **Sensei's Divining Top** to utilize **Counterbalance** and miracle cards.)*

---

## Vintage

**Also Eternal, but where Legacy has an extensive banned list, in Vintage no cards are banned for power
level reasons.** Some are banned for other reasons, and **some are restricted to a maximum of one copy.**

**Duke notes it isn't played at Grand Prix or Pro Tour level partly because the best decks use cards
that are difficult to obtain.**

> **Vintage is as close to "anything goes" as you can find. The decks are outrageously powerful, with
> wins on the second — or even the first — turn being quite possible.**

**What it has that Legacy doesn't:**

- **Fast artifact mana** — **Black Lotus**, **Mox Sapphire**, and many others.
- **Extremely powerful card drawing** — **Ancestral Recall**, **Wheel of Fortune**.

**His advice on approaching the Eternal formats:**

> **It can be difficult to dive straight into the Eternal formats, so I recommend trying Modern first in
> order to bridge the gap between Standard and Legacy or Vintage.**

> **However, once they get there, many players fall in love with Legacy and Vintage because they're such
> deep, interesting, and rewarding formats to play.**

---

## The close

> **The best way to become a complete player is to cross-train in as many different formats as possible.
> Playing Modern or Legacy might teach you lessons that you can use to improve your game in Standard or
> Block Constructed.**

## Notes for use

- **⚠️ This is the most dated installment in the course.** It was written in April 2015. Standard has
  rotated many times; Modern's legality window and banned list have both moved substantially; **Splinter
  Twin was banned in January 2016**, months after publication; Block Constructed no longer exists as a
  supported format; and formats Duke doesn't mention (Pioneer, Commander as a competitive format, Alchemy,
  Historic, Explorer) have since become significant. **Treat every specific card, deck, and legality claim
  here as historical.**
- **What survives completely is the framework.** The five variables — speed, power, balance, diversity,
  complexity — and the observation that **format size drives all of them** remain the right way to
  approach any format you haven't played, including ones invented since.
- **The Modern proactivity argument is the durable strategic content**, and it generalizes: the more
  diverse and unpredictable a format, the worse reactive decks perform, because you cannot hold answers
  for every angle at once. That's the same argument Duke makes in installment 08 about facing ten unknown
  opponents, scaled up to a format-level claim.
- **The two-mechanism analysis of Legacy is genuinely insightful** and worth keeping even though the
  format has moved: cantrips multiply *your* options, free spells multiply *their* options, and the
  interaction of the two is what makes a format complex rather than merely powerful. That's a portable
  way to assess any format's difficulty.
- **The "small formats have irreplaceable key cards" point** connects directly to installment 26's
  treatment of key cards in mulligan decisions, and to installment 41 on choosing a deck.
- **Cross-reference:** installment 42 (The Metagame) is the natural follow-on; installment 41 (Choosing
  Your Deck) applies these variables to a decision; installment 30 covers the Limited formats this
  installment excludes.
