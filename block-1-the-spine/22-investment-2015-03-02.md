# Investment

**Reid Duke — Level One, March 2, 2015**

---

## The concept

**Magic can be a fast and brutal game.** Typically you want your cards to have as big an impact as early
as they possibly can. **However, some of the game's most powerful cards involve investment — spending
resources with the promise of a payoff later.**

## Present discounted value

**Duke grounds it in economics.** One of the most important things he took from studying economics in
college was **present discounted value**. After pages of equations and hours of mind-numbing lessons, he
learned what his teachers could have said in one sentence:

> **Money in your pocket today is worth more than the same amount of money tomorrow.**

**Why:** with money today you can invest it, or buy something that makes you happy — say a pack of Magic
cards. **With the same money tomorrow, you'll have one less day of investment, or one less day of
enjoying your pack.**

**Applied to Magic:**

> **The same effect early in the game — for example, putting a 2/2 creature into play — is worth much
> more than the promise of getting the same effect later.**

**Two reasons:**

1. **Advantages beget advantages.** Just as you can invest money, you can use your 2/2 toward winning.
   **Things snowball, and it's harder to come back from behind than to stay ahead once you get an early
   advantage.**
2. **The game might end first.** There's the risk the game is decided, **or over**, before you get to
   make full use of your slower effects.

> **All in all, being quick to impact the game is a valuable quality of a card, while being slow to
> impact the game is a very real drawback.**

**The balancing caveat:**

> This is not to say you should blow all your resources right away, **or that you should spend all your
> money the second you finish reading this article.** However, it does mean you should **recognize the
> cost of waiting, and strike the right balance between forcing the action and being patient.**

## Investment and tempo

> **Investment, as a concept in Magic, falls under the umbrella of tempo.**

**Recall that tempo is board presence** — getting ahead of your opponent, or at least not falling behind.

> **So cards that require an investment instead of having an immediate impact are bad for tempo, giving
> your opponent a window to pull ahead of you.**

**Your goal becomes to drag the game out long enough for the investment to pay off, at which point it
might help you turn the tables.**

**The saving grace:**

> **Investments can sometimes be mana efficient.** Spending your mana in the most effective way possible
> is another important aspect of tempo, and sometimes an investment accomplishes that.

**Some cards require you to invest mana early, then have a free impact every turn after.** They put you
at a tempo disadvantage on the turn you cast them, **but if the game goes long enough they can pay off
in a big way.**

## The case study: the Sieges

**Fate Reforged's Sieges** — Citadel Siege, Monastery Siege, Palace Siege, Outpost Siege, Frontier Siege
— are **perfect examples of cards that require investment.** Small or no impact the turn you cast them,
**tremendous advantage as the game goes long.**

**Duke works Outpost Siege, Khans mode.** For most decks it's comparable to **drawing an extra card
every turn.**

- **Long game:** you might draw ten extra cards — **an advantage so big you almost can't lose.**
- **Quick game:** you might get zero, one, or two — **not worth the four mana you spent.**

> **This is the nature of investment, and what makes it such a challenging concept.**

### The two questions to ask

**How good is it? That depends entirely on the nature of tempo in your deck, format, or matchup.**

> **"How long is the game likely to go?"** and **"What's the cost of spending four mana on turn four?"**

> **In other words, it's just like an investment in real life. How much are you spending and how much do
> you stand to make?**

**Worked both ways:**

**If turn four is important in your matchup, the cost is too high.** Against a deck with **Goblin
Rabblemaster**, turn four is a point where **you're likely to fall hopelessly behind if you can't put an
effective blocker onto the battlefield right away.**

**If your opponent is a control deck not attacking your life total**, you can feel more comfortable
**taking a turn off from impacting the board.**

**And the payoff side moves with it:**

> The Goblin Rabblemaster deck is trying to end the game quickly, **so win or lose, Outpost Siege won't
> be in play for as many turns and its payoff will be lower.** The control deck wants the game to go
> long, **so perhaps six or eight extra cards isn't unrealistic.**

**Note that both variables move together** — against fast decks the cost is higher *and* the payoff is
lower, which is why investments are so matchup-polarized.

**The general rule:**

> **Investments will be better in slower matchups and slower formats.** Outpost Siege, Citadel Siege,
> and Palace Siege are bombs in Sealed Deck. **More specifically, they depend on exactly what else you
> could or should be doing with your mana at that point in the game.**

## Investment in everyday cards

**The concept applies to basically any Magic card, not just obvious ones.**

- **Elvish Mystic, Abzan Banner, and even lands** — you spend resources in early turns **in exchange for
  more mana in later turns.**
- **Creatures, when attacking** — a creature is **an investment that begins to pay off the following
  turn.** Which is why **creatures with haste, creatures with enters-the-battlefield abilities, and
  planeswalkers are sometimes preferable**, for their ability to impact the game immediately.
- **Spells split both ways.** **Wild Slash** and **End Hostilities** have their full impact immediately.
  **Divination is an investment** — it might take a couple of turns to play the fresh cards you draw
  into, **but you'll have gained card advantage when all's said and done.**

## Rebound

**An old mechanic returning in Dragons of Tarkir.** If a spell with rebound resolves, **you get its
effect twice — once immediately and once the following turn.**

> **Rebound is a healthy, balanced way to make an investment.** The fact that it has **some immediate
> impact** prevents you from falling too far behind on tempo, **and the investment pays itself off in
> full within a relatively short period of time.**

## The preview: Profound Journey

**On its own merits** it provides card advantage, **returning two of your permanents from the graveyard
to the battlefield.**

**The deckbuilding angle Duke is more excited about:** building around it to maximize your chances of
returning something powerful.

**Where it belongs.** Although white, **its effect seems at home in the Sultai colors, which are very
concerned with the graveyard.** His first instinct is an **Abzan graveyard deck**, using green and black
to fill the graveyard quickly — colors that also have **some of the most powerful creatures in Standard
you'd want to return directly to play.**

**Its distinguishing feature:** compared to similar cards, **it can return noncreature permanents.**
Perfect in a **Whip of Erebos** deck, letting you return the Whip if it dies or gets milled. **And there
are powerful planeswalkers around.**

### The weakness, analyzed through investment

> We focused today on investment, and **the fact that you have to wait a turn for the rebound takes away
> a little of the card's value.** In particular, **when you're talking about a seven-mana spell, you
> really want it to swing the game in your favor immediately, even if you're on the brink of losing.**

**The mitigation — and this is the sharp bit:**

> One way to mitigate the cost of the investment is to **return a permanent with a large immediate
> impact.**

**The anti-pattern:** you *could* return **Atarka, World Render**, **but now you're making an investment
in both the rebound of Profound Journey and in waiting a turn to attack with your creature.** You'll be
in great shape if the game lasts a few more turns, **but we don't want to count on that.**

**Stacking two investments on top of each other is the error.**

**The right targets:** **Ashen Rider**, **Hornet Queen**, or even **Siege Rhino** or **Doomwake Giant** —
creatures with an immediate impact that **help you survive to rebound Profound Journey.** Planeswalkers
are also great.

> **Permanents with immediate impact on the board will help you reap the rewards of your investment as
> quickly as possible.**

### The non-obvious interaction

> On the rebound, **you can return something that may have died after you cast Profound Journey the
> first time.**

So if you can **chump block with a creature**, or have a sacrifice outlet like **Butcher of the Horde**,
**you can wind up returning the same card twice.**

> This makes it **doubly difficult for your opponent to attack you while Profound Journey is exiled to
> rebound.** If your opponent is going to kill your Hornet Queen in combat, **they had better be sure
> they're ending the game as well.**

**Duke's summary on rebound:**

> **Evaluating spells with rebound requires an understanding of investment, since getting an effect next
> turn is less valuable than getting it immediately.** However, **casting a spell with rebound is an
> investment you can typically feel safe making**, since you get at least some immediate impact and
> realize the full rewards within a fairly small period.

## Notes for use

- **Present discounted value is the right mental model** and it's genuinely transferable: an effect
  delayed is an effect discounted, and the discount rate is set by how fast the format is. That single
  idea explains why the same card is a bomb in Sealed and unplayable in Modern.
- **The two questions are the practical test:** how long will this game go, and what does spending this
  mana now cost me? Run both before slotting any slow card.
- **The variables move together, which is the underrated point.** Against fast decks an investment costs
  more *and* returns less. It's not a linear tradeoff, it's a compounding one, which is why investment
  cards are so sharply matchup-dependent rather than mildly so.
- **"Don't stack two investments" is the most concrete lesson** — the Atarka example. A slow payoff whose
  payoff is itself slow is usually a trap, and it's an easy error because each half looks powerful.
- **This is Duke's version of a concept Flores credits to Team CMU.** Flores's own "Investment" article,
  described in `block-0-orientation/04-shoulders-of-giants-2013-08-06.md` (entry #4), draws the line
  between self-replacing filtering (Brainstorm) and non-replacing options (Mystical Tutor). Duke's
  treatment is broader — any delayed effect — but the shared root is that timing is part of a card's
  cost. Alan Comer's Turbo Xerox in Block 3 is the deckbuilding consequence.
- **Cross-reference:** tempo (installment 05), the early/late stage split (installment 06), and lands
  that enter tapped (installments 02 and 13) are all investment arguments in other clothes.
