# Tempo & Card Advantage: A Delicate Balance

**Reid Duke — Level One, November 17, 2014**

---

## The problem statement

Of all the concepts in Level One, **tempo and card advantage have the most power to directly
influence the outcome of a game.** They're the most talked-about in Magic theory and draw the most
interest.

Duke's framing of why this installment exists: it seems strange that by now no one has cracked the
code. **No player, writer, or theorist has produced simple, all-encompassing guidelines for managing
these two resources.**

**Part of the problem is that they frequently compete.** You sacrifice tempo to gain card advantage,
or sacrifice card advantage (or card quality) to gain tempo. **These tradeoffs stem from either how
you build your deck or how you play the games.**

> **There's no equivalency formula between the two resources that holds in every situation.** It's
> impossible to say "this much tempo is a fair trade for this much card advantage." And it's
> impossible to say one is more important, since falling too far behind in either loses the game.

**So the best you can do** is identify, in a given game, how the values of tempo and card advantage
are changing, and where to focus your efforts.

## The two stages

Duke's organizing device, which he cheerfully admits is uncreatively named.

### The early stage

**Both players have hands full of spells but limited mana.** It's a race to get ahead and develop the
board. **This is the stage where substantial tempo advantage is possible, and the rewards can be very
great.**

### The late stage

**The dust has settled.** Players have played out the lands and spells from their opening hands.
Either one player has emerged ahead, or the game stayed even and slowed down. **There's more mana and
less to do with it. Tempo advantage becomes much harder to gain.**

### Why the split is the whole challenge

**The length and importance of the two stages vary wildly.**

- **Two slow White-Blue Control decks:** the early stage might be a tiny window where one player
  lands a quick planeswalker for a minor advantage.
- **A hyper-aggressive weenie deck:** the game might be decided before it ever reaches the late
  stage.

**Identifying which stage you're in, and playing accordingly, is what ensures you're making the best
use of your cards.**

## The early stage

There's a chance of getting out ahead. **Come out fast, unbalance your opponent, push a tempo
advantage, and you can earn an easy win.** Most often that means quickly getting ahead on board with
creatures and planeswalkers.

**Two ways an early tempo advantage pays:**

**1. Finish the game outright**, if your deck is fast enough. Often the best case is simply killing
your opponent before they get to cast all their spells.

**2. Convert it into a late-stage advantage of another form.** Life total is the easy example.

### The red aggro illustration

Red aggro decks come out fast, often deploying several cheap creatures before the opponent casts a
spell. They gain early tempo and convert it to damage. Eventually the slower, more powerful deck
might stabilize with **End Hostilities** or **Elspeth, Sun's Champion**, and the game shifts to the
late stage.

**All things equal, the slower and more powerful deck has the late-stage advantage.** But if the aggro
deck converted its early tempo into 14 or 16 damage, **it can easily win in the late stage with a
simple burn spell or two.**

> **An aggro deck's ability to win in the late stage is called reach.**

### Converting tempo into card advantage

Despite the emphasis on tradeoffs, **an early-stage tempo advantage can be converted into card
advantage under the right circumstances.**

The perfect example is a planeswalker like **Xenagos, the Reveler**. Land him quickly and every turn
he survives uncontested he adds a free 2/2 Satyr — card advantage. Eventually the game reaches the
late stage and maybe they kill him, **but much of the damage is already done and you're left with
extra creatures on the battlefield.**

Some creatures do this too: **Goblin Rabblemaster** making tokens, or **Jeskai Elder** providing the
virtual card advantage of perfecting your hand.

### Whose stage is it?

**If one deck is faster**, it has the early-stage advantage. Its goal is to jump ahead on tempo and
press. **The slower deck's goal is to keep pace, minimize damage** (to life total or any other aspect
of the game) **and get to the late stage as quickly as possible.**

**If the decks are comparable in speed, the player who goes first has the early-stage advantage.**
Duke references the previous installment: mirroring your opponent's plays when you're on the draw is
typically a losing battle. **You start behind and remain behind all game.**

> **To "break serve," the player on the draw has to trade resources, slow the game down, and try to
> reach the late stage on even footing**, or as close to it as possible.

## The late stage

**The early stage is defined by the bottleneck of mana:** many spells, few lands. **The late stage
begins when the bottleneck gives way** and a great expanse of open space and available mana is left.

> **The limiting factor shifts away from mana (and tempo) and toward card advantage and other
> resources like life total.**

**Now you're glad to have card-advantage spells** like **Dig Through Time** or **Bitter Revelation**.
Since the early stage is about developing your board, **err on the side of playing creatures and
removal first and leave card drawing for the late stage**, when things have slowed and you have the
leisure.

**In the late stage most decks have spent their hands and play off the top of their libraries.** So it
becomes crucial to squeeze every bit of value out of each spell, **even if it means slowing yourself
down to do so.**

**The scry example makes it concrete:** in the early stage you'd scry a land or a cheap play to the
top. **In the late stage you're looking for long-run power, even at the cost of slowing down.**

## Permission spells, by stage

Permission has a very interesting relationship with tempo. **If you're ahead or even on board, it's
great for preventing you from falling behind. When you're already behind, it can't do much to get you
back into the game.**

**The structural problem:** permission spells are **reactive cards usable only in the proper window.**
You have to decide *in advance* to hold up mana. Only if you left mana available *and* your opponent
cast a spell do you get the option.

### Early stage

**If you commit three mana to holding up Dissolve, you should often be willing to counter whatever
they cast, even if it wasn't what you hoped to counter.** Otherwise the three mana is wasted, and
**failing to use your mana in the early turns is a sure-fire way to fall behind on tempo.**

**The counterplay, from the other side.** If you suspect they've left mana open for Dissolve, **cast
one of the weaker spells from your hand.** That gives them the choice of falling behind on tempo or
spending the Dissolve, letting your more powerful spells through later.

**Better still:** if you have an activated ability like **Rakshasa Deathdealer**, or a creature with
outlast or monstrosity, **use your mana on that instead of casting a spell and never give them a
chance to use their mana at all.**

### Late stage

**Here you have the option to be patient and wait for their best spell.** There are very few other
ways to defend against a **Crater's Claws** for X=10, so you might save Dissolve for that even if it
means letting a **Heir of the Wilds** resolve first.

### The worked example: Ben Stark's Jeskai, Pro Tour Khans of Tarkir

Duke uses one decklist to show both modes in the same deck. **Nullify** maindeck and **Disdainful
Stroke** in the sideboard are both two-mana permission spells serving opposite roles.

**Nullify is maindeck** because one of Jeskai's weaknesses is having little to do on turn two. Stark's
plan is to play his second land and pass, **intending to Nullify absolutely anything the opponent
casts.** It's an **early-stage tempo play that keeps him from falling behind.**

**Disdainful Stroke is exactly the opposite.** Jeskai normally spends several cards undoing a **Siege
Rhino** or an **Elspeth, Sun's Champion**. Disdainful Stroke comes in against slow decks as a
**late-stage play** where Stark can patiently wait to answer the single most important card.

## Bounce spells, by stage

### Early stage

**In the early stage, with the mana bottleneck on, bouncing a creature can feel like making your
opponent skip their turn.**

**The Khans-era sequence Duke gives:** you're on the play, cast a two-drop then a three-drop. Their
first play is a three-drop and you **Force Away** it. **You've already dealt noticeable damage and
have two creatures to their zero.** They probably have a hand full of great cards, but with only three
lands they're unlikely to cast more than one spell a turn. With a good follow-up you're in great
position.

**The guideline when pushing an early tempo advantage:**

> **Cast your bounce spell the first time you don't have something else to spend your mana on.**

Play out your creatures first, then cast Force Away on the first turn you have no creature to cast,
or when you can cast two spells in one turn. **Bouncing one of their first creatures has a
long-lasting effect, because it takes them that much longer to empty their hand and reach the late
stage.**

**The anti-pattern:** don't cast Force Away on the *last* creature they cast. **If you bounce a
creature and they have nothing else to do with their mana next turn but recast it, you enjoyed a
tempo advantage for a brief snapshot in time.** That can be okay if you gain something immediate,
like a big attack, but generally it's not how bounce should be used late.

### Late stage

**Bounce typically results in card disadvantage**, since you spend a card without destroying one of
theirs. **In the late stage that can translate directly into a loss.** So the challenge becomes
finding a window to cast it *without* suffering card disadvantage.

**The clean windows:**

- **Bounce a creature with an Aura attached.** The creature comes back; **the Aura is gone forever.**
- **Bounce a token creature.** It's **lost to the opponent forever.**

**Absent those, patience is key.** Three patient uses:

1. **Save your own creature from removal.** They cast **Rite of the Serpent** on your **Jeskai
   Windscout**; you respond by bouncing it to your own hand. **A minor tempo setback, but you traded
   Force Away for a powerful removal spell out of their hand.**
2. **Beat a combat trick.** They cast **Awaken the Bear**; you Force Away the creature. **An even
   trade in cards, and probably a lot of damage saved.**
3. **Break up a double block.** They double-block your **Glacial Stalker**; you bounce one blocker so
   the Stalker survives and eats the other.

> **In the late stage, avoiding card disadvantage is far more important than tempo concerns.**

## The close

**In the early stage, cash in your cards quickly if you smell blood and think you can earn a quick
win. In the late stage, things slow down and it takes patience and creativity to find the best use
for your cards.**

Some games are decided long before the late stage arrives, and then card advantage hardly matters.
**But if you can't see when the texture of a game begins to change, you won't make the best use of
your resources, and you'll be disadvantaged against a player who's more patient and more mindful of
card advantage.**

> **Magic is about adapting.** The best way to play your cards changes with circumstances. Identify
> what's important in a game and play accordingly.

## Notes for use

- **This is the installment that makes tempo actionable**, because it supplies the missing variable:
  *when*. Tempo is worth a lot early and little late, and the whole skill is noticing the handoff.
- **The single most useful heuristic here:** cast your bounce spell the first turn you have nothing
  else to spend mana on, and never on the last creature they cast. That one rule fixes most bounce
  misplay.
- **The Ben Stark decklist is the best teaching object in the course so far**, because it shows one
  deck deliberately carrying two cards of the same type tuned to opposite stages. That's the concrete
  version of "identify the stage."
- **"Reach" is defined here** and it's the term you need for the Philosophy of Fire material in Block
  2: an aggro deck's ability to convert an early tempo lead into a late-stage kill.
- **The "break serve" note connects directly to installment 21, Play or Draw.**
- **Compare to Eric Taylor** (`block-2-core-theory-paired/04-its-about-time-1997-08-11.md`). Taylor
  argued the same tradeoff seventeen years earlier via Memory Lapse versus Power Sink: the same card
  is correct or catastrophic depending on which resource your deck accumulates. Duke's contribution
  is that the answer also changes *within a single game*, not only between decks.
