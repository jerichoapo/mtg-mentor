# Playing From Ahead, Playing From Behind

**Reid Duke — Level One, March 30, 2015**

---

## The premise, inverted

**Duke opens with a flattering observation and then turns it over.**

> Most people play their best in close games. I see it in myself, in other people, in Magic, in
> virtually every aspect of life. **Fierce competition and a hard fight bring out the best in us.** It's
> part of what's allowed our species to survive on Earth for so long. **A heart-warming testament to the
> human spirit. Right?**
>
> **…Right?**

> **Well, put a different way, most people play their worst when games aren't close.** They either lose
> hope or give up completely when things look bad. **They get overconfident and careless when everything
> seems to be going their way.**

> So sure, trying your hardest when things are close might be good for a caveman fighting sabertooth
> tigers, **but it's quite a self-destructive quality in a Magic player.**

**The two mandates:**

> **When you're behind, you need to give yourself every chance to steal a win. When you're ahead, you
> need to stay tight and close the door on your opponent.**

**And the arithmetic of why it matters:**

> **If you can come back to win one out of a hundred games that look hopeless, you'll be a better Magic
> player for it. If you allow one out of every hundred would-be wins to slip through your fingers, your
> chances of winning tournaments will plummet.**

# Playing from behind

## The Craig Jones moment

Duke uses one of the most famous moments in Pro Tour history.

**The situation:** Craig Jones, on an aggressive deck with burn, is in a losing position against Olivier
Ruel, **the game slipping away with each passing turn.** He draws **Char**.

**The commentary split, which is the whole lesson in miniature:** one commentator wants **the obvious
play — take out the opponent's best creature.** The other advocates **the bold play: fire Char directly
at Ruel.**

**Jones chose the latter, and the rest is history.**

## Playing to your outs

**Why the obvious play was wrong:**

> Looking at that momentary snapshot, burning the opponent's creature seems like a good play.
> **However, Craig Jones was very far behind, and making that play would've still left him in a losing
> position.** It would have **postponed the inevitable for a few turns, but he would've lost the game
> regardless.**

**Duke's line for it:**

> **When Captain Blackbeard is making you walk the plank, do you really care how long the plank is?**

**What Jones did instead:** thought through his possible paths to victory and **decided this moment was
do or die.**

> **His odds were slim, but slim odds are always better than a guaranteed loss.**

He cast Char at the opponent, **then drew Lightning Helix for the last 3 points.**

**The definition:**

> **An out is a card or situation that you need to show itself in order for you to win.**

> When you're losing, **your focus needs to change from simply making obvious, sensible plays to
> considering your outs.** Figure out what your outs are, **and play assuming that one of them will come
> up.**

> If you successfully draw the card you need, **you want to be in the best possible situation to take
> advantage and turn a loss into a win.** If you don't, **the outcome will be the same as it would have
> been otherwise.**

### The four-path proof

**Duke lays out the decision formally.** Suppose the only way back is drawing **End Hostilities** next
turn.

| | You play… | You draw End Hostilities? | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | normally | **no** | **You lose.** |
| **2** | **as if you'll definitely draw it** — hold a creature in hand, chump block the 4/4 | **no** | **You lose.** |
| **3** | normally | **yes** | Board evens up, but **you're at a disadvantage with a low life total. Uncertain, likely a loss.** |
| **4** | **as if you'll definitely draw it** | **yes** | Board evens up **with a higher life total and an extra creature in hand. Uncertain, likely a win.** |

**The conclusion:**

> **It costs you nothing to play to your out.** If it doesn't come up, you lose either way. **If it does
> come up, you've given yourself the best chance to turn a loss into a win.**

> **It doesn't matter whether you have four copies of End Hostilities or only one. If it's your only
> path to victory, then that's the path you should start walking.**

## Complicate the game

**Playing to outs is simple in concept, harder in practice**, because it's rare that there's exactly one
route to victory. **With more than one out you have to ask what the odds of each are and how to
maximize both at once.**

**And sometimes you can't even enumerate them:**

> Often the situation is so complex that you can't see every possibility and can't clearly define your
> outs right away. In this case **you can neither start down a specific path nor continue to play
> normally, and yet you have to soldier on.**

> **In such a situation, my advice is to try to complicate the game state as much as possible.**

**Why:**

> **Complicated situations mean more choices and more possibilities.** When you have zero creatures
> facing two creatures, **there's no room for interpretation — you're losing.** When you have two
> creatures against four, **you might have some space to maneuver.**

- **Perhaps your opponent makes an overly aggressive attack** and opens a draw like **Become Immense**
  or **Rush of Battle** as an out.
- **Perhaps they play too defensively** and give you time to set up **Icy Blast** or **Flying Crane
  Technique**.

**The behavioral rule that follows:**

> **When I'm ahead, or when the game is close, I tend to trade creatures at most opportunities. When I'm
> losing, I might be willing to take some extra damage in order to maintain a complicated board state
> where unexpected things can happen.**

## Allow your opponent to make mistakes

> **We normally think of an out as a card we need to draw, but it can take other forms. Sometimes your
> out will be your opponent making a particular decision.**

**The example:** you're behind on board but hold **Trumpet Blast**.

- **Option A:** pass, chump block with a bunch of creatures, survive one more turn before inevitably
  losing.
- **Option B:** make an aggressive attack. **If they block a certain way, you're dead to the counter
  attack. But if they block the way you want, you cast Trumpet Blast, make favorable trades, and the
  game continues.**

## Don't concede

> **Don't concede a game of Magic until you're at 0 life.**

**Two reasons:** sometimes you draw a card or combination **you simply hadn't thought of**, and sometimes
**your opponent makes an odd decision that opens the door.**

> **Even on the final turn, when you're defenseless against a lethal attack, you should make your
> opponent turn their creatures sideways.** They might decide to play around **Kill Shot**; they might
> **miscount their damage and hold back**; anything can happen.

> **The only reason to concede is to save time or conceal information for a future game.** As long as
> you're still playing, you always have some chance of winning. **If you concede, you have no chance.**

# Playing from ahead

> **Losing hope when you're behind is a sure-fire way to lose a game. Overconfidence when you're winning
> can be just as deadly.**

Duke frames this half as the mirror image of the first, **though not quite as simple as that sounds.**

## Think from your opponent's perspective

> **When you're winning, your focus should turn to the ways you can lose.** Just as you consider your
> outs when behind, **when ahead you should consider the possible cards or events that would reverse the
> situation.**

> **The best way is to put yourself in your opponent's shoes and consider what they're probably hoping
> for. Remember that if your opponent has not yet conceded, they're still trying to beat you. You need
> to stay one step ahead.**

**Applied:** if their plan is to get in a couple more points and then draw a burn spell, **go out of
your way to protect your life total.**

> **When you're winning you have resources to spare, so you can sometimes afford to make
> otherwise-unfavorable trades and blocks if it means closing a door on one of your opponent's paths to
> victory.**

**And the exact inversion of playing to outs:**

> If the only way they come back is a board sweeper like **End Hostilities**, **the situation is the
> exact reverse of playing to your outs. You're protecting yourself from your opponent's outs.**

> **You'll win either way if they fail to draw it, so you might as well pretend they're going to.** Hold
> creatures in hand, and maximize your chances of coming back if they're fortunate enough to draw it.

## Simplify things

> **A simple game is a controlled game and a predictable game. In such a case, your advantages are more
> likely to remain advantages.**

**The magnification argument, which is the sharp bit:**

> Consider how **an advantage is magnified when both players have fewer resources.**

**Say your opponent mulligans to five.** On turn five you'll have **eleven cards to their nine** — not a
huge difference. **But if you've traded for their first creature in combat, used removal on the second,
and cast a Thoughtseize**, now you have **eight cards to their six.**

> **Your advantage has become both easier to identify and far more important to the outcome of the
> game.**

## Don't worry about slowrolling

**Duke spends real space on this, and it's a culture argument as much as a strategy one.**

**The definition:** to **slowroll** means **holding the game-winning card longer than you need to instead
of casting it right away.** At 3 life, if he draws Lightning Strike, he either shows it immediately or
he's slowrolling.

**The complaint:** slowrolling makes the loss more painful for the person receiving it, and some players
get angry about it.

> **I've always found this to be a strange and harmful attitude within the Magic community.**

**His position, carefully bounded:**

> **Of course it's absolutely unacceptable to slowroll with the intention of causing your opponent mental
> anguish. However, this is virtually never what actually happens.** For every ten thousand players who
> get mad about being slowrolled, **I'd be surprised if even one of their opponents had done it
> maliciously.**

**And the actual strategic reason it matters:**

> **It's a horrible idea to ever rush the final turn of a game.**

> Okay, I drew Lightning Strike and you're at 3. **I'm likely to win, but what might go wrong? Could you
> have a permission spell? Could you have a way to gain life? Have I forgotten that you have a Windswept
> Heath in play with two Coursers of Kruphix?**

> **It's completely unacceptable to blunder when you're so close to a win.** Always take your time and
> execute your plays in the best way you can, **particularly on the deciding turn.**

**His advice:**

> **Erase "slowroll" from your vocabulary, and don't worry about how much time you take on the final
> turn — I never do.** If your opponent gets angry, that's their fault, not yours. **When you play a
> tournament your goal is to play the game the best you can and give yourself the best possible chance
> of winning.** So long as you treat your opponent with respect and aren't rude in other aspects of the
> game, **they're not justified in getting mad.**

**The courtesy option he offers:** sometimes, feeling especially considerate, he'll say something like
**"I think I might be able to win, but I'm still thinking it through."** **Even that is going above and
beyond the call of duty.**

## Stay focused

Duke closes by calling back to installment 01:

> In the very first article I wrote for Level One, I named **focus** as the most important skill a Magic
> player can cultivate. **Focus is what allows you to stay present, minimize mistakes, and see what you
> need to do in order to win.**

> **Being far ahead or far behind are circumstances that often cause players to lose their focus.
> Recognize if this is happening to you, and do everything you can to correct it.**

## Notes for use

- **The four-path table is the single cleanest piece of reasoning in the course.** It proves that playing
  to your outs is *free*: in the branches where the out doesn't arrive you lose either way, so the only
  branches that differ are the ones where it does. Internalize the structure, not just the conclusion —
  it applies to any decision under a low-probability requirement.
- **"Slim odds are always better than a guaranteed loss"** is the line, and the Craig Jones example is
  the canonical demonstration. Note that the *obvious* play was the losing one, which is why this needs
  teaching at all.
- **The complicate/simplify pair is the most actionable behavioral rule here.** Behind: keep the board
  messy, take extra damage to preserve variance. Ahead: trade everything, shrink the game. Most players
  do the opposite of both, trading off when behind because trades feel safe.
- **The mulligan-magnification argument is subtle and correct:** a fixed card advantage is worth more as
  the absolute resource count falls. That's an argument for trading resources when ahead that has nothing
  to do with board position.
- **The slowroll section is really about not blundering on the last turn**, and the cultural argument is
  in service of that. The list of things to check — permission, lifegain, an untapped fetchland with two
  Coursers — is a good habit to keep.
- **Cross-reference:** focus is from installment 01. "Complicating the board" is the inverse of the
  simplification instinct in installment 18's chump-blocking discussion. Protecting against the
  opponent's outs is line-up theory (installment 23) run from the winning side. Inevitability
  (installment 16) is what tells you which side of this installment you're on.
