# Choosing Your Deck

**Reid Duke — Level One, June 8, 2015**

---

## The question he's asked most

> **What deck should I play next weekend?**

> **And just as often as I'm asked, I'm left wishing I could offer some kind of simple and helpful
> answer.**

> **Choosing a deck is a very complicated and challenging issue. It's the one we Magic players agonize
> over the most — the one that keeps us awake at night.**

---

## Copying decks vs. building your own

**On netdecking:**

> **Among certain groups there can be a bit of a stigma against "netdecking."** Among a small circle of
> friends it's okay to establish whatever guidelines you like. **However, once you venture into
> tournament Magic, netdecking is the norm and you should be prepared to face, if not embrace, it.**

> **If anything, building your own deck from scratch can be considered admirable, but netdecking should
> not be looked down upon.**

### The case for copying

**1. It's tried and true**, with proven tournament results.

**2. You inherit thousands of players' work.** This is the argument worth keeping:

> A countless number of players play Mono-Red and **incorporate their own instincts, experiences, and
> opinions into their decklists.** They play the land count they feel is correct, structure the deck the
> way they think gives the best chance, and sideboard what they predict will help.

> **There's no guarantee any one individual is exactly correct, but over time the better versions win
> more, and their characteristics get incorporated into future versions. It's Charles Darwin's theory of
> survival of the fittest.**

**3. It frees your preparation time.** **If you've already decided on mono-red, now you can put all your
efforts toward tuning your list and practicing your gameplay.**

### The case for building

> **Building a deck from scratch is an extremely challenging endeavor. Even among world-class Magic
> players there are only a select few who even attempt it, let alone ones that could be considered
> masters.**

> **There's a common saying that for every one good deck idea there are nine bad ones.** It takes
> creativity, intuition, persistence, hard work, and discipline to succeed against those odds.
> **Generally speaking, you'll be at a disadvantage when you try to build your own deck.**

**But two reasons to do it anyway:**

1. **When you succeed, you gain a great advantage over the field.** **You'll have a deck no one is
   expecting and no one has prepared for. They won't know what cards you're likely to throw at them.**
2. **More importantly, deckbuilding is one of the best ways to learn and grow as a player.** **When
   you're in charge of everything from deckbuilding to gameplay, you develop a deeper understanding of
   exactly what's going on in your games, and what's causing you to win and lose.**

> **Don't feel obligated to always build your own decks, especially when preparation time is limited.
> However, deckbuilding is tremendously helpful and rewarding, and I encourage everyone to give it a try
> at least once in a while.**

---

# The six selection criteria

## 1. A deck that's good for the metagame

> **If you can accurately predict the metagame, you might be able to build, choose, or adjust a deck to
> make yourself razor-sharp against the decks you're most likely to face.**

> When Blue-Black Control is the most popular deck, **it's great to enter a tournament ready to jump for
> joy whenever your opponent leads off with a first-turn Temple of Deceit.**

> **Sometimes, when you're specifically gunning for certain decks and those decks aren't specifically
> gunning for you, you can turn it into a huge advantage.**

**The three-layer uncertainty warning:**

> **There's a lot of uncertainty in predicting the metagame. Often the field is going to be quite
> diverse. Often you're going to be mistaken, at least a little, about what other players will show up
> with. Even when you can predict things to a tee, there's still no way to predict exactly which
> individuals you're going to be paired against.**

## 2. A deck with raw power

**Duke's definition is unusually precise:**

> **"Power" is a bit of a nebulous term. It refers to how good a deck or card is without the context of
> format and metagame.**

> **I like to think about the power level of a deck as: "how well would this deck perform, relative to my
> other options, against an infinitely large sample of different opponents playing different decks from
> different formats?"**

**The tradeoff with criterion 1:**

> **Green Devotion has a lot of raw power. It's fast and explosive and, left to its own devices, it will
> outclass most things other players will be doing. However, it can be a bad metagame choice if too many
> people are playing End Hostilities, Crux of Fate, and Perilous Vault.**

> **Sometimes the circumstances are right to make a metagame choice, and other times it's best to default
> to a deck with a lot of raw power. Finding an intersection of both is ideal.**

## 3. A proactive game plan

> **With how diverse and uncertain Magic tournaments can be, there's a huge advantage to focusing on your
> own game plan and knowing that if you can succeed in executing it, you're likely to win.**

> **It's more difficult to have answers for everything your opponents might have. The longer the games
> go, the more you open yourself up to things going wrong.**

**And the important extension:**

> **I advocate having a proactive game plan even in decks that are otherwise slower and more
> controlling.** **Dragonlord Ojutai** is a great example of a proactive card for a control deck. **It
> helps you leverage a small advantage into something insurmountable, and can close a game fast once
> you're ready.**

## 4. Inevitability

> **You have inevitability if you're virtually guaranteed to win the game if things drag on
> indefinitely.**

> **When you have inevitability, all you need to do is survive in order to win. Often, defending yourself
> is easier than actually having to put your opponent away.**

**The danger, and why criteria 3 and 4 belong together:**

> **The most dangerous scenario is to believe you have inevitability when you really don't. This is why a
> proactive game plan is important, because you want the ability to go for a win if something unexpected
> happens.**

**His example of a deck with both:** **Esper Dragons** — successful because **it has inevitability over
most of the format's popular decks**, and **despite being slower, it has a healthy proactive game plan in
its ability to simply dominate the board with Dragons.**

## 5. The nature of sideboarded games

> **A common pitfall of new Constructed players is undervaluing the sideboard.**

> **It's easy to get excited about a deck like Mono-Red because its win rate is so high in game one,
> before the sideboard gets involved. However, you don't really see the full picture until you begin to
> play games where the Abzan and Esper players are allowed to stock their decks full of Drown in Sorrows
> and Pharika's Cures.**

> **How your deck fares in sideboarded games can be a major selling point.**

**Why midrange wins here:**

> **Midrange decks historically benefit the most from sideboarding. Because they're well rounded, they
> have maximum flexibility in what they can sideboard, and yet are difficult for opponents to attack.**

> **In general, the more extreme your strategy — suicidal aggro, control decks that lack proactive game
> plans, and linear strategies — the easier you will be to sideboard against.**

## 6. Do you enjoy the deck?

> **This may seem like a strange factor to bring up in a dedicated strategy column, but I absolutely
> believe it can impact your tournament results.**

**The positive loop:**

> **When you're having fun, and when you're passionate about a deck, you're going to perform better.**
> With a deck you enjoy **it's easier to focus, and natural to spend more time thinking about it.
> Consequently you're more likely to have an inspired idea for a maindeck change or a new sideboard
> card.**

**The negative loop:**

> **When you don't enjoy playing your deck, you can feel helpless and stagnant. You won't look for ways
> to improve it, and might make bad mulligan or gameplay decisions because you simply don't have faith
> that the deck is capable of something more.**

---

# The single most important factor

> **That brings us to what I believe to be the single most important factor in choosing a deck: how well
> can you play the deck?**

**The questions to ask:** Have you played it in previous tournaments? Have you done well? How much have
you practiced? Have you played similar decks in other formats?

> **How well you pilot your deck of choice is usually more important than which deck you choose. I'd
> rather play a weaker deck well than play a stronger deck poorly.**

## Sticking with the same deck

> **I believe that one of the biggest secrets to success in Constructed Magic is sticking with the same
> deck through multiple tournaments.**

**What accumulates:**

> **You begin to learn the deck more intimately; you play better; you develop a deeper understanding of
> why you're winning and losing games; you sideboard better; you become better qualified to change cards
> and improve the deck.**

**Why tournaments specifically, not just practice:**

> **You can accomplish a lot of this through practicing at home, but there's really no substitute for
> testing yourself in tournament conditions against a variety of opponents.**

> **As important as predicting the metagame is, it's also crucial to prepare for the unexpected. When you
> master your deck you'll be faster to adapt, and will know what to do even when you play against a deck
> you've never faced before.**

**And the learning argument again:**

> **Just like building a deck from scratch, truly mastering a deck is one of the best learning
> opportunities you can find. Developing a very deep understanding of a single deck will teach you
> lessons you'll carry to other decks and formats.**

**Duke's own testimony:**

> **Over the years, the periods of greatest success and greatest growth in my career were the ones where
> I was able to stick with the same deck through a long series of tournaments.**

> **I encourage you, unless you find a fatal flaw in the deck you've been playing, to err on the side of
> sticking to one rather than switching back and forth.**

## The close

> **You don't need to have the absolute best deck in order to do well in a tournament. However, you do
> need to choose one that will allow you to realize your potential as a player.**

> **Think hard about your decision, and then dive in with both feet and don't look back.**

## Notes for use

- **The headline conclusion is the one people ignore:** piloting matters more than selection, and
  **sticking with one deck across multiple tournaments is the biggest available edge.** Every incentive
  in Magic culture — new sets, new lists, metagame chatter — pushes the other way.
- **The six criteria are genuinely in tension and Duke doesn't pretend otherwise.** Metagaming and raw
  power trade off directly; proactivity and inevitability are complements that guard each other's failure
  mode. Use them as a checklist, not a formula.
- **His definition of raw power is worth stealing:** how would this deck do against an infinite sample of
  opponents playing anything from any format? That strips out metagame contingency and isolates what's
  actually robust.
- **"Believing you have inevitability when you don't" is the most dangerous error named here**, and it's
  why he wants a proactive plan even in control decks. Cross-reference installment 16, where the
  30-cards-and-100-mana test is the way to check the belief.
- **The enjoyment criterion is not soft.** The mechanism is concrete: enjoyment → attention → tuning ideas
  and better in-game decisions. Disliking your deck produces fatalistic mulligans and stagnant lists.
- **The survival-of-the-fittest argument for netdecking** is the cleanest defense of copying lists in this
  library, and it reframes a stock list as accumulated distributed testing rather than as laziness.
- **Cross-reference:** installment 42 (The Metagame) is the prerequisite for criterion 1; installments 39
  and 40 for criterion 5; installment 16 for inevitability; installments 08 and 38 for the proactivity
  argument; installment 10 for why midrange sideboards best; installment 45 (Becoming a Better Player) for
  the broader learning theme.
