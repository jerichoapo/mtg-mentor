# The Basics of Booster Draft

**Reid Duke — Level One, August 3, 2015**

---

## Why draft matters

> **Booster Draft is one of the most played Magic formats among casual players, as well as at every level
> of competition.** With Sealed Deck it's the Limited format of choice for most tournaments, including
> Pro Tours, Preliminary PTQs, and Grand Prix.

**Its dual appeal:** popular among occasional players **who want to avoid the pressures of maintaining a
collection and a Constructed deck**, and equally popular among serious players **who enjoy the depth and
challenge of the format.**

> **Learning Booster Draft should be a priority for any aspiring Magic player.**

**The mechanics:** like Sealed with the added step of drafting. **Usually eight players in a circle each
open a pack, pick one card, and pass; repeated across three packs.** At the end of a Magic Origins draft
players have **42 cards, not counting basics, from which to build a 40-card deck.**

## Why draft decks are better than sealed decks

> **In Sealed you open six packs and in Draft only three. Nevertheless, the total number of cards you see
> in a draft is much higher.** Since you choose which cards you think will be best, **in a way your total
> pool is much larger in a draft.**

> **You can expect Draft decks to be slightly better, more powerful, faster, and most importantly more
> focused than in Sealed.** You can choose all your cards to work toward one concerted strategy.

> **Things will never be as extreme as Constructed, but your Draft decks might fit the mold of an aggro
> deck or a control deck depending on your choices.**

## Synergy

> **Synergy means cards working especially well together.**

**Three scales of it:**

1. **Between two cards** (sometimes called a combo) — **Act of Treason** letting you sacrifice your
   opponent's creature to a **Nantuko Husk**.
2. **One card with the rest of your deck** — **Shaman of the Pack** in a deck with lots of Elves.
3. **Built into the deck as a whole**, which Duke calls perhaps the best case. His example: **lots of
   cheap creatures with renown and lots of combat tricks to keep attacking.** The tricks force through
   the renown creatures, **and the cheap creatures give ample opportunities to use the tricks
   effectively.**

## Archetypes

> **An archetype is a recurring strategy with numerous possible variations. The shell or pattern remains
> recognizable despite individual cards changing.**

**The Magic Origins example: Blue-Red Artifacts.** **Ghirapur Gearcrafter** goes perfectly in it, **but
you can build the deck without it.** Same for **Artificer's Epiphany**, **Chief of the Foundry**,
**Whirler Rogue**.

> **The recognizable traits of every Blue-Red Artifacts deck will be flying creatures, removal spells,
> card advantage, and taking advantage of artifact synergies. If you draft it well, these features will
> exist no matter what individual cards you wind up with.**

**On preparation and repetition:**

> **In a perfect world you'd start a draft with one or more archetypes in mind. Having a vision for the
> way you want your deck to end up is extremely helpful.**

> The first time you draft Blue-Red Artifacts or Black-Red Sacrifice **you might be muddling your way
> through. However, once you've drafted an archetype five or six times, you'll begin to learn its
> strengths and common pitfalls, and be able to draft accordingly.**

## Balance beats synergy

**The corrective, and it's the important one:**

> **A desire for synergy, or to draft a particular archetype, should never overshadow the basic
> principles of Magic.** Foremost in your mind should be **your mana base, mana curve, creature count,
> removal spells, and card advantage.**

> **A well-built deck with a smooth mana curve and a good mix of creatures and spells will function just
> fine even if it doesn't have much synergy. A deck with a lot of synergy but a bad mana base and bad
> curve will still be clunky and awkward.**

> **Synergy is good, but it should not come at the expense of what's most important.**

**And the same for raw power:**

> **Avaricious Dragon** might have no special synergy with a Blue-Red Artifacts deck. **However, you'd
> better believe I'm picking it over Ghirapur Gearcrafter, Artificer's Epiphany, or virtually any other
> card you can imagine. After all, this is still Limited Magic and a bomb is a bomb.**

## Choosing your colors

**Duke lays out three strategies in ascending sophistication, and explicitly endorses the simplest for
beginners.**

### 1. Decide before you open

> **A completely valid strategy that I often recommend as best for beginners. There's no shame in it.**
> In fact, employed at higher levels of competition, **so long as you did a good job drafting, building,
> and playing, you'd have a perfectly fine chance to do well.**

**The legal warning he attaches:**

> **It's illegal to coordinate with the drafters sitting next to you by telling them what colors you
> intend to draft or asking them what colors they intend to draft.**

### 2. Let your first picks decide

> **Draft the best cards out of your first couple of packs regardless of color, then draft whatever
> colors your first couple of picks were.**

**The benefit:** you get the upside of opening a premium card in any color **but don't have to worry
about wavering. You won't waste picks on a color that doesn't wind up in your deck.**

### 3. Stay open

> **Advanced drafters will be adaptable and let what happens in the draft determine their colors.
> Sometimes they won't completely decide until halfway through.**

**Two distinct benefits:**

**Capitalizing on your own openings.** Open **Avaricious Dragon** in pack two: **having pre-decided your
colors, it's completely up to chance whether you can use it. Having stayed open, you jump on it.**

**Finding an open color — the more important one.** Duke inverts the example: *he* opens the Dragon, has
already committed to white-green, and passes it to you.

> **If you're able to correctly identify that I'm not drafting red, then you have the ability to get
> that Dragon along with every other good red card that I'm going to pass you over the course of the
> draft.**

## What the other drafters are doing

> **What makes Booster Draft so interesting is that it's a dynamic process with lots of moving parts.
> What you do affects the drafters around you, and what they do in turn affects you.**

**The cooperative logic, despite being opponents:**

> **Ideally you wouldn't fight for colors with the drafters next to you, because doing so would wind up
> making both of your decks worse.** If you knew for a fact that the players on either side were drafting
> blue-red and black-red, **it would probably be in your best interest to draft white-green**, because
> you'd get passed all the best white and green cards they didn't want.

### Signals

> **When neighboring drafters learn what one another are drafting, they're able to cooperate and will
> both wind up with better decks. This is accomplished through signaling.**

**What signaling is not:**

> **Signaling doesn't mean winking, shaking your leg under the table, or tipping your baseball cap three
> times. It certainly doesn't mean talking about the draft as it's happening. All of these things are
> strictly illegal.**

> **Instead, signaling comes in the form of what cards you pass, receive, do not pass, and do not receive
> from your neighbors.**

**The canonical example:** **Fiery Impulse** is a premium card any red drafter would want. **Seeing one
pick six means five players passing to you declined it.** For each of them it probably means one of two
things:

- **(A) They picked a stronger red card** — possible for the player who opened the pack if there was a
  bomb rare.
- **(B) They aren't drafting red** — more accurately, **they weren't drafting red at the time they made
  the pick.**

**Both directions:**

> **A late Fiery Impulse is a signal that red might be open**, meaning if you draft red you have a higher
> chance of being passed strong cards as the draft goes on. **Likewise, passing a Fiery Impulse sends a
> signal downstream.** If you pass one, **it shouldn't come as a great surprise if you later find out
> that a player you were passing to drafted red.**

**The warning against overreacting, which is the practical takeaway:**

> **You also shouldn't go crazy trying to adapt your draft to signals. Sometimes, by the time you know
> what your neighbors are drafting, it's so late that all the best cards are already gone anyway. The
> more you switch colors, the more picks you waste and the lower the chances of piecing together an
> effective deck.**

### The three-factor rule

> **Choose your colors and archetype based on a combination of three factors: what you want to draft,
> what cards you've already drafted, and what you think might be open.**

> **The weights change based on circumstances and as the draft progresses. Generally speaking, though, I
> don't think any one is more important than the other two.**

## Counterdrafting

> **Do not counterdraft.**

**Why he raises it at all:**

> I'm hesitant to mention counterdrafting in Level One because it's a relatively unimportant aspect of
> the format. **However, I do so in the hope of helping readers avoid a common pitfall of beginning
> drafters, which is that once they learn the concept, they put far too much emphasis on it.**

**The definition:** **spending a pick simply to take a card out of the draft so no one else can have
it.**

**His fair-minded framing, then the verdict:**

> **Counterdrafting isn't mean or unsportsmanlike; it's simply a strategic decision that's part of the
> game. The problem is that it's typically a bad strategic decision.**

**The math:**

> In an eight-player draft you have **seven potential opponents.** A positive addition to your own deck
> **has a relatively great impact. Hurting one of your seven opponents' decks won't greatly increase your
> chances of winning any given round.**

**The three-condition chain for regret:**

> For me to regret passing you the Avaricious Dragon: **first, we have to get paired** — typically you
> play three of your seven opponents. **Second, you have to draw your Dragon. Third, you have to beat me
> because you had it instead of whatever the next-best card would've been.**

> **This is possible, but it's not particularly likely.**

**The rule:**

> **It's correct to counterdraft if and only if there's nothing you want from the pack.** Prioritize your
> own deck first and make counterdrafting only an afterthought. **If there's a time for it, it's usually
> much later in the pack, once most of the good cards have dried up.**

## The close

> **Booster Draft is as hard as you want to make it. Concepts like signaling and counterdrafting are deep
> and complex, but the fundamentals are relatively easy. You can go far by just focusing on things like
> balance, mana curve, and card advantage.**

---

## Notes for use

- **"Booster Draft is as hard as you want to make it" is the honest summary.** The floor — pick good
  cards, build a balanced deck — is reachable immediately. The ceiling involves reading seven other
  people in real time.
- **The three color strategies are a genuine progression, and Duke's endorsement of the simplest is
  worth taking at face value.** Pre-deciding colors is not a beginner's crutch that costs you equity; it
  costs you the upside of open colors while eliminating the much more common error of wasted picks from
  wavering.
- **Balance over synergy, and power over both.** The ordering is: a sound curve and mana base first, raw
  bombs second, synergy third. Most drafters invert this and build cute decks that stumble.
- **The signal example is worth memorizing in its exact form:** a premium card arriving late means the
  players upstream weren't in that color *at the time they picked*. That last qualifier is what keeps
  signals honest — they're historical, not current.
- **The counterdraft math is the best-argued small point in the installment.** Three independent
  conditions must all hold for a counterdraft to have paid off. Compare against the certain value of
  improving your own deck.
