# Inevitability

**Reid Duke — Level One, December 8, 2014**

> What are you waiting for?

---

## The framing question

Duke opens on the question itself: applying for your dream job, asking out the person you like — **what
are you waiting for?** It's a good question in a game of Magic too, because **nothing's going to happen
if you just wait around.**

Except that isn't quite true. **Something is going to happen.** In life, Benjamin Franklin named it:
nothing is certain except death and taxes. **In Magic, the question is more complicated.**

## What inevitability is

> **What's going to happen if the game drags on indefinitely?** In most cases one player or the other
> will have an advantage as the game goes long. **If one player is virtually guaranteed to win the game
> if it goes long enough, we say that player has inevitability.**

**Duke then sharpens his own earlier definition.** In the control installment he had written: *you have
inevitability if your deck is powerful enough that you'll win a very high portion of games that go
long.*

> **In truth, the concept is even more extreme than that.** I might have a powerful deck with lots of
> card advantage, **but if my late-game strategy does not utterly and unquestionably crush my
> opponent's, then I don't really have inevitability.**

### The test

> **If you were to let both players draw 30 cards and spot them 100 free mana every turn for the rest
> of the game, who would win?**

You'd probably expect the player with the more expensive and powerful cards, **and sometimes it will
be.** But Duke immediately complicates it:

- **What about the player with enough burn spells to kill you from 20 life?**
- **What if one player cast Scout the Borders and will therefore run out of cards before the
  opponent?**

> Things begin to look strange when you consider the extreme long term, **but these are important
> questions to ask.**

### Why it matters

**They all serve the larger question of what you are waiting for:**

> **I can have the patience of a saint if my waiting will eventually lead to a win. If I'm waiting
> around to die, I'll look for ways to speed up the game and end things before it gets to that point.**

**The consequence for both deckbuilding and in-game decisions:**

- **You have inevitability** → focus on defending yourself and simply seek to survive.
- **Your opponent has inevitability** → force the action and try to end the game.

## Achieving inevitability

**Inevitability is a late-stage issue**, concerned with the point where **tempo no longer matters** and
both players have ample mana and ample time to spend it.

> **More than that, think of it as a contest of a complete deck against a complete deck, instead of
> about whatever particular cards happened to be drawn early in the game.**

It's related to power and card advantage, **but it's also about ensuring your late-game plan trumps
your opponent's.** Two requirements:

1. **Your opponent must not be able to shut you down** — by killing your creatures and planeswalkers,
   for example.
2. **Your opponent must not be able to circumvent your defenses and kill you directly** — as with a
   flurry of burn spells.

### Win conditions

**Control decks tend to play a small number of win conditions, but very reliable and very resilient
ones. They're chosen in order to grant inevitability.**

**The comparison Duke uses:**

> **A single Pearl Lake Ancient will beat out an unlimited number of Hero's Downfalls, Murderous Cuts,
> and Crackling Dooms if given enough time.**

**Whereas** if your U/B Control deck's win condition were **Riverwheel Aerialists**, a single removal
spell could shut you down. **And even at two, four, or twenty copies, it remains within the realm of
possibility that your opponent kills all of them and leaves you floundering.**

> **Pearl Lake Ancient provides inevitability in a way that other cards cannot.**

**The historical example.** A year prior, W/U Control was popular and traditional knowledge held that
**Elspeth, Sun's Champion** was the best win condition — huge immediate board impact, fast clock, good
in virtually every matchup.

**The problem:** against a deck with ways to answer planeswalkers, **your two or three Elspeths could
die, and you'd no longer have an easy way to win no matter how much card advantage you accrued.**

**The fix:** control players turned to resilient win conditions, **Elixir of Immortality** or
**Ætherling**. Both were much slower to impact the game and **cards you never particularly wanted to
draw early.**

> **Nonetheless, the presence of a single copy of either meant having inevitability.** Survive
> indefinitely and you win by recycling your graveyard (Elixir) or attacking with an unkillable
> creature (Ætherling).

### Stopping your opponent's endgame

**Duke immediately checks his own claim.** Saying a single Elixir or Ætherling *provides* inevitability
is rather silly, **because the question is relational — it's about how two decks match up.**

> **What if your opponent also has Ætherling? What if your opponent has something even more powerful?
> Perhaps something I'd never even thought of before?**

> **If your goal is for the game to go on indefinitely, you must have a plan for all eventualities.**

**The tool: permission.** Dissolve stops their Ætherling. It also stops **Crater's Claws for 20**, or
whatever spell they'd use to take your win condition away.

**The distinction Duke draws:** **Dissolve is a hard counter** — it counters virtually any spell under
virtually any circumstances. **Temur Charm is a permission spell but not a hard counter.**

> **I like to include at least a couple of hard counters in any blue control deck I build**, because
> they're great for locking up the late game and contributing to inevitability. **Sometimes their
> early-stage applications aren't great, but in the late stage, when you save them for the small number
> of cards that might give you trouble, they're invaluable.**

### Lifegain

**The second contributor.** Duke is careful to distinguish good lifegain from bad:

**Bad:** experienced players know a card like **Font of Vigor** is relatively weak, because it asks you
to spend mana and a card for no board impact. **That's both tempo disadvantage and card disadvantage.**

**Good:** **incidental lifegain**, a repeatable source built into the deck. His examples:

- **Nyx-Fleece Ram** as one of your cheap defensive creatures.
- **Dismal Backwaters** and **Radiant Fountain** in the mana base.

> **Once you take control of a game, gaining life is a great way to shut the door on your opponent.**

**And the reason it belongs in this installment specifically:**

> **Aggro decks go far out of their way to have reach** — a plan for finishing off a weakened opponent
> in the late stage, most often burn spells or unblockable creatures. **Taking away your opponent's
> reach is a prerequisite for achieving inevitability.** In many cases, lifegain does that job well.

## The consequences of inevitability

> **The player with inevitability wants the game to be long and the player without it wants the game to
> be short.** If you have it, you can feel secure waiting around. **If your opponent has it, you should
> be forcing the action.**

**The biggest consequence: you can focus your efforts entirely on defense.** All you need to do is sit
around and not die, **and you eventually win in the late stage, at your convenience.** So your deck
construction and gameplay might as well center on defense.

### The counterintuitive deckbuilding conclusion

Duke poses the obvious objection to Pearl Lake Ancient. **It costs seven mana and will be very slow to
defend you if the opponent has even a single removal spell.** So in a fast matchup, wouldn't
**Riverwheel Aerialists** be better simply for being cheaper? Wouldn't a five- or four-mana win
condition be better still?

**His answer is no, and the reasoning is the sharpest thing in the installment:**

> From a deck-construction standpoint, **Pearl Lake Ancient is a great win condition even against fast
> decks** — oddly enough, despite being a slow, inefficient card you'd never want to draw early.

**The key is slot economy.** One or two Pearl Lake Ancients provide inevitability. **To feel comfortable
winning a long game with less resilient creatures, you might need six or eight of them, which takes up
far more space in your deck.**

> **Against that super-fast red deck, I'd feel better having two Pearl Lake Ancients and four Drown in
> Sorrows rather than devoting six slots to other creatures in order to win the game.**

**The rule that follows:**

> **This is why it's common to see control decks playing a very small number of win conditions. Do the
> bare minimum you need in order to achieve inevitability. After that, focus the rest of your deck on
> early defense. Survive, slow the game down, and a win will eventually fall into your lap.**

## A masterpiece of inevitability: Ivan Floch, Pro Tour Magic 2015

**Floch won the Pro Tour by, in essence, doing nothing.**

**The deck:**

- **No creatures.**
- **Just three planeswalkers**, which could only damage the opponent in the slowest and most indirect
  ways.
- **One Elixir of Immortality.**

**The plan:** draw cards, gain life, recycle the graveyard with the Elixir. **Eventually run the
opponent out of cards, dole out a slow and painful death via two Mutavaults, or — much more often — the
opponent sees the writing on the wall and concedes.**

> **Floch took the concept of minimal win conditions to the extreme. He didn't even need Elspeth, Sun's
> Champion.** His deck consisted entirely of card drawing, defense, and that single Elixir.

> **Ivan Floch spent the entire tournament waiting.** After three days of competition, he'd outwaited
> all the other competitors, and was the winner.

**By constructing the deck that way, he put the pressure on his opponents to close out the game. Nobody
could.** Duke's closing line: they all fell victim to the inevitable. **Death. Taxes. And Elixir of
Immortality.**

> **Well, what are you waiting for?**

## Notes for use

- **The 30-cards-and-100-mana thought experiment is the practical tool.** It strips out tempo and draw
  luck and asks which *deck* beats which *deck*, which is exactly the question inevitability is about.
  Run it on any matchup before you decide whether to race or to durdle.
- **The slot-economy argument is the deep idea.** Resilience lets you win with fewer cards, and those
  freed slots go to defense. That's why control decks run one or two absurd finishers instead of six
  reasonable ones. It's a deckbuilding insight disguised as a card-evaluation one.
- **Duke's definition is looser than Zvi's, and he knows it.** Compare **Zvi's**, quoted in Flores:
  *a player has inevitability if and only if from the current position they will win a long game, and
  in a matchup if and only if they have it on turn one.* See principle 4 in
  `block-2-core-theory-paired/02-eight-core-principles-of-whos-the-beatdown-2012-03-16.md`. Zvi's is
  positional and can flip mid-game; Duke's is closer to a deck property. Both are useful; the Zvi
  version is more precise.
- **Inevitability is the missing variable in "Who's the Beatdown?"** Flores's three questions identify
  who *should* attack. Inevitability tells you who can afford to *wait*, which is the same answer
  arrived at from the other end. Read this installment directly against Block 2 file 01.
- **"Taking away reach is a prerequisite for inevitability"** is the cleanest link between the aggro
  and control halves of the course. Reach was defined in installment 08; this is its counter.
- **Floch's deck is the reductio** and worth remembering as the endpoint of the logic: if waiting wins,
  build a deck that does nothing else.
