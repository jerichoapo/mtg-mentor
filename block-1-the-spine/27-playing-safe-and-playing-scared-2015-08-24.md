# Playing Safe and Playing Scared

**Reid Duke — Level One, August 24, 2015**

---

## The distinction

**Duke frames this as an advanced-player problem.** You know the fundamentals and the common pitfalls
now. **As you become more advanced there's a fine line to walk.**

## The benefits of playing safe

> **Playing safe means avoiding risks, and taking the game down a path where catastrophe cannot strike
> you.**

**His three examples of it done right:**

> - "I'm not going to play a fourth creature, just in case my opponent casts **Languish** next turn."
> - "I saw **Hixus, Prison Warden** in game one, and those five untapped Plains look very suspicious.
>   I'll make a more conservative attack."
> - "I have this game locked up, but don't want to lose if my opponent draws a haste creature. I'll
>   leave my **Satyr Wayfinder** on defense."

> **Playing safe is an incredibly important skill when games are even, or when you're ahead.** Being a
> great Magic player means squeezing out every advantage, **and giving your opponent as few
> opportunities as possible to steal games away from you.**

**The margin argument:**

> When you're ahead, **there's a huge difference between a line that gives you a 90% chance to win and a
> line that gives you 80%.** If you play recklessly and leave doors open for comebacks, **your results
> will suffer in the long run.**

## The dangers of playing scared

> **But playing safe has a dangerous evil twin. Players will often think they're playing safe when in
> reality they're playing scared.**

**His three examples of it done wrong:**

> - "I'm not gonna block with my **Sylvan Caryatid**; what if my opponent has **Titan's Strength**?"
> - "I suspect my opponent has **Clash of Wills**. I'm not going to cast anything until they tap out!"
> - "If I hold this **Foundry Street Denizen** in hand, I'll have something to rebuild if my opponent
>   casts **End Hostilities**."

### Danger 1: losing to a card they never had

> **You can sometimes allow yourself to lose the game even if your opponent doesn't have the card you're
> worried about.**

> **It's good to think about the possibility of a permission spell, but how can you win if you don't
> cast your spells?**

> Your mono-red deck will inevitably be scared of Languish, **but if you hold back your creatures, you
> might instead lose to a Siege Rhino, or to an opponent with two spot removal spells.**

### Danger 2: extra draw steps

> **Another grave danger of playing scared is giving your opponent extra draw steps.**

**The false logic he names:**

> Players are prone to reasoning such as **"the only card I can lose to is Dragonlord Atarka, so I'll
> play around it."** Most games are far more complicated than that.

> **If you play conservatively and give your opponent a handful of extra draw steps, there's likely to
> be some turn of events you hadn't considered that could cause you to lose.**

## Duke's own example: walking the fine line

**The setup.** On Blue-Black Control, stabilized at a low life total against Red-Black Dragons. He
didn't want to lose to **Stormbreath Dragon** or **Kolaghan, the Storm's Fury**, **so he declined to
attack with Silumgar, the Drifting Death for a few turns** until he drew a second flying blocker.

**What happened:**

> My opponent didn't draw a haste dragon, **but what he did draw was a Thunderbreak Regent to block, and
> since I'd missed an attack, I was forced to give him one more draw step than I'd counted on.**
> Wouldn't you know it, **that final draw step was a burn spell, and I lost the game.**

**His verdict is honest and unresolved:**

> **Did I play safe, or did I play scared? It's hard to say.** But what I do know is that **there's
> tremendous danger in giving your opponent extra draw steps, even when you think you've planned for all
> eventualities.**

## How to play safe without playing scared

### Step 1: do you have the luxury?

> **Often this involves gauging how well the game is going for you, and how likely you are to win if it
> drags on.**

- **Is your opponent racing you in the air with Dragonlord Ojutai?** You probably can no longer afford
  to play around **Crux of Fate**.
- **Are you at one life against a burn deck?** You need to be aggressive and **give them as few draw
  steps as possible.**

### The general rule about control decks

**This is the counterintuitive and most useful passage in the installment:**

> **As a general rule, it's a bad idea to play too slowly against late-game control decks. Ironically,
> control decks are most often the ones playing the permission spells and board sweepers you'd normally
> want to play around.**

> **Unfortunately, in many cases, trying to mitigate the damage of an End Hostilities is a losing
> battle. It's playing scared, not playing safe.**

**The reasoning:**

> These control decks tend to be so powerful in the late game that **they're likely to win if they have
> a clean board state on turn five, whether or not you've held back creatures.** You might cast a
> Foundry Street Denizen and a Goblin Piledriver to rebuild, **but those cards will be virtually useless
> if your opponent follows up with Elspeth, Sun's Champion.**

> Of course this depends on particulars, and there are certainly times to hold back a creature or two.
> **However, when in doubt, I recommend trying to close the game as quickly as possible. The more you
> hold back, the more time your opponent has to deploy their powerful late-game cards.**

**The condition, stated positively:**

> **Playing safe is a good idea when the game is going well for you, and when you want it to be longer
> instead of shorter.**

### Steps 2–5: the procedure

**2. Think about the realistic ways things could go poorly** — not the worst case, the realistic ones.
> *"It would be bad if my opponent draws a removal spell for my blocker."*

**3. Think about what you can do to mitigate those risks.**
> *"I could hold back an extra creature."*

**4. Weigh the pros and cons.**
> *"If I hold back, I'll survive the next turn no matter what, but I'll have to give my opponent two
> extra draw steps. Plus, if they do remove my biggest blocker, I'll have to start chump blocking and
> might lose anyway…"*

**5. Act — but reevaluate often if anything changes.**

## Get to know your instincts

**Duke quotes Hall of Fame pro Zvi Mowshowitz**, and this is the most valuable passage in the
installment:

> **"…You need to get to know your instincts. It's not about good or bad instincts, it's about the
> biases that your instincts display. Watch to see which way you naturally lean. If you tend to focus on
> controlling the game all the time, then when the decision is close, choose the non-controlling path.
> If you tend to be hyper-aggressive, this is where you hold back. That helps protect you from
> yourself…"**

### Duke's own calibration

> A few years back I identified my own natural leaning toward being conservative. **It wasn't inherently
> a bad thing; I still felt I was playing well and that most of my decisions were sound. However, when I
> did make a mistake, it was much more likely to be because I was playing too conservatively.** In other
> words, **I was playing scared too often.**

**The fix he describes is deliberately small:**

> When I faced a close decision, I would say to myself, **"Reid, you have a tendency to play scared too
> often — is this one of those times?"**

> **It wasn't any kind of cataclysmic change in my play style, it was just a slight recalibration to a
> gauge that was a little off-center.** I learned that I should go for it when I smelled blood, and that
> I shouldn't pass up opportunities for advantage.

## When you're at risk of playing scared

**Three specific circumstances.**

### 1. When the texture of the game has shifted

> **Playing while you're ahead and playing while you're behind require two somewhat different thought
> processes, and it's not always easy to switch between them.**

**Both directions:**

- **Ahead, then slipping.** You've been in the safe-playing mentality, **but the game has turned and you
  may no longer have the luxury.**
- **Aggressive, then dominant.** On an aggro deck feeling pressure to end the game fast — **right in
  most situations, but circumstances arise where you have a firm hold and ought to start playing safe.**
  You get them low and hold a burn spell: **you might get over-excited and rush, when you should wait
  for a moment when they don't have mana open for a permission spell.**

### 2. When you move up a level of competition

**Duke says he knows this from experience.**

> Take a player who puts up great results at Friday Night Magic. They've played at the local store for
> months or years and are one of the best. **They know that if they carefully play a long game, leaving
> few openings, they'll beat most opponents via their edge in skill and experience.**

> When they move up to the RPTQ or Grand Prix level, **they'll sometimes have a hard time adjusting.**
> Now they're playing against players as good or better, **and the wins don't come as easily. If they're
> playing scared and failing to capitalize on opportunities, they'll be at a disadvantage against
> equally-matched opponents.**

### 3. When you respect your opponent too much

> When players face an opponent they consider particularly skilled, **they're at particular risk of
> playing scared.** For different people it might be a pro at a Grand Prix, the local all-star, or
> simply **the person who won last week's FNM.**

> They **give their opponent too much credit**: "Surely she has the Clash of Wills!" Or, "He's
> definitely going to cast Languish next turn!"

**Duke's correction, and it's the sharpest reasoning in the piece:**

> **The reality is that every Magic player is merely human** (unless they're making a new Air Bud movie
> that I don't know about). **A strong player is no more likely to have Languish in their top ten cards
> than any other player piloting the same deck.**

> **In fact, against strong opponents you ought to be slightly more willing to take risks, since you can
> expect them to play well and make fewer mistakes if the game drags on.**

## The close

> Walking the line is about making an honest evaluation of the game state and the other factors at play.
> **However, it's important to recognize that we're all human, and there are conditions where our
> judgment can be clouded.**

> **Play safe when it means tightening your hold on the game, but don't play scared when it means giving
> your opponent too much breathing room.**

## Notes for use

- **The single-sentence test:** playing safe is for when you want the game to be *longer*. If you'd
  rather the game were shorter, caution is costing you, not protecting you.
- **Zvi's instinct-bias advice is the most valuable thing in this installment and generalizes far beyond
  Magic.** The claim isn't that your instincts are wrong, it's that they're *biased in a consistent
  direction* — so on close calls, deliberately break toward your non-default. That's a cheap, permanent
  correction, and it requires only that you identify your lean.
- **The card-quality argument against deference is worth memorizing:** a strong opponent is no more
  likely to hold a specific card than a weak one playing the same deck. Skill affects *play*, not
  *draws*. And the corollary is counterintuitive but correct — **take more risks against better players,
  because long games favor the player who makes fewer mistakes.**
- **"Playing scared against control decks" is the most commonly-made version of this error.** Holding
  back against a sweeper feels like discipline; it usually just hands the control deck the turns it
  needs. Duke's rebuild example — the held creatures being useless against the follow-up Elspeth — is
  the argument in one line.
- **Note that Duke leaves his own example unresolved.** He genuinely doesn't know whether he played safe
  or scared. That honesty is the point: the line is real but not always locatable in the moment, which is
  why the bias-correction approach beats trying to compute it each time.
- **Cross-reference:** installment 24 covers the ahead/behind mentalities this builds on; the
  "texture shifted" risk is the transition between them. Installment 12's closing warning ("the worst
  thing you can do is let fear of a counterspell cause you to lose when your opponent doesn't have one")
  is the same lesson applied to permission. Installment 45 returns to self-assessment.
