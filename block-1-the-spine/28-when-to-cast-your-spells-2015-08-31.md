# When to Cast Your Spells

**Reid Duke — Level One, August 31, 2015**

---

## The second question

**The first question is whether to cast the spell this turn or wait.** Answer it, **and you're far from
done**, because another question follows immediately:

> **When is the best time to cast your spell?**

> **Timing can be everything.** With an instant you might cast it **at the end of your opponent's turn,
> during combat, in your main phase, in the upkeep, or at any of a dozen different points.**

**How this differs from sequencing:**

> Timing is one aspect of sequencing, and **information management is important to both.** However, with
> timing there's more to consider: **minimizing risk, maximizing opportunity, and making things as
> difficult and inconvenient as possible for your opponent.**

## The default

> **All things equal, it's best to wait until the last possible moment to cast your spells.**

**Three benefits:**

1. **You have the most information available when you cast.**
2. **You conceal information from your opponent for as long as possible.**
3. **Your mana remains untapped for longer**, which **multiplies the number of possibilities your
   opponent must consider, and might make them play more conservatively than they otherwise would.**

**The warning against following it blindly:**

> **It's very dangerous to become locked into the pattern of always waiting until the last moment. It's
> fine as a default option, but it's not always best.**

### The two reasons to cast early

> **1. If the spell can help you gather information to inform another important decision.**
>
> **2. If you fear a certain reaction from your opponent.** In particular, **look for times when all of
> your opponent's lands are tapped.**

> **There are plenty of spells you'll want to cast at any safe opportunity, even if it means casting an
> instant at sorcery speed.**

---

# The types of spells

## Combat tricks

**Normally cast at your final opportunity** — after blockers are declared, before damage. **But
sometimes deviate.**

### Case 1: wait, because you suspect they'll act

**Deviate when you strongly suspect your opponent has an action to take** — an activated ability on
board like **Anointer of Champions**, or just a gut feeling they have a trick.

> **It's always an advantage to act last, so if you're sure your opponent is going to do something, you
> might as well wait.**

**The mechanical reason this works:** the attacking (active) player normally acts first, **but if the
blocking player takes an action, the attacking player gets another chance.**

**The worked example.** Turn four, you attack with **Shambling Ghoul** and **Timberpack Wolf**, holding
**Titanic Growth**. Your opponent blocks each with a **Cleric of the Forward Order** and has **two Plains
untapped.**

**The tell:**

> **Why would your opponent block your 2/3 Shambling Ghoul with a 2/2 Cleric when they're still at a
> high life total? Something is fishy.**

**So you pass priority.** They cast **Mighty Leap** on the Cleric blocking Shambling Ghoul. **Now you
cast Titanic Growth on Shambling Ghoul, trumping the Mighty Leap and leaving your opponent with nothing
on the board.**

### Case 2: cast early, because a later decision depends on it

**The opposite deviation.** Your opponent attacks **Watercourser** into your **Timberpack Wolf** with
**three Islands untapped.** You want to block and cast Titanic Growth.

> **If you suspect Negate or Calculated Dismissal, cast Titanic Growth *before* you block.**

**Why:**

- **Block first, Growth countered** → **you lose your creature too.**
- **Growth first, countered** → **you decline to block**, and **taking two damage is a small price to
  pay for keeping your creature in play.**

## Removal spells

**Waiting gains information, and can even goad your opponent into investing mana or an Aura into a
creature before you kill it. But waiting can open the door to disaster.**

**The nightmare with damage-based removal:**

> You pass the turn, and after your opponent untaps you cast **Fiery Impulse** on their **Charging
> Griffin**. **They respond with Titanic Growth, and not only does the Griffin live, but you take 7
> damage to boot.**

**And it isn't only conditional removal:**

> Things can go wrong with "unconditional" removal like **Ultimate Price** as well. **Sometimes it's
> simply best to cast your removal right away, before your opponent can untap their mana**, so that a
> permission spell or a protection spell like **Feat of Resistance** can't ruin your day.

## Card drawing

**Duke widens the category:** anything **generally progressing your game plan** — **Dragonlord's
Prerogative**, **Dig Through Time**, but also **Collected Company**, **Abzan Charm** for two +1/+1
counters, or **Lightning Strike** at your opponent's face.

**Cast early when it enables another play:**

- **Jace's Ingenuity** to try to hit your land drop.
- **Collected Company** to try to hit a haste creature and attack.

**Cast early when it informs a decision:**

- **If you miss on Collected Company, will you need to hold back additional blockers?**
- **If you Dig Through Time and find Clash of Wills, perhaps you play an Island instead of an Opulent
  Palace this turn.**

**And the standing danger:** **if you don't want your instant countered, cast it when your opponent is
tapped out.**

## Creatures

**Everything cast at sorcery speed.**

> **If you cast a creature before combat and your opponent counters it, their mana will be tapped and
> you might have more flexibility during your attack.**

**Two upsides of a pre-combat Fleecemane Lion:**

1. **More options for using Dromoka's Command during combat.**
2. **It might make your opponent think you have Dromoka's Command when you really don't.**

> The theory behind timing each of these types is the same. **Think clearly about the consequences of the
> spell, and about the common ways your opponent might react to it.**

# The types of responses

## Permission spells

> **Perhaps the most natural fear when casting a spell is that it will somehow fail to resolve.**

**The principle:**

> **Don't risk other aspects of your game plan on your spell resolving if you don't have to.**

**The two-removal example.** You hold **Ultimate Price** and **Languish** against a **Jhessian Thief**.
You'd prefer to use Ultimate Price and save Languish, **but your top priority is not taking a hit from
the Thief.**

> **Maybe you should Price the Thief right away, and if your opponent counters it, you'll know to cast
> your Languish as a follow-up.**

**The inconvenience play, which is the best idea in this section.** Opponent at three life, you hold
**Lightning Strike**, unsure whether they have permission, **and you certainly don't want to give them
extra draw steps to find one.**

> **Cast Lightning Strike during your opponent's upkeep.** If they don't have the counter, **you win
> either way.** If they do, **you at least force them to spend mana on their own turn**, possibly opening
> a window to resolve another burn spell if you draw one.

## Pump spells

**The "minus/minus" case.** You want to **Bile Blight** their **Swordwise Centaur**, but fear **Aspect of
Hydra**. Own turn or theirs?

> **It can literally depend on how much devotion to green your opponent has.**

- **If Aspect represents exactly +2/+2**, the Centaur survives either way — **but if you wait until they
  attack, you only take damage from a 2/1 instead of a 3/2.**
- **If their devotion to green is five**, Aspect **greatly outclasses the Bile Blight, and you take
  additional damage if you wait.**

**And the upside case Duke adds:**

> So far we've been planning for the worst-case scenario, **but you should also think about the
> best-case scenario. Maybe if you wait on your removal spell, your opponent might jump the gun and cast
> their pump spell, allowing you to kill two birds with one stone.**

> **Timing your removal in the face of a possible pump spell is about minimizing risk, maximizing your
> potential for value, and making things inconvenient for your opponent.**

## Protection spells

> **Protection spells like Center Soul are similar to pump spells, except they don't necessarily come
> with the risk of dealing you extra damage. Alternatively, they're like permission spells that can only
> counter removal spells.**

**All the same concepts apply.** You can inconvenience your opponent by forcing them to spend mana on
their own turn, **but be careful about giving an extra draw step, or allowing all your plans to be undone
if they can save the creature.**

---

## Timing in the face of uncertainty

> Things would be relatively simple if you always knew how your opponent would react. **Things are much
> more complicated in practice, when you don't know if, when, and how your opponent is going to act.**

> **The best you can do is be present in the moment and consider all the possibilities. Sometimes you'll
> have to make an educated guess, and you'll guess wrong. That's okay.**

**The failure mode to actually avoid:**

> **What you really want to avoid is being blindsided by a protection spell because you simply hadn't
> considered the possibility that your removal spell might fail to kill its target.**

### The five questions

> 1. **Is there a risk of your spell failing?**
> 2. **How can you time your spell to make it most likely to succeed?**
> 3. **Can you gain relevant information by timing your spell in a particular way?**
> 4. **Can you gain additional value by timing your spell in a particular way?**
> 5. **How can you most inconvenience your opponent if they do have a response?**

**Duke's close:**

> **Deciding to cast your spell is only the first step. Take great care in timing your spells, in order
> to make them as effective as possible and minimize the risk that anything will go terribly wrong.**

## Notes for use

- **The five questions are the deliverable.** They're quick enough to run in real time and they cover
  the four distinct concerns — risk, information, value, and inconvenience — that timing trades between.
- **The default is "wait," and the two named exceptions are what make it usable:** cast early when the
  spell informs a later decision, or when their mana is down. Everything else in the installment is an
  instance of one of those two.
- **The Titanic Growth pair is the best-constructed teaching example in the course.** Same card, same
  format, opposite timings, and the variable is *what else you're risking on it resolving*. Wait when
  only the spell is at stake; act early when a second resource (your creature, your block) rides on it.
- **"Why would they make that block?" is the transferable read.** A block that makes no sense at face
  value is information. Duke's response — do nothing and let them commit first — costs nothing and wins
  the exchange.
- **The upkeep Lightning Strike is the most elegant single play here.** It converts an unknown into a
  no-lose proposition: either it resolves and you win, or you tax their mana on their own turn. Look for
  this shape generally — timings where both branches favor you.
- **The failure mode Duke names is the one to internalize:** not guessing wrong, but *never having
  considered* that the spell could fail. Guessing wrong is variance; not considering is a mistake.
- **Cross-reference:** installment 20 (Sequencing) covers the information half systematically;
  installment 04 supplies the priority mechanics that make the combat-trick cases work; installment 12
  (Permission Spells) covers playing around counters from both sides; installment 27 warns against
  letting this discipline curdle into playing scared.
