# Sideboarding in Limited

**Reid Duke — Level One, January 12, 2015**

---

## Why it matters more than people think

> **In some ways, Limited Magic feels like the purest form of the game.** Given its lower power level
> relative to Constructed, **games are often closer and less frequently decided by a single card,
> combination, or big turn.**

> **In games like these, small advantages really add up and it's crucial to use every tool at your
> disposal. An important one, and one that's often underappreciated, is the sideboard.**

---

## 1. Perfecting your answers

> **The most useful sideboard cards in Limited are your borderline removal spells.**

**Premium removal is maindeck anyway** — Murderous Cut, Suspension Field. **The sideboard candidates are
removal that's borderline** either **in power level** (expensive ones like **Bring Low** or **Rite of
the Serpent**) **or in specificity** (**Windstorm**, **Naturalize**).

**Two examples of a card flipping from marginal to excellent:**

**Barrage of Boulders** — maybe maindeck, maybe not. But if in game one your opponent casts **Hordeling
Outburst**, morphs a **Ponyback Brigade**, and adds more 1-toughness creatures, **suddenly it's a great
card you most certainly want.**

**Windstorm** — you wouldn't think of it as stellar in Khans Limited. **However, it kills a number of
extremely troublesome creatures, including what might be the single most powerful card in the format,
Wingmate Roc.**

### The core skill

> **The most important skill in Limited sideboarding is finding weaker cards that can trade for stronger
> cards.**

> **You won't always have as many rares as your opponents in Sealed and Draft, and if you want a chance
> to win even when the packs are stacked against you, you need to be able to use your more everyday tools
> effectively.**

**The examples:**

- **Despise** isn't effective taking an Alpine Grizzly or Sage-Eye Harrier. **But it might be one of
  your only ways to beat Sarkhan, the Dragonspeaker or Zurgo Helmsmasher.**
- **Disdainful Stroke, Cancel, and Stubborn Denial** offer a game plan for beating **the Duneblast or
  Crater's Claws that might otherwise destroy you.**

> **Any card that can answer a bomb, even in an inefficient or indirect way, is useful in Limited. They're
> the cards you'll most often look to during sideboarding.**

---

## 2. Slow matchups and fast matchups

**Why you build balanced in the first place:**

> **In Limited you typically want to build a balanced deck** — cards capable of playing both offense and
> defense, creatures spread across the curve. **One reason is that you simply don't know ahead of time
> how fast your opponents' decks will be.**

> **However, after sideboarding you have the ability to gear your deck more for the matchup you're
> facing.**

**Duke's own policy, and when he breaks it:**

> In Khans Limited I take great pains to **always include at least three creatures that cost less than
> three mana**, because against a fast deck I want to trade creatures early and avoid falling behind.

> **However, once I see my opponent is playing a slower deck and is incapable of beating me with a quick
> creature rush, I can throw my policy out the window. I might "get greedy" and sideboard out my cheap
> creatures for more expensive ones that will be better in a long game.**

**What he brings in against slow decks:** anything offering card advantage **even if a bit slow**, like
**Dutiful Return**, and **late-game answers like Cancel.**

**Against fast decks:** **bring in even more cheap cards to trade off early and keep pace.**

---

## 3. Matching up powers and toughnesses

**The most distinctive idea in the installment, and the most Limited-specific.**

> Under the right circumstances I might sideboard in something like **Firehoof Cavalry, which is a
> remarkably poor card by most metrics.**

> **Despite the general quality of the card, it might be just perfect for trading with the cheap
> creatures my opponent is attacking me with.** Against a deck full of **Mardu Hateblade**, **Leaping
> Master**, and **Mardu Skullhunter**, it will find something to trade with.

**And the line that makes the point:**

> **It might even trade with a Temur Charger. It doesn't matter that Temur Charger is an uncommon and
> Firehoof Cavalry is a common; it doesn't matter that Temur Charger is a strong card someone might take
> highly in a draft. All that matters is that Firehoof Cavalry has 1 power and Temur Charger has 1
> toughness.**

> **Matching the right powers against the right toughnesses will give you a huge advantage. It's
> something you should pay close attention to and be constantly looking for.**

**The worked defensive version.** On Abzan, undecided on your 40th card:

| If your opponent has… | You want… |
|---|---|
| **Mardu Skullhunter** | **Jeskai Student** |
| **Hooting Mandrills** | **Archers' Parapet** |
| **Snowhorn Rider** | **Rotting Mastodon** |

> **Whichever you choose for your maindeck, you should be ready to switch between them during
> sideboarding.**

**And the aggressive version:**

- **Want your 2/2s to attack through 3-toughness creatures?** → **Dragonscale Boon**.
- **Through 5-toughness creatures?** → **sideboard the Boon out for Awaken the Bear.**

## Sideboarding in Sealed Deck

> **Sideboarding is most important in Sealed Deck.** You don't choose what cards you're given, and
> you're likely to have a lot of strong options past what winds up in your deck. **Every card you don't
> use comprises your sideboard.**

**Everything above applies, and often.** But Sealed adds a possibility Draft doesn't have:

> **It's possible in Sealed Deck to completely shift colors or strategies during sideboarding.**

**The example:** you built an Abzan deck and a Jeskai deck close in power level. **A small factor such
as Jeskai having a Cancel might lead you to switch when your opponent has a Duneblast.**

**The side benefit, which is genuinely clever:**

> Imagine you win game two with a flurry of flying creatures. **Your opponent might sideboard in
> Windstorm for game three while you transform completely back into your Abzan deck with few or no
> flying creatures.**

> **Be aware of all your sideboarding options in Sealed, and be aware that your opponents might be up to
> the same tricks.**

---

## Sideboarding in Booster Draft

> **In Draft you won't have the same flexibility.** Drafting Abzan, **you almost certainly won't have
> enough cards to transform into Jeskai.** So your changes will typically be smaller, **but that doesn't
> mean they aren't important.**

**The extra layer unique to Draft:**

> **Sideboarding in Draft features an extra layer of complexity because you actually have to draft your
> sideboard cards.**

> **If there isn't a card you want to maindeck, you still might want to snatch up that Dutiful Return,
> Naturalize, or Barrage of Boulders just in case you need it later.**

> **Practice identifying possible sideboard cards and keeping track of what weaknesses your deck might
> have.** A slow Abzan deck with few fliers and little removal **might really need that Windstorm, and
> you should put a premium on drafting it just to have in your sideboard.**

---

## General tips

### Look through both every game

> **Always look through both your main deck and your sideboard between every game in a tournament match
> of Limited.** This might seem annoying and time consuming, **but it's a very good habit. You'll learn
> quickly, and you'll begin to spot things you never would have otherwise.**

**Both directions:**

- **Cutting a strong card that's dead in the matchup.** *"I'd rarely think of cutting **Smite the
  Monstrous** from my deck, but if I've played two long games without seeing any 4-toughness creatures,
  then it's correct to do so."*
- **Finding a gem you'd overlooked.** *"Hey, maybe I can use this **Molting Snakeskin** to save a
  creature from Duneblast."*

> **It doesn't cost you anything to think about your sideboard.** It will often result in a small
> advantage, **and at worst it will accelerate your learning process and train you to think outside the
> box.**

### Bring extra sleeves — three reasons

1. **It saves time during sideboarding**, helping you finish matches in time and **making life more
   enjoyable for you and your opponent.**
2. **It helps you remember what to consider.** *"Sleeve up that Windstorm and that Rakshasa's Secret
   before the tournament starts and you'll always remember to give them their due consideration."*
3. **It hides information.** **If you de-sleeve five cards and replace them with five, your opponent
   knows you sideboarded in five cards.** And imagine switching colors in Sealed without extra sleeves.

### Pretend to sideboard even when you don't

> **Even when you don't sideboard, you should at least pretend to. Shuffle a couple of sleeved cards into
> your deck and then pull them out.**

> **This way you keep your opponent guessing.** Did you bring in Disdainful Stroke? Did you cut the one
> you cast in game one? **If you make it obvious that you're not changing anything, you're only making
> things easier on your opponent.**

### Think about it in advance

> **When I play a Sealed tournament I spend the entire day between rounds analyzing my card pool and
> considering my options. I also ask my friends for their input.**

**Two things this surfaces:**

- **Sometimes I realize I made a mistake building my deck, and I wind up making a particular sideboard
  change in every match I play.**
- **Other times I find a good sideboard option for a situation I might not have been able to find on the
  fly during a match.**

> **Again, it doesn't cost you anything to think about your sideboard. Knowing your options ahead of time
> can only help you.**

## Notes for use

- **"Weaker cards that trade for stronger cards" is the governing skill**, and it's the answer to the
  complaint that your opponent opened better rares. You don't need a better bomb, you need an inefficient
  answer to theirs.
- **The power/toughness matching section is the most transferable idea here** and genuinely inverts card
  evaluation: rarity and general quality become irrelevant, and the only question is whether the numbers
  line up. Firehoof Cavalry trading with Temur Charger is the whole argument.
- **"Always look at your sideboard between games" is free.** Duke's point that it costs nothing and
  accelerates learning even when it changes nothing is correct, and the Smite the Monstrous example shows
  the direction people forget — *cutting* good cards that happen to be dead.
- **The fake sideboard and the extra sleeves are both information-management plays**, and they belong
  with installment 20 (Sequencing) and installment 25 (Going Through the Motions). Making it obvious you
  didn't board is a free gift to the opponent.
- **The Sealed color-switch, plus the Windstorm bait**, is the most advanced play in the installment:
  win game two with fliers, let them board in the answer, then present a deck with no fliers.
- **Cross-reference:** installment 30 (Sealed) and 31 (Draft) supply the format context; installments 39
  and 40 cover sideboarding and sideboard plans in Constructed; installment 23 (Line-Up Theory) is the
  in-game version of "the right tool for the right job," and Duke uses the same Sealed bomb framing there.
