# Midrange Decks

**Reid Duke — Level One, October 27, 2014**

> Pushing extremes is often a good way to learn, but finding balance is often the best way to play.

---

## The third category

Aggro wants to attack; control wants to defend. **Midrange decks feature similarities with both and
don't quite fit either mold.** They're built with both offense and defense in mind and **have the
ability to adapt to whatever situation they face.**

## Adaptability and versatility

**The case study: Ari Lax winning Pro Tour Khans of Tarkir with Abzan Midrange.** He navigated
thirteen rounds against control decks, fast creature decks, burn decks, opposing midrange decks, and
more.

**Three keys to his success:**

1. **The ability to play offense when he could.**
2. **A willingness to play defense when he needed to.**
3. **An excellent sideboard** that let him attack each opponent in the perfect way once he knew what
   he faced.

**What the deck looks like:** a healthy number of creatures, but also an emphasis on **answer cards,
mana development, and resilient noncreature threats.**

> **Its strategy is based neither on speed nor longevity, but on grinding small advantages and setting
> up a winning board state.**

### The design rule

> **The perfect card for a midrange deck is one that's good on either offense or defense.**

**What's absent is as telling as what's present.** Nothing like a **Firedrinker Satyr**, a card suited
only to attacking. Nothing like a **Jace's Ingenuity**, where you sacrifice substantial tempo as an
investment for a long game.

> **Midrange decks are adaptable in part because their individual cards are versatile — they have a
> range of uses for a range of possible scenarios.**

### Siege Rhino as the archetypal midrange card

**On offense:** capable of unloading a lot of damage fast, and it can win the game alone if
unanswered.

**On defense:** high toughness and lifegain make it excellent at catching you up from behind against
an opposing creature deck. **It comes down, stabilizes the board and your life total, buys time to
cast the rest of your spells, and when you're ready, it starts attacking.**

**The rest of the creature base follows the same logic.** Siege Rhino and **Wingmate Roc** are
hand-picked as game-enders that are also good on defense. **Beyond that, every creature has a
noncombat ability useful in a long game:**

- **Elvish Mystic** and **Sylvan Caryatid** can block in a pinch, but exist mainly as extra mana
  sources, giving the deck a speed boost to keep pace with aggro.
- **Courser of Kruphix** holds off weenies while providing long-run card advantage and a healthy bit
  of lifegain.

### Abzan Charm: versatility defined

**Three distinct modes is the definition of versatile**, and Duke points out the modes map onto the
three postures a midrange deck needs:

| Mode | Posture |
|---|---|
| Exile the opponent's creature | **Defensive** |
| Pump your creature for damage or to win a fight | **Offensive** |
| Draw extra cards | **An investment for the late game** |

> **You will never be disappointed to draw Abzan Charm because it's useful in nearly every imaginable
> situation.**

### Answers that are never dead

**Note how Lax filled the deck with answer cards without choosing any likely to be dead in a
particular matchup:**

- **Thoughtseize** — every deck plays nonland spells, and it's particularly excellent against
  noncreature spells in control decks.
- **Abzan Charm** — three modes, so you find the right one for the situation.
- **Hero's Downfall** and **Utter End** — even against control you can take out a planeswalker or a
  **Banishing Light** to free one of your own threats.
- **Elspeth, Sun's Champion** — destroys troublesome creatures like **Stormbreath Dragon**, and also
  serves as Abzan's most powerful win condition.

## The sideboard

**Impossible to discuss midrange without it, because flexibility is their greatest strength.** They
lack aggro's speed and control's late-game power, **but they excel at exploiting weaknesses and
finding the best approach to any circumstances.**

> **Midrange decks tend to shine after sideboarding, when they can hone themselves to perfection for a
> particular matchup.**

**The key asymmetry:** in the maindeck, avoiding cards that can be dead is tremendously helpful. **In
your sideboard there's no need to sacrifice power for versatility.**

**Because midrange is well balanced and doesn't lean heavily on any one thing, it can sideboard
virtually anything.**

**Symmetric effects.** Lax used a number, such as **End Hostilities**. Most creature decks shy away
from these, but **Lax knew that in the matchups where he'd board it in, his opponents would be so
decimated that he'd gladly sacrifice one or two of his own creatures as collateral.**

**Proactive threats, not only answers.** Because midrange plays creatures and can pressure a life
total, **it can also board in specific threats that give certain opponents trouble.**

**The Nissa illustration.** **Nissa, Worldwaker** is very effective against control. It would be
clumsy for one control deck to board her against another — attacking the life total isn't part of the
plan, so unless she takes the opponent from 20 to 0 alone she hasn't accomplished much. **But inside
an Abzan deck with Siege Rhinos, Wingmate Rocs, and other planeswalkers, Nissa represents a threat
that could easily be too much for an opposing control deck.**

### The two-fold sideboard advantage

> 1. **The flexibility of their strategy means they have access to all the best sideboard cards in
>    their colors.**
> 2. **The balance of their deck means they are very difficult to sideboard against.**

> **The more extreme the strategy, the easier it is to sideboard against; the more balanced the
> strategy, the more difficult it is to sideboard against.**

## Mana and tempo: the unique problem

**A problem spawned by everything above.** Every deck must have early plays, **but it's extremely
difficult to find cheap creatures that are good on both offense and defense.** Low power and toughness
means they're too likely to be outclassed later.

**One option is to eschew cheap creatures for removal spells, but then you have a lot of dead cards
against control.**

> **The challenge is to find early plays that are good across all matchups and maintain their value
> throughout the game.**

**The Abzan solution: mana creatures.** Beyond meeting the criteria, they let you **play a slightly
higher concentration of expensive cards and a slightly lower land count.**

**The related solution: tapped lands.** Midrange is well-suited to **Temples** (Temple of Malady) and
**tri-lands** (Sandsteppe Citadel).

> **To preserve the power level of your deck, plan to spend your early turns making investments in
> tempo in order to develop your mana. It's all worth it if you function smoothly, reliably, and
> effectively in the midgame.**

## Card advantage: incidental, not direct

**Card advantage matters to midrange, but where control can play Divination and Jace's Ingenuity,
midrange must be more creative.**

> **If your goal is to shine in the midgame, it's a bad idea to spend a crucial turn drawing cards
> instead of developing or managing the battlefield.**

**So midrange looks for incidental card advantage — card advantage that comes indirectly, or is
attached to another effect.**

**Courser of Kruphix** is the perfect example. On turn three you really want to add a creature, and
Courser does that **while continuing to pull its weight for as long as it's in play.** Playing lands
off the top means **getting more cards into play without spending anything from your hand**, and an
increased chance of drawing nonland cards, **mitigating mana flood.**

**Abzan Charm** again: playing Divination would be a bad idea, but **Abzan Charm's primary function is
removal**, and there's tremendous value in having a card-advantage option available when the situation
calls for it.

**Planeswalkers.** Duke gives three reasons midrange is best suited to them:

1. **Enough creatures alongside them to control the battlefield.**
2. **Enough removal to protect them even when behind on board.**
3. **Aiming at a slightly longer game, so they get tremendous long-term value from activating every
   turn.**

### Virtual card advantage through card quality

**Very often this is simply having bigger creatures than your opponents.**

**The illustration.** Abzan versus a red weenie deck. As the game goes long, Abzan might draw **Siege
Rhino** while red draws **Foundry Street Denizen**. **Abzan gains virtual card advantage simply by
having a higher average card quality.**

**Then Duke enumerates the three outcomes of a Siege Rhino facing a swarm of small red creatures, all
of which favor Abzan:**

1. **Red doesn't attack** → Abzan has **blanked their creatures**, at least temporarily.
2. **Red attacks and loses a creature to get damage in** → Abzan **killed a creature for free.**
3. **Red trades a creature plus a Titan's Strength or Lightning Strike to kill the Rhino** → Abzan
   **got a two-for-one.**

> **The more card advantage you can build into the structure of your deck, the better off you'll be.**

## Ending the game, and turning the corner

> **The most important difference between a midrange deck and a control deck is that the midrange deck
> does not necessarily have inevitability.** It is not guaranteed to win just by surviving long
> enough.

Some midrange decks risk mana flood. Others struggle with particular cards or combinations. **Lax's
Abzan would have trouble with Hornet Queen in game one, needing multiple cards to answer it** — a
minor problem, not a fatal flaw.

**The solution is simple: kill your opponent.**

Everything about creatures playing defense has a second purpose. **Once you're ready, you turn your
creatures and planeswalkers on your opponent, use removal to kill their blockers, and they can be dead
before they know what happened.**

> **We call this turning the corner — the turn where your goal shifts toward killing your opponent.**

**Why it's crucial specifically for midrange:** because they lack inevitability, **the answer to
certain troublesome cards is to win either before they show up or very soon after.** Playing Abzan and
smelling a Hornet Queen coming, get the opponent low enough that a Siege Rhino backed by **Ajani,
Mentor of Heroes** can finish the job.

> **When you play a midrange deck, you're never totally out of the game.** You have a diverse suite of
> answers for the most common problems, and where you don't have the right answer, **you have the plan
> B of simply killing your opponent.**

## The second example: Yuuya Watanabe's Jeskai Midrange

Also Top 8 at the same Pro Tour. **Despite sharing virtually no cards with Abzan and playing out
completely differently, the principles are largely the same:**

- **Defensive posture against faster decks, aggressive posture against slower decks.**
- **A small handful of potent creatures.**
- **After sideboarding, adapts to whatever role the matchup needs.**
- **Even more than Abzan, a remarkable ability to turn the corner**, ending games with a flurry of
  burn spells.

**Duke's closing tournament argument:**

> In a tournament, a collection of various strategies bash into one another until one comes out on
> top. **Instead of guessing which strategy will be best, you can play something more flexible and
> adapt to whatever your opponents will be up to.**

## Bonus: Commander 2014 preview — Myriad Landscape

Duke ties the preview to two themes from the installment: **incidental card advantage**, and the
**midrange lack of early plays** solved by **investing early turns in tapped lands.**

**Short term, Myriad Landscape is worse than a basic land**, entering tapped and producing only
colorless. **For a deck with lax early mana requirements, not a steep price.**

**Long term it turns itself into two basic lands**, which is **mana ramping and card advantage without
ever playing a spell from your hand.** Exactly the incidental card advantage that keeps a deck running
smoothly.

**Comparison to Krosan Verge.** Functionally quite similar, and Krosan Verge was a defining card when
Standard-legal. Duke argues **both are Legacy-power-level cards under the right circumstances and
probably deserve more attention than they get.**

**The important difference:** Myriad Landscape can search for **any type of basic land**, which makes
it a strong consideration for decks that benefit from many lands of the same type, such as **Cabal
Coffers** or **High Tide** builds. He adds it could also be good if you had a reason to play
**Snow-Covered** basics, such as **Skred**.

## Notes for use

- **The archetype trilogy completes here.** Aggro wins on tempo, control wins on inevitability,
  midrange wins on neither and therefore must **turn the corner**. That term is the most useful thing
  in this installment.
- **The "no dead cards maindeck, no versatility needed in sideboard" split** is a genuinely good
  deckbuilding rule that generalizes to every archetype.
- **The three-outcomes Siege Rhino analysis** is the clearest demonstration of virtual card advantage
  in the whole course. Every branch favors the bigger creature, which is what "card quality as card
  advantage" actually means in practice.
- **Cross-reference to Block 2, sharply.** Midrange is the archetype that most often has to answer
  Flores's question, because it can be either the beatdown or the control depending on matchup. Duke's
  "defensive posture against faster decks, aggressive posture against slower decks" is role assignment
  restated. See `block-2-core-theory-paired/01-whos-the-beatdown-1999-04-13.md`.
- **Note the direct contradiction with installment 07.** Linear Strategies says every card must serve
  the plan and you cannot afford detours. Midrange says the opposite: every card must serve *multiple*
  plans. Both are correct, and the choice between them is the choice Flores frames as raw power versus
  flexibility.
- **Reading caution:** heavy Khans-of-Tarkir specificity, plus a Commander 2014 preview at the end.
  The framework survives; the card evaluations are a decade stale.
