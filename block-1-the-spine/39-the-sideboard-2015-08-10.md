# The Sideboard

**Reid Duke — Level One, August 10, 2015**

---

## The framing

> It's easy to explain that a Magic deck consists of 60 cards with a limit of four copies of any card.
> **It's a bit more challenging to teach about the extra fifteen cards that come along with — yet are not
> part of — the deck. Harder still is conveying just how important those cards are, and how vital it is
> to choose them carefully.**

**The mechanics:** matches are best two of three. **Game one uses maindecks. After game one, players may
swap in any number of their fifteen sideboard cards to tailor the deck toward winning the next game.**

**The legality note:** **you may play more than 60 cards, but your deck must always be at least 60 and
your sideboard at most fifteen.**

> **Sideboards win tournaments. Because your sideboard cards can be more specialized — pinpoint focused
> for a certain task or matchup — they're often your most powerful tools.**

> **Sometimes sideboarding can be the most important factor in determining how two decks match up.**

---

# The four things a sideboard does

## 1. Answering problems

> **You sideboard with the goal of making your deck better suited for a matchup. What simpler way is
> there than to bring in the perfect answers for your opponent's threats?**

**The classic case:** artifact and enchantment removal. **Except in extreme circumstances players don't
maindeck cards like Smash to Smithereens for fear they'll be dead against certain opponents. But it's
nice to have access when you face a deck reliant on artifacts.**

**Other pairings he gives:**

| Opponent's plan | Your answer |
|---|---|
| Lots of enchantments | **more enchantment removal** |
| A swarm of cheap creatures | **a sweeper like Languish** |
| One particularly devastating spell | **Negate** |

> **Your sideboard lets you find the perfect tool for any job. Playing game one ought to show you exactly
> what "the job" is in the case at hand.**

## 2. Adding threats

**Three distinct versions:**

**Overload their answers.** Against a control deck with very few creatures, **take out ineffective
creature removal and increase your concentration of threats.**

**Upgrade existing threats.** Against sweepers, **Hangarback Walker** gives more resilience. A Goblins
deck might want **Subterranean Scout** against annoying blockers. **Sideboarding is about perfecting your
deck, and a few minor upgrades can be a big part of that.**

**Diversify your threats — the most common goal.**

> **It's all about sticking a threat your opponent cannot effectively answer. And the more angles you can
> attack from, the better chance you have of doing so.**

**His example:** **Molten Vortex** for red decks. **Control will try to shut you down by killing your
creatures and countering your burn spells. There's not a lot they can do to overcome the slow bleed from
a Molten Vortex in a protracted game.**

> **In general, look for planeswalkers and other noncreature threats as excellent sideboard cards against
> slow decks.**

## 3. Hate cards

> Answering problems and adding threats are **versatile** ways to use sideboard slots — you'll turn to
> them often, as minor upgrades in a lot of matchups. **Another approach is to look for hate cards:
> single cards extremely effective at beating a particular deck, color, or strategy.**

**His examples:**

- **Orbs of Warding** — **decks heavy with burn will hardly be able to touch you.**
- **Gaea's Revenge** — **a nightmare for blue control decks hoping to sit back on permission and
  removal.**
- **Tragic Arrogance** — **can utterly demolish a creature ramp deck like Green Devotion.**

**The rule of thumb, which recurs throughout the course:**

> **The more extreme a strategy is, the easier it will be to sideboard against.** You can hamstring a
> small creature deck with a cheap sweeper like Languish. **You can shut down a combination deck by
> knocking out one of their pieces before they set up.**

> **Any time you find yourself with extra space in your sideboard, identifying and playing hate cards
> against a popular deck is a great way to improve your chances.** You might not use them as often as
> your versatile cards, **but sometimes they can swing a matchup at the low cost of a slot or two.**

## 4. Transform sideboards

> **The most interesting thing about sideboarding is that it's done in secret. Most of the time your
> opponent won't even know what cards are in your sideboard, let alone which ones you'll bring in.**

**Two examples:**

- **A control deck with very few maindeck creatures.** If your opponent boards out creature removal while
  you board in threats like **Jace, Vryn's Prodigy** or **Hangarback Walker**, **you might steal an easy
  win.**
- **A seemingly aggressive Jeskai deck transforming into control** with **End Hostilities**, **Elspeth,
  Sun's Champion**, and **Dig Through Time**. **An opponent who goes overboard with creature removal and
  lifegain can be easily outdone in the late game.**

**Duke's warning, and it's firm:**

> **Transform sideboards can be fun and impressive when they work out, but I recommend turning to them
> only as a hail mary when nothing else is working.**

> **Sideboarding should be about perfecting your deck, not about clumsily trying to employ two conflicting
> strategies at the same time.**

# How to build your sideboard

> **Building your sideboard is as challenging and as important as building your main deck. From my
> experience, it's typically given only a small fraction of the attention it deserves.**

## The elephant method

**Duke credits Hall of Famer Zvi Mowshowitz and quotes the definition:**

> **"Writing out ideal realistic lists for all matchups and then trying to make the unique cards in those
> lists add up to 75 cards before deciding on the specific 60 for the main deck and the specific fifteen
> for the sideboard."**

**Duke's gloss — the procedure in three steps:**

1. **Consider what you'd like your deck to look like after sideboarding in each matchup you expect to
   face.**
2. **Make sure you have the proper number of cards to bring in and take out in each matchup.**
3. **Construct your deck and sideboard accordingly.**

> **You're thinking of your deck as a complete 75-card unit. You look at the big picture, and your
> sideboard is as important to the big picture as your maindeck is.**

## Don't over-sideboard

> **It's dangerous to bring in so many sideboard cards that you compromise the original game plan of your
> deck. In sideboarding, continue to pay close attention to your creature count, your mana curve, and the
> other important details of your deck's composition.**

**Two failure cases:**

**Aggro.** **Swap out six creatures for six answer cards and you're liable to lose too much of the
aggressive potential that made you choose the deck in the first place.**

**Control.** Against fast aggro you might be unimpressed by **Read the Bones**, especially since it costs
life.

> To be sure, it's not one of your best cards in the matchup, **but you do need to maintain a certain
> density of powerful late-game cards, or else you might no longer be able to win.**

**And Duke's admission of his own repeated error, which is the most useful line in the installment:**

> **Many times I've made the mistake of sideboarding in too many cheap removal spells, only to find
> myself suffering from mana flood and losing the long games my deck had been originally designed to
> win.**

> **It's possible that Read the Bones is so important to the overall structure of your deck that you
> should leave it in, even against an opponent who's attacking your life total.**

## The worked example: Mike Sigrist, 2nd at Pro Tour Magic Origins

**Blue-Red Ensoul Artifact.** Duke chose it specifically because **the risk of over-sideboarding was
especially large:**

> **His deck had a very specific and focused game plan.** If he took out too many cheap artifacts, **his
> Ensoul Artifacts would lose their effectiveness.** If he shifted gears and became too defensive, **he
> wouldn't be able to get opponents into range of his Shrapnel Blasts.**

**So the sideboard was mostly answers:**

| Card | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Roast** | **Green creature decks with Courser of Kruphix, Siege Rhino, Whisperwood Elemental** |
| **Seismic Rupture** | **Defense against creature swarms; nearly a hate card against mono-red** |
| **Disdainful Stroke** | **Slower decks that rely on expensive spells** |

**The structural discipline:**

> When Mike would bring these in, they would **either be upgrades for the reactive cards in his maindeck
> (Stubborn Denial or Collateral Damage), or he would only make small adjustments here or there.**

> **A top priority was always to maintain the structure and mana curve of his deck, and make sure he could
> still function at maximum efficiency after sideboarding.**

**And the three slots that weren't answers:**

> His final three slots were **Thopter Spy Network**, a way to **diversify and upgrade his threats in
> matchups where he needed extra late-game power.** Decks like Abzan Control and Blue-Black Control would
> **defend, slow the game, and grind small advantages. Thopter Spy Network would make this plan look silly
> by ensuring his opponents would be buried under a mountain of card advantage.**

## Practice with sideboards

> **Sometimes the way two decks match up after sideboarding has virtually no resemblance to the way they
> match up in game one. When this is the case, you want to know about it ahead of time instead of facing
> a trial by fire in the actual tournament.**

> **The key is simply to give it the attention it deserves rather than throwing it together the morning
> of the tournament.**

> **Personally, in the late stages of my tournament preparation, I like to be playing virtually all of my
> practice games with sideboards. If you aren't playing any sideboarded games, or are doing so only as an
> afterthought, I recommend rethinking your process.**

## Notes for use

- **The elephant method is the deliverable.** Build 75 cards, not 60 plus 15. Write the list you *want*
  for each expected matchup first, then work backwards to find which cards must be maindeck and which
  must be board. Zvi's phrasing is worth quoting exactly.
- **"Games two and three are the majority of the match"** is the implicit argument for why this matters
  as much as maindeck construction. Most players invert the effort.
- **The over-sideboarding warning is the most practically valuable content**, and Duke's own confession —
  boarding in too much cheap removal and then flooding out — is the specific, recognizable failure. The
  check is mechanical: after boarding, recount creatures, recount the curve, and ask whether you can still
  win the way the deck was built to win.
- **Note the counterintuitive corollary:** a card that's *bad in the matchup* may still need to stay in
  because it's *structurally load-bearing*. Read the Bones against aggro is the example. Sideboarding by
  card quality alone breaks decks.
- **The extremity rule appears here in its clearest form** — the more extreme a strategy, the easier to
  hate out. That's the cost side of the linear-deck tradeoff from installment 07 and the reason midrange
  boards well (installment 10).
- **The transform sideboard is deliberately demoted to a hail mary.** Worth heeding: two half-decks beat
  neither.

  installment 26 covers how sideboard cards change mulligan decisions; installment 42 (The Metagame)
  determines which matchups to build for in the first place.
