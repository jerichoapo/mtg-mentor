# Sealed Deck

**Reid Duke — Level One, September 15, 2014**

---

## Formats, and where Limited sits

**Up to this point Level One covered fundamentals applicable to any game with any deck.** This
installment begins the format-specific material.

**What a format establishes:** what cards are legal — **which sets you can build from, whether any cards
are banned, and any special rules unique to the format.**

**The two major categories:**

- **Constructed** — you build a deck, usually 60 cards, at home and bring it.
- **Limited** — you open fresh packs at the tournament and build, usually 40 cards, on the fly.

> **Limited formats, while difficult to master, are perfect for beginning players, since they won't be
> at a disadvantage for not owning an extensive collection.**

**Sealed Deck specifically:** **open six booster packs, add as many basic lands as you like, and build a
40-card deck.**

> **Of all formats, Sealed Deck is a great starting point because the fundamental concepts of Magic
> shine through most clearly.**

## How Sealed compares to other formats

> **Six booster packs is not a whole lot to work with. With all players having limited resources, it
> makes for a format with a low overall power level.**

**The consequence for deckbuilding:**

> **You typically won't be able to build a very focused deck, because you won't get enough cards of any
> one particular type. Instead, aim for a well-balanced deck with a healthy mix of threats and answers.**

**The consequence for game length:**

> **Games of Sealed Deck often go long, rarely ending before turn six or seven, and sometimes stretching
> until turn fifteen or twenty.**

> **If you're not careful you can spend the seven cards from your opening hand very quickly and be at
> the mercy of drawing one card per turn for a large portion of the game.**

---

## The three ways a Sealed game gets decided

Duke structures the article around three failure modes, in order.

---

## 1. Mana

> **Mana ought to be your number one priority in Sealed Deck. Building a reliable mana base and a
> balanced mana curve is half the battle.**

**On color count:** he notes he normally strongly advocates two-color decks, **and in Sealed, sticking to
two colors is usually best, as three or more colors can be prone to awkward mana draws.**

**The Khans exception:** that set **emphasized multicolored cards and was designed for players to
most often play three colors**, so **mana would be a special challenge.**

### Nonbasic lands are the starting point

> **You can add any number of basic lands, but you're limited to only the nonbasic lands you open.**
> Lands that produce more than one color **will be extremely valuable, and looking at which nonbasic
> lands you have is a very good place to start when you first open your packs.**

**The worked example.** Building white-black-green (Abzan):

- **Six Plains, six Forests, six Swamps** → **not very reliable.** In some games you won't draw Plains,
  in some no Forests, in some no Swamps.
- **Replace three basics with Sandsteppe Citadel, Scoured Barrens, and Jungle Hollow** → **extra sources
  of all three colors, and the deck becomes more reliable.**

### Splashing

> **Focus your deck primarily around two colors and make your third a splash for a couple of powerful
> multicolored cards.**

Center on white and green with a little black: **you get to play more Plains and Forests, and you reduce
the impact of not drawing black mana right away, since fewer cards depend on it.**

### The two-sided conclusion

> **It's a sad fact that some portion of Sealed games are determined by one player having an awkward
> mana draw. It won't matter how good your cards are if you simply can't cast them.**

> **Build with a reliable mana base and you'll minimize the number of games you lose to an awkward mana
> draw. Build with a balanced mana curve, with enough cheap creatures, and you'll maximize the number of
> games that you punish your opponent for stumbling on mana.**

---

## 2. Bombs

> **A bomb is a card so powerful that it will often determine the outcome of a game all on its own.**

**Two responses:**

**Play your own.**

> When you open your packs, **take note of your very best cards, especially among your rares. Single
> cards with an abnormally high impact can give you a huge advantage in Sealed, and it's often worthwhile
> to go out of your way to play with them.**

**Have answers to theirs.**

> **Short of bombs themselves, removal spells are among the best cards in Sealed Deck. Having an answer
> to your opponent's bomb often means the difference between defeat and victory.**

**And the discipline that goes with it:**

> Not only should you include as many removal spells as possible, **but you should also be patient with
> them during gameplay. It's a shame to waste your removal on your opponent's Mardu Skullhunter only to
> lose to Necropolis Fiend later in the game.**

---

## 3. Long games and creature stalls

**When both players avoid the first two pitfalls, expect a long game.**

> Once in a while one player has an excellent draw of powerful creatures and wins convincingly. **But
> it's difficult to build a powerful or focused deck with only six packs. Much more likely is that both
> players deploy creatures of comparable power until the board gets clogged and neither can profitably
> attack. This is called a creature stall.**

> **Creature stalls are common in Sealed Deck, so it's important to have a plan for breaking parity.**

**Two ways to break one:**

**1. Card advantage** — either technical or virtual. **Drawing extra cards or having a high concentration
of high-impact cards gives you an advantage in a drawn-out game.**

**2. Evasion creatures** — flying or otherwise unblockable.

> **The value of evasion creatures skyrockets in Sealed Deck.**

**And if neither player breaks it:**

> The game may be decided by **mana flood**. It can feel unlucky, **but note that this is an inevitable
> part of a game that goes long and is very common in Sealed Deck.**

**Three defenses against flood:** build the mana base and curve carefully; include as much card advantage
and as many high-impact cards as possible; **look for mana sinks** — cards with **outlast** being the
Khans-era example.

---

## High-impact cards

> **In a long game it's easy to spend the seven cards in your opening hand quickly and be stuck playing
> off the top. In games like these it's of paramount importance that you get as much as you can out of
> every one of your cards.**

**Two techniques, both counterintuitive:**

### Don't play too many cheap cards

> **Cheap cards are typically less powerful — lower impact — than expensive cards. This makes them less
> valuable in general, and less beneficial to draw off the top in a creature stall.**

> **Play enough cheap creatures that you won't get overrun, and then begin to focus on your late game.**

**His number:** it depends on deck and format, **but you typically won't need more than five or six one-
and two-mana creatures in Sealed Deck.**

### Make sure you have enough creatures

> **Removal spells are great and you should play as many as you can get your hands on. However, you also
> want as many creatures as possible — I'd say between fourteen and nineteen is ideal.**

> **You should play with very few cards that are not creatures or removal spells. If a card is not a
> threat and doesn't directly answer a threat, it had better do something very, very special for you to
> play it in Sealed Deck.**

---

## The checklist

**Duke's recap of what to look for in a Sealed deck:**

1. **Reliable mana base**
2. **Balanced mana curve**
3. **As many of your best cards as possible, especially your bombs**
4. **As many removal spells and answers to bombs as possible**
5. **Ways to win a long game and break a creature stall** — card advantage, evasion creatures,
   high-impact cards
6. **Resilience to mana flood**
7. **High concentration of creatures, low concentration of noncreature, non-removal cards**

## The process: how Duke actually builds

> **While there's not necessarily a "best" process, it is important to have some kind of process, or
> else you might wind up wasting a lot of time and being unable to hone in on your best options.**

**His five steps:**

**1. Open all six packs and sort.** Separate the five individual colors, and sort multicolor cards into
the five wedges (Abzan, Jeskai, Sultai, Mardu, Temur).

**2. Look at your nonbasic lands.** **Right away this might rule out certain color combinations where
your mana would be too unreliable. If you have a lot of fixing in certain combinations, you'll be drawn
toward those.**

**3. Gauge your most powerful cards.** What are your rares? **Which are bombs?** What about premium
uncommons and removal?

**4. Separate out the cards you're not excited to play.** The bulk of your noncreature, non-removal
cards, and some low-impact creatures. **You can always revisit this pile if your deck comes up a few
cards short.**

**5. Start laying out actual decks.** At this point you have a lot of information: **where's your fixing,
where are your bombs, what colors are deep in solid cards, what colors are shallow?**

> If any combination stands out after sorting, start there. **If not, it's okay to pick one at random —
> after all, you have to start somewhere.**

**Then evaluate against the checklist:** how's the curve, are there obvious weaknesses. **Repeat for any
other combination you're curious about.**

> **I recommend using the full amount of time to build your Sealed Deck; there are tons of possibilities
> to consider. At a certain point it'll be a matter of intuition — just go with what your gut tells you
> is best.**

---

## Notes for use

- **The three-failure-mode structure is the useful frame:** games are lost to bad mana, to an unanswered
  bomb, or in a long grind. Each has a distinct deckbuilding answer, and the checklist maps onto them
  one-to-one.
- **"Don't play too many cheap cards" is the most counterintuitive advice in the installment** and
  directly contradicts Constructed instincts. In a format where games run to turn fifteen, low-impact
  early creatures become dead topdecks. Five or six one- and two-drops is the cap, not a floor.
- **14–19 creatures and almost nothing that isn't a creature or removal** is the concrete build
  constraint. It's a high bar and worth applying literally.
- **Patience with removal is the play-skill half of the bomb problem**, and it's the same lesson as
  line-up theory (installment 23), which uses the identical Sealed framing: save the answer for the
  card that actually requires it.
- **The five-step process is worth adopting wholesale**, particularly step 2. Sorting nonbasic lands
  *before* evaluating card power inverts most players' instinct and prevents falling in love with a
  color pair the mana can't support.

  conditions for choosing to draw are most often met in this format; installment 12 notes permission
  rises in value in Sealed because of bombs.
