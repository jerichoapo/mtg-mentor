# Flexibility

**Reid Duke — Level One, September 14, 2015**

---

## The premise

> **Answering the toughest questions in Magic is about walking fine lines. It's about striking perfect
> balances. Power and consistency, risk and reward, speed and staying power.**

> **Choosing flexible cards or building flexibility into the structure of your deck will lower your risk
> and increase your consistency.** In other words, **flexibility helps prevent things from going
> wrong.**

> **If flexibility doesn't come at the cost of too much power, you should jump at the opportunity.**

## Modal spells

> **A modal spell is simply a spell that asks you to choose "modes" when you cast it.**

**Abzan Charm** offers three distinct options tailored to varying circumstances:

> **In the middle of a hairy combat step? Add two +1/+1 counters. Troublesome creature on the other side
> of the board? Exile it. None of the above? Cash it in for two new cards.**

**The key claim, and it's the same argument Duke made about Valorous Stance in installment 07:**

> **If you were to separate Abzan Charm's modes into three separate cards, it's possible that none of
> them would be good enough for competitive play in Standard. However, combining all of the options into
> one neat package makes it one of the best cards in the format.**

> **It greatly increases the likelihood that the Abzan player will have the right tool in any
> situation.** In my opinion, **the flexibility of Abzan Charm is one of the things that's made Abzan the
> most successful deck in Standard.**

## Mana sinks

**Recall:** cards that, **while not necessarily expensive in themselves, can make use of your extra mana
in late-game scenarios.** They **increase consistency by providing insurance against mana flood.**

**Why they count as flexible — they're good at both ends:**

> **If they aren't expensive to put into play, they won't make your deck slow, top-heavy, or reliant on
> having tons of mana. However, they're also helpful if you do draw too many lands, or if the game is
> dragging into the very late stages.**

## Cards with X in the mana cost

> **One particular category of mana sink is cards with "X" in their mana cost, like Hangarback Walker.
> "X" means flexibility. It means you can spend however much mana you please, and the card's effect will
> scale accordingly.**

**Hangarback Walker:** **you can put it into play right on turn two, but if you draw it on turn eight
you can make it much bigger.**

## The preview: Endless One

**Also a creature with X in its cost.**

> **You can cast it early if you need to in order to keep pace in tempo. Later in the game, it can
> easily be the biggest creature on the table.**

### Plugging holes in your curve

**This is the most useful idea in the installment.**

> **Imagine your goal is simply to use all of your mana to cast creatures on every turn.** Ideally a
> one-drop on turn one, a two-drop on turn two, a three-drop on turn three, and so on. **To maximize
> your chances you build with a healthy mix of creatures at each cost.**

> **Inevitably, though, you're going to have games where your draws don't line up.** Maybe you have a
> one-drop and a two-drop but no three-drop. **Maybe you miss your land drop on turn five and can't
> continue moving up the curve.**

> **Endless One guarantees these things won't happen. On any turn, with any amount of mana, you can
> spend it on Endless One.**

### The general principle

> **That's the real beauty of flexibility — it helps you in the games where your draw isn't perfect. It
> raises the floor for how bad things can go for you and ensures that you have something to work with in
> each and every game you play.**

## The cost of flexibility

**Duke is careful not to oversell it:**

> **Flexibility is an important factor in striking the balance between power and consistency. Playing
> with flexible cards will always increase your consistency, but will sometimes come at the cost of
> power.**

**Three concrete comparisons:**

| Flexible card | What you'd rather have at that cost |
|---|---|
| **Hangarback Walker** for two | **Fleecemane Lion** |
| **Hangarback Walker** for four | **Siege Rhino** |
| **Abzan Charm** on Polukranos | **Ultimate Price**, same job for one less mana |

**The summary line:**

> **Your flexible cards will rarely let you down, but they'll also rarely be the best possible play you
> can make on a given turn.**

### Why Endless One passes the test anyway

> **Endless One doesn't force you to give up too much power for the sake of flexibility.** Yes, spending
> four mana for a 4/4 isn't quite a Siege Rhino, **but it's still a good rate and is likely to be one of
> the biggest creatures on the board.**

**Three additional advantages of being colorless:**

1. **Playable in any deck.**
2. **Immune to Ultimate Price.**
3. **Positioned to play well with colorless-themed cards from Battle for Zendikar.**

**And one precise limitation Duke flags:** **it's not an artifact**, so it isn't destroyed by
**Kolaghan's Command** or **Conclave Naturalists**.

**Plus the synergy note:** like Hangarback Walker, it features **+1/+1 counters, which pair well with
Abzan clan and Dromoka brood cards** — **Hardened Scales** being one example.

## The close

> **It's a good habit to always keep an eye out for flexible cards.** You'll find flexibility in Endless
> One. **More importantly, you'll find it in any quality card that strikes a healthy balance between
> power and consistency.**

> **Putting cards like Endless One into your decks will lower the chances of a bad draw, but they won't
> cost you much in terms of raw power.**

## Notes for use

- **"Flexibility raises the floor" is the sentence to keep.** It doesn't make your good draws better, it
  makes your bad draws playable. That's a variance-reduction argument, and it's why flexible cards look
  unimpressive in a vacuum and perform well over a tournament.
- **The Abzan Charm claim generalizes and is worth stating as a rule:** three modes that individually
  wouldn't make the deck can combine into a card that's among the format's best. Evaluate modal cards on
  *how often you'll have the right mode*, not on the average power of the modes.
- **The curve-hole argument is the concrete deckbuilding use of X spells.** An X creature isn't
  competing with the best card at any single cost — it's competing with *having nothing to do* on the
  turns your draw misses. That's a much lower bar and a much more common situation.
- **Duke's honesty about the cost is what makes this installment credible.** Flexible cards are almost
  never the best play on a given turn. The trade is: slightly worse ceiling, substantially better floor.
  Whether that's right depends on whether your deck's problem is power or consistency.
- **Cross-reference:** this is the general statement of the argument Duke first makes about **Valorous
  Stance** in installment 07 (a card that does two jobs earns a slot neither job alone would justify) and
  about **Abzan Charm** in installment 10. Mana sinks are from installment 02; the flood/screw framing is
  from installments 02 and 13. Installment 15's "answers that can never be wrong answers" is the same
  idea applied to removal.
- **Reading caution:** the back half is a Battle for Zendikar preview. The Endless One evaluation is a
  good worked example of applying the principle, but the card context is a decade old.
