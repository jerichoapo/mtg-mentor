# Damage Racing

**Reid Duke — Level One, May 4, 2015**

---

## The question

We tend to think of one player as the aggressor and the other on defense. **But role assignment can be
fluid, and often both players use elements of offense and defense at the same time.**

> **What's the best way to kill an opponent who's also trying to kill you?**

## What a damage race is

> **A damage race, or simply a race, shows itself when neither player is able to take firm control of
> the game.**

**Johnny is attacking Jenny. Jenny cannot effectively stop the attack. Her only course of action is to
race — to win before Johnny's creatures deal the finishing blow.**

**What drives races:** most often one or both players having creatures with **evasion** (flying,
unblockable). **But anything that makes it easier to attack than to block might lead to a race** —
prowess, heroic, or the threat of a combat trick are a couple of the innumerable reasons **it might be
more profitable to attack than to sit back and block.**

**The counterintuitive framing, which is the point of the installment:**

Duke lists the images racing evokes — the last minutes of a hockey game where even the goalie leaves
his post, soldiers pouring over enemy ramparts, Han Solo and Princess Leia trying to shut the Death
Star's shields down. **Primal rage, boiling blood, reckless abandon.**

> **Ironically, however, it can be the aspect of Magic that requires the most precision, thinking
> ahead, and careful planning.**

> **It's easy for a race to come down to just a few life points in either direction. Winning a close
> race is about thinking ahead, squeezing every bit of value out of all your resources, and planning
> to make sure you're on the winning side.**

## Using your life total as a resource

**Life is a resource, but with important differences from mana and cards.** You can spend mana and
cards to impact the game, often repeatably. **Life total does not help you control the board or the
flow of the game. It cannot be spent to your advantage. Sort of.**

### In what sense life matters

> **In some ways, the only meaningful life total is 0.** Being at 1 or at 20 doesn't impact your
> ability to play the game or your control over the board.

**But a low life total limits your options:**

- **You must block a creature that attacks you.**
- **Even the threat of an attack can limit you.** Do you need to leave an extra blocker for fear of a
  removal spell or a haste creature?
- **It constrains your own cards.** Can you still use **Mana Confluence** or cast **Read the Bones**?

### Each point of life has a different value

> **Your last point of life is worth everything — you'll do whatever is in your power to protect it.**
> On the other hand, **you're thrilled to start the game with Llanowar Wastes and Elvish Mystic, even
> though it means being at 19.**

**And it's matchup-dependent:** against Esper Control you don't mind taking frivolous damage; **against
Mono-Red Burn, every point really matters.**

**Which leaves the hard question the rest of the installment answers:**

> **How do you equate a change in your life total against a card in your hand or an advantage on the
> board?**

## Chump blocking

> **To chump block means to block with a creature to preserve your life total, even though you're going
> to lose the creature.**

**The default: avoid it when you can, especially early.**

> **Creatures in play can generate advantages as the game goes on, whereas your life points cannot.**

**The standard example.** Everyone learns quickly it's a bad idea to chump block an attacking
**Fleecemane Lion** with an **Elvish Mystic** on turn three. **The Mystic still has work to do**,
helping you cast your four-, five-, and six-mana spells. **Taking the hit sacrifices one resource, 3
life, in exchange for a relevant board effect: keeping the Mystic.**

**But chump blocking is an important technique**, and knowing when to use it wins close races. **Later
in the game you might be thrilled to chump block Surrak, the Hunt Caller if it buys the extra turn you
need to take over.**

### The factor Duke says matters most

> **You're overwhelmingly likely to have the same opportunity later in the game.**

Take 3 down to 17, tap the Mystic for mana for a couple of turns, **and once it's done its job, chump
block the Fleecemane Lion on turn six.** In fact **you'll probably get to save even more damage later**
by blocking a bigger creature, or by blocking the Lion once it's monstrous.

> **When the game is shaping up to be a damage race, strive to get the most value possible from your
> chump blockers.** There's often little point chump blocking early, because you might still get value
> from the creature and **your opponent's most threatening attackers aren't even in the picture yet.**
> As long as you have life to spare, **take the damage from the 3/3, because you might be able to block
> a 5/5 later.**

### But don't wait too long

**Three things go wrong if you do:**

1. **You might go too low and unexpectedly die** to an evasion creature or a burn spell.
2. **Your opponent might spend removal on your chump blocker**, denying you the block entirely.
3. **Your opponent might have an unexpected card** like **Press the Advantage** or **Flying Crane
   Technique** to ruin your plans.

> **Winning a race is about thinking ahead. It's not often good to chump block early, but trying to
> wait until the last minute can sometimes be disastrous.** Be aware of the changing value of your life
> points, and look for the opportunities to best protect them.

## Turning the corner

**Back to Johnny attacking Jenny.** Jenny has two countermeasures: **defending herself or attacking
back.** Sometimes a combination is best. **Sometimes her approach needs to change as the game goes on.**

> **When you find yourself being attacked, it often means you're behind. If you immediately turn to an
> all-out race, you're likely to lose.** Instead, your first priority is to stem the bleeding, using
> blocking, removal, and any other measures available.

**You can't always establish wrought-iron control, but you can usually do something to slow the
attack.** Once you've done all you can, **you need to know how to turn the corner.**

> **Turning the corner is like flipping a switch, where your game plan suddenly changes. You turn the
> corner in the moment when you stop playing defense and instead set your sights on killing your
> opponent.**

### The worked example: Abzan Aggro vs. Mono-Red

**Why Abzan Aggro is a strange defender.** It isn't built for defense — it **damages itself via
Thoughtseize, Mana Confluence, and other pain lands**, and it **isn't chock-full of removal** like Abzan
Control or Esper Control. **But it has one advantage those decks lack: it can win the game very quickly
once ready.**

**The game, turn by turn:**

1. **Mono-Red** casts early creatures and starts attacking.
2. **Abzan** casts **Fleecemane Lion** as a potential blocker. **Mono-Red immediately Lightning Strikes
   it**, deploys another creature, and keeps attacking.
3. **Abzan** casts **Anafenza, the Foremost**. **Mono-Red attacks with everything.** Anafenza kills one
   creature in combat, **but a lot of damage still gets through.**
4. **Abzan untaps on turn four at a low life total, so it's not time to attack yet.** Casts **Siege
   Rhino**. **Life totals: 17 to 7, Mono-Red ahead.**
5. **Mono-Red untaps with no profitable attacks into the two giant creatures and passes.**
6. **Now Abzan turns the corner.** Attacks with Anafenza and Rhino (putting a counter on the Rhino),
   then casts **another Siege Rhino**. **Life totals: 10 to 5, Abzan ahead.** If Mono-Red can't win now,
   **it faces death the following turn.**

**Duke's analysis of what happened:**

> Mono-Red got a substantial early lead, but Abzan stabilized **by using creatures normally meant to be
> attacking in a defensive capacity** — not attacking with Anafenza on turn four. **And perhaps most
> importantly, Abzan found the best chance of winning by turning the corner and ending the game before
> the opponent could find a way past their defenses.**

**Why the timing was critical — reach.** One of Mono-Red's strengths is **reach**, an aggressive deck's
ability to finish off a weakened opponent. **It's possible Mono-Red was holding a Wild Slash or a
Lightning Strike. Given enough time, they could have drawn another burn spell and won.**

> **Therefore it's critical that the Abzan player gave the opponent the narrowest possible window to do
> so.**

**The general lesson:**

> The Abzan player didn't win by killing all the opponent's creatures. **They never locked the opponent
> out, nor had total control. Instead, they just went for the throat.**

> **There's a ton of value in being able to end the game quickly, because you leave much less room for
> things to go wrong.** If your opponent can present a card or combination you might have trouble with,
> **you want the option to get around it by simply going for the win.**

> **Even decks that intend to play defense can benefit from a strong ability to turn the corner.**

## Preparing to race

**The final point, and the most immediately actionable:**

> **You want to get in damage on your opponent any time you have a chance to safely do so.** You might
> think of yourself as playing defense at a certain stage, **but that doesn't mean you can't attack when
> the opportunity presents itself. If you're not intending to block with a creature, attack with it.**

**Two reasons:**

1. **You never know when the game will boil down to a race.** They might play a flier you have no
   removal for. **Now you're forced to race, and any early damage you got in might prove valuable.**
2. **You might have firm control but need to turn the corner** before they draw a burn spell. **If early
   damage changes the clock by one turn, that's one less draw step for your opponent to kill you.**

## The previews: Modern Masters 2015 Edition

Duke notes all signs pointed to **aggressive black-red** being viable in the draft format. **Cheap
creatures backed by burn and black removal is a timeless strategy.**

- **Vampire Lacerator** — as aggressive as it gets, **sacrificing your own life to damage the opponent
  at a faster-than-normal rate.**
- **Goblin Fireslinger** — for all intents and purposes **an unblockable attacker you can count on
  starting turn two.** Its substantial bonus is **chump blocking in a close race**: it's always untapped
  during your opponent's attack step, **and you can even get in that 1 extra point before it bites the
  dust.**
- **Stormblood Berserker** — one of the strongest uncommons in M12 draft. **Bloodthirst lends itself to
  racing, since your creatures lose value if you're not attacking.** In that case **the best defense is
  a good offense.**
- **Dismember** — **the poster card for using your life total as a resource.** Three mana for removal is
  a little expensive, **but the flexibility to pay life in exchange for a tempo advantage can be game
  winning.** A staple in a huge number of Modern decks, some not even black.

**Duke's closing:** master the art of damage racing. **Even if it's not your preferred way to play, it
might only take a Goblin Fireslinger on the other side of the table to force your hand.**

## Notes for use

- **The core reframe:** racing is not recklessness, it's the most arithmetic-heavy part of the game.
  Every point counts, and the winner is usually whoever counted further ahead.
- **The chump-block timing rule is the most immediately usable content in the course.** Delay chump
  blocks to soak the *biggest* attacker, but not so long that removal, evasion, or a trick takes the
  option away. That's a genuine skill with a clear failure mode on both sides.
- **"If you're not intending to block with it, attack with it"** is a free-value rule that costs nothing
  to adopt and compounds across a match. It's the same argument as installment 04's "you should be
  attacking and blocking more than you are," now justified by race math rather than by creature value.
- **Turning the corner is defined most fully here**, having been introduced in installment 10 and
  reused in installment 13. The Abzan-versus-Mono-Red walkthrough is the canonical illustration.
- **This is the Level One companion to the Philosophy of Fire.** Flores's argument that life is a
  finite resource measured in damage rather than cards is the theoretical version of Duke's practical
  one. Read `block-2-core-theory-paired/06-the-philosophy-of-fire-2004-04-23.md` and
  `block-2-core-theory-paired/07-2-3-4-the-philosophy-of-fire-and-the-high-water-mark-2012-07-27.md` alongside this.
- **Note the interlock with reach and inevitability.** You turn the corner precisely to deny the
  opponent draw steps in which to find reach. That makes racing a function of who has inevitability
  (installment 16) and who is the beatdown (installment 17). These three installments are effectively
  one argument split across three weeks.
