# Mulligans

**Reid Duke — Level One, January 26, 2015**

---

## Why this is hard

**Whether to mulligan can be among both the most challenging and most important decisions in a game.**

**Why it can't be solved:**

> A Magic deck is capable of many millions of different opening hands, and your first handful of draw
> steps offers a comparably large number of possibilities. **It's simply impossible to think through
> all the possible six-card hands you might get if you mulligan, or the possible sequence of draws if
> you keep.** Add that you'll play different decks, in different formats, against different opponents,
> and **you will never ever face the same mulligan decision twice.**

> **Mulligans are not an exact science.** Someone might tell you to mulligan your hand, but under normal
> circumstances it would be impossible for them to prove it.

**So the standard is calibration, not correctness:**

> **Even world-class players can have different opinions on a close mulligan question.** Whether you
> mulligan slightly more or slightly less, **so long as you're consistently falling in the "reasonable"
> part of the spectrum, you can feel like you're doing pretty well.**

## The rules, and free mulligans

**To mulligan means to shuffle your hand back into your library at the start of the game and draw a new
hand with one less card.**

**On house rules:** some people allow one or more free mulligans, **but this is never the case in
tournaments.** If you're playing for fun and it makes things more enjoyable, feel free. **But if you
have tournament aspirations, Duke recommends playing with tournament mulligan rules even when
practicing.**

**And a specific warning:**

> **Even if both players mulligan, you should play the game with each starting with six cards.** While
> both players drawing an extra card may seem like a wash, **a mulligan impacts different decks in
> different ways, and has different consequences whether you're on the play or on the draw.**

> **In a tournament, if your opponent suggests a free mulligan, you should never agree to it.**

## Basic mulligan strategy

**The core point, which Duke concedes sounds obvious:**

> **Starting the game with fewer cards is bad.**

> **Taking a mulligan is more than an annoyance or an inconvenience; it's a disadvantage, and a big one
> at that. We work so hard to earn card advantage, and by taking a mulligan your opponent is getting
> free card advantage.**

**It's not unrealistic to win from six, but noticeably less likely than from seven. The problem is that
you're also less likely to win when you keep a bad opening hand.**

> **So don't be afraid to mulligan, either. Whenever you're unhappy with your opening hand, treat it as
> what it is: a choice between two bad options.** Keep a level head and make the decision you deem to
> give the best chance of winning.

### The Two to Five Lands Strategy

Duke's baseline for players without the experience to judge:

> **Keep your hand if you have between two and five lands. Mulligan if you have zero, one, six, or
> seven lands.**

**His own assessment of it:**

> This is the strategy I give to people first learning Magic, **but it's not terribly far off from the
> way I make my own decisions.** I'd estimate it leads to the right decision **approximately 90% of the
> time.** Even when it doesn't, **you'll typically only be making a very small mistake.**

## Mulligans in Limited

> **Compared to Constructed, games of Limited are closer, go longer, and run on slimmer margins. These
> are exactly the circumstances that make mulligans most painful.**

**So in Limited, mulligan relatively infrequently.**

**Two questions to ask:**

1. **Do I have all my colors of mana?**
2. **Do I have anything to play in the first three turns?**

**Adjustments:**

- **Your deck is very fast** → slightly more inclined to mulligan, since you want a cheap creature to
  press the early advantage.
- **Your opponent's deck is very fast** → consider mulliganing a slow hand so you don't fall behind.

> **You still probably shouldn't mulligan a solid hand with three lands and four spells in Limited,
> almost no matter what.**

## Mulligans in Constructed

**This is where you deviate from Two to Five Lands**, because **games are faster, decks are more
powerful, and you're punished more quickly for keeping a sub-par hand.** Decisions vary a lot by what
you're playing with and against.

### Aggro decks

**A fast deck on either side of the table should make both players more willing to mulligan.**

**Example:** on a red weenie deck aiming to unload damage as fast as possible, **your chances drop
dramatically without a creature to play in the first two turns.** Something like **four Mountains and
three burn spells is a hand you should strongly consider mulliganing.**

### Control decks

**The opposite end.** You don't have devastating early plays, **so there's not much in particular you're
looking for.** And **your strategy is based on card advantage, so starting down a card seriously
interferes with it.**

> **Control players should mulligan relatively infrequently**, unless they don't have enough lands, or
> are playing against a fast deck where they desperately need early defense.

### Midrange decks

**Closer to control than to aggro.** The key difference:

> A midrange deck is likely to have **some cards that are great in a given matchup and some that are
> duds.** Thoughtseize is great against control but weak against red aggro; **Bile Blight is the other
> way around.**

**So the rule depends on information:**

- **You don't know their deck** → mulligan relatively infrequently.
- **You do know their deck** → more flexibility to look for your best cards. **You can feel free to
  mulligan a hand with three removal spells against a control player who hardly has any creatures.**

### Linear decks

**Linear decks break Two to Five Lands completely, both playing with them and against them.**

**Playing W/U Heroic (Tom Ross's list).** The deck doesn't play like a normal Magic deck — **instead of
fighting fair it wins quickly while sidestepping common defensive measures.** It sticks a heroic
creature, targets it repeatedly to make it enormous and unblockable, **and holds protection spells like
God's Willing to save it from removal.**

> **W/U Heroic is excellent at employing this one game plan and not great at very much else. If your
> opening hand is not conducive to the game plan, you should mulligan.**

**Duke's example hand has both colors and a good mix of lands and spells — and is still a mulligan:**

- **You already can't achieve your first goal, which is to put a heroic creature into play.**
- **You don't have a protection spell**, so even if you draw a creature, **a single removal spell shuts
  you down.**
- **Your goal is to be fast.** You're good at pressing an early advantage **but not great at clawing
  back from behind, which means you don't have time to wait around and draw out of an awkward hand.**

> **You have a much better chance of winning with a six-card hand than with this one.**

**Playing against it, on Abzan Midrange (Paul Cheon's list, Top GP Denver).**

> **Trying to damage-race against W/U Heroic is a hopeless cause, as is trying to defend yourself by
> blocking.** Instead, your goal is to shut down the game plan by killing the heroic creature or
> Thoughtseizing it away.

**Thoughtseize is your best card, and your removal spells are next.**

**The example hand, on the draw, knowing the matchup, contains none of your best cards:**

> If your opponent has a good hand, **you're likely to be in big trouble by the time you're even casting
> Read the Bones.** You could use it to find a removal spell, **but it's very unlikely you'll beat a
> single God's Willing with this hand, let alone more than one.**

### Key cards

> **There are some decks and matchups where a single card is abnormally important.**

His examples: **Thoughtseize against W/U Heroic**, and better still, **Jeskai Ascendancy in the Jeskai
Ascendancy combo deck** and **Anger of the Gods against mono-red aggro.**

**The rule, in both directions:**

> When a card's presence abnormally affects your chances, **it should influence your mulligan
> decisions.**

- **Against W/U Heroic with a borderline hand and no Thoughtseize** → mulligan comfortably, **because
  any six-card hand with Thoughtseize is likely to be better.**
- **On the flip side** → **you might consider risking a one-land hand with two Thoughtseizes**, because
  of the card's strength in the matchup.

## Mulliganing below six

> **The impact of losing a card becomes magnified as your number of cards gets smaller. Mulligans beyond
> the first one are very costly and should be avoided whenever possible.**

**The operating rule:**

> **After one or more mulligans, if you look at your opening hand and can see any realistic route to
> victory, you should keep.**

**The concrete example:** in Sealed, **keep a six-land six-card hand after a mulligan, so long as you
have all your colors.** The chances your first five draw steps help you **are better than the chances
you'll mulligan into the exactly perfect five-card hand you're looking for** — which would be roughly
three lands with all your colors and two relatively cheap spells. **That's a pretty tall order.**

**When you must go lower:** if your six has no lands or almost no realistic route to victory, you have
to go to five.

> **Going below five cards might as well be the kiss of death, and you should keep almost any five-card
> hand that has one or more lands.**

**Two takeaways:**

1. **Loosen your standards as your card count drops.**
2. **Factor this in as a risk of mulliganing your seven.** What if your six is bad and you have to go to
   five, or fewer?

## The closing frame

> Despite the complexity in the background, **every mulligan decision boils down to a simple question:
> are your chances of winning higher if you mulligan, or if you keep?**

**And the emotional interference to remove:**

> Sometimes you'll feel frustration over previous mulligans, or **you might fear being embarrassed if
> you keep a risky hand and lose. If you can take these factors out of the equation, you'll be better
> off for it.**

## Duke's footnote

> At high levels of competition, particularly in Constructed, **you should be slightly more willing to
> mulligan than the Two to Five Lands Strategy would tell you.** This contributes to a stigma that
> keeping a borderline hand is the sign of a weak player and mulliganing one is the sign of a strong
> player. **However, there's a huge risk to mulliganing too much as well.**

He points readers to a further article, **"Reasons to Keep Keeping."**

## Notes for use

- **Two to Five Lands is the single most portable rule in the course.** It's approximately right 90% of
  the time by Duke's own estimate, and the errors are small. Adopt it as a default and deviate only for
  the reasons this installment enumerates.
- **The footnote is more interesting than the rule.** Duke explicitly names the social dynamic — that
  mulliganing reads as skilled and keeping reads as weak — and warns that the stigma pushes people into
  over-mulliganing. That's a bias correction, not a strategy tip, and it's rarer than it should be in
  strategy writing.
- **"Choose between two bad options" is the right frame.** The mulligan question is never keep-versus-
  ideal, it's keep-versus-six, and the six is also bad. Most misplayed mulligans come from comparing the
  hand to a fantasy rather than to its actual alternative.
- **The below-six rule inverts the standard**, and the reasoning is worth keeping: at five you are not
  looking for a good hand, you are looking for any route to victory. Loosening standards as resources
  shrink is correct and counterintuitive.
- **Key cards are the bridge to sideboarding and metagaming.** When one card dominates a matchup, the
  mulligan becomes a search for that card, which changes the decision entirely. Cross-reference
  installments 39–42.
- **Note the linear-deck exception connects to installment 07.** A deck with no plan B has no ability to
  "draw out of" a bad hand, so its mulligan standards must be tighter than its land count suggests.
- **This is part 1 of 3.** Part II covers Limited (installment 36) and Part III covers Constructed
  (installment 26). Duke devoted three separate weeks to this, which is a signal about where the
  available skill gain actually is.
