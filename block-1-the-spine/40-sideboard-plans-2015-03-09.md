# Sideboard Plans

**Reid Duke — Level One, March 9, 2015**

---

## Duke takes a contrarian position

> **The topic of sideboard plans is actually a rather controversial one. Many of my colleagues recommend
> against having firm sideboard plans, instead emphasizing improvisation and flexibility.**

> **In my opinion, this advice does more harm than good.**

**His argument:**

> It's never good to limit your options, and there will come times when you should deviate. **However,
> creating a sideboard plan is a way to unify your deck construction with your in-match decisions and to
> ensure they serve one another as well as possible.**

> **Your card choices are made based on what you intend to do during sideboarding, and what you do during
> sideboarding fits with your card choices.**

> **Put a different way, having a sideboard plan means seeing the big picture instead of focusing on
> questions one at a time, independent from one another.**

### The two-stage failure without a plan

**Stage one — the deckbuilding error.** You're on a black deck and put **three Pharika's Cure** and
**three Bile Blight** in your sideboard because they're good against aggressive red.

> In round 2 you sideboard against an aggressive red deck **and realize your deck simply has too much
> removal.** Now you either **leave some on the sidelines, or start cutting maindeck cards that were good
> for the matchup anyway.**

**Stage two — compounding it in the match, which Duke calls even worse.**

> You make the obvious decision to trim some **Read the Bones** and expensive cards for the Cures and
> Blights. **But now you've lost the late-game power that was the original strength of your deck.**

> **You kill your opponent's first flurry of creatures only to find yourself flooding out and losing a
> long game. Because you weren't thoughtful enough about your sideboarding, you wound up losing a match
> you might've otherwise won.**

---

## Count the cards in and the cards out

> **This is the risk that you won't have the same number of cards to sideboard out as the number you have
> to sideboard in.**

**The procedure:**

> **For each matchup, go through your maindeck and count the number of cards that are dispensable and
> that you're unhappy with. This is a good target for how many sideboard cards you ought to have for that
> matchup.**

**Worked in both directions:**

**Against aggressive red.** You decide you can afford to trim **one expensive card and two Read the
Bones** — leaving two to maintain late-game strength.

> **Now you know that three cheap removal spells is an ideal number to have in your sideboard.**

**Against creature-light control.** You want to cut **six removal spells** — leaving three **Hero's
Downfall** to kill planeswalkers and as insurance against a surprise creature off their sideboard.

> **Now you know you need to find six non-removal sideboard cards so you don't have any dead weight
> against a control deck.**

> **If you do this preliminary planning in advance of the tournament, you'll have a better-tuned deck and
> you'll make fewer mistakes during sideboarding.**

## Sideboard for the matchup, not for the card

**The best-told story in the installment.**

**Week one.** You're on a white deck. Against a red deck the game drags out, your opponent casts
**Outpost Siege** naming Khans, and **despite high confidence in your deck you simply can't beat the
long-run card advantage.**

**Week two.** You come prepared with **four Erase** in your sideboard. In the finals you face the same
opponent with the same deck. And…

> **Do you really want to bring in those four Erases?**

**The analysis:**

> **Yes, Erase is a perfect answer to Outpost Siege; it's cheap, efficient, and reliable. But Erase is not
> a good card against your opponent's mono-red deck.**

- **She has no enchantments other than Outpost Siege**, and **you don't even know how many she has — it
  might be only one or two.**
- **Her deck is quite fast and you need every piece of early defense you can get.**
- **If you draw dead cards in your opening hand, it's likely to cost you the game.**

> **You might not want to bring in Erase at all, and certainly not four copies.**

**The rule:**

> **When you add cards to your sideboard, you should do so with matchups in mind, not particular cards in
> mind.**

**And the form of reasoning that *would* justify it:**

> Perhaps Erase is a good card against this particular mono-red deck, **but that would only follow from
> logic along the lines of: "This matchup is very favorable for me in the absence of Outpost Siege, so
> it's worthwhile to sideboard a narrow answer card because it prevents one of the main ways I can
> lose."**

> **Such specific and thoughtful analysis is great, and it might lead you to play one or more copies. But
> it's important to do so for the right reasons, and to understand the consequences.**

# When to be flexible

**Duke concedes the point his colleagues were making:**

> **I advise creating firm sideboard plans, either in your head or on paper.** (**You are allowed to
> reference notes in between games in a tournament.**) **This will help you make the best possible card
> choices and give you something reliable to fall back on.**

> **However, if you're comfortable doing so, you should be ready to deviate when the situation calls for
> it. Tournament Magic requires quick thinking and the ability to adapt, and if you become too rigid and
> formulaic it can sometimes hurt your chances.**

**Four triggers for deviation:**

## 1. Is your opponent's deck abnormal?

> Depending on your preparation you'll develop plans against **the top four, five, or maybe ten
> archetypes you expect to face. You won't have a plan for the fifty variations of these decks that are
> out there. You won't have a plan for the new creations some opponents will bring from home.**

> **You'll have plans for the most common versions of the most common decks, but you'll inevitably be
> forced to adapt to small variations.**

**His example:** you have a plan against traditional mono-red, **but what if this opponent is splashing
white for Chained to the Rocks? Does that change the value of your enchantment removal? Does it change
the value of your big creatures like Arbor Colossus?**

## 2. Is your opponent's sideboard strategy abnormal?

> **Always reevaluate your sideboarding between games 2 and 3.**

**The same Outpost Siege story from the other side.** You're on mono-red, and normally your best card
against mono-white is Outpost Siege. But:

> **Last game you cast turn-four Outpost Siege, it got Erased. Turn-five Outpost Siege, Erased. Turn-six
> Outpost Siege, Erased.**

**The solution is to sidestep rather than fight:**

> **You only have three enchantments in your entire deck, and your opponent has demonstrated the
> willingness to sideboard in three or more dedicated enchantment-removal spells.**

> **Instead of fighting this losing battle, what if you sidestep it? Sideboard out your three Outpost
> Sieges and leave your opponent with dead cards. Perhaps winning the old-fashioned way is your best
> chance.**

## 3. Is your opponent playing in an abnormal way?

> **Is your opponent using cards differently than you're used to, and does this change the value of
> anything in the matchup?**

**Both directions:**

**A conservative opponent.** You're on control with **End Hostilities**, but your opponent **has
demonstrated they'll be careful and never play out a second creature.**

> **Against this opponent, a simple spot-removal spell like Murderous Cut could be better than End
> Hostilities.**

**An aggressive opponent.** If you can count on them **always playing the most powerful spell at the
first opportunity, this is an opponent you can use permission against.**

> **If your opponent always plays Stormbreath Dragon on turn five regardless of circumstances, then you
> know exactly when to leave mana open for your Disdainful Stroke.**

**Duke's own flag on difficulty:**

> **At this point we're beginning to get into some rather advanced techniques. If this seems beyond your
> skill level right now, that's completely okay. Just put it on the shelf as something you might work
> toward in the future.**

## 4. Mind games and guessing games

> **Sometimes there's a lot of value in just being unpredictable.**

> **Keep your End Hostilities in for game 2, but sideboard them out for game 3. This way your opponent
> might play conservatively and hold back creatures.**

> **You can get value from the mere threat of the card without actually devoting any slots in your
> deck.**

## Color-hate cards (Dragons of Tarkir previews)

**The general argument first, and it's the durable part:**

> These cards are made even better by **Tarkir block's emphasis on multicolor cards.** Yes, you can
> **Glare of Heresy** an Elspeth, Sun's Champion, **but you can also Glare of Heresy a Siege Rhino, a
> Jeskai Ascendancy, or a Zurgo Helmsmasher.**

> **The overlap of multicolor cards dramatically increases the value of these color-hate cards.**

### The cards, and one genuinely good general point

**Surge of Righteousness.** **Instant speed, which makes it a particularly great answer to the haste
creatures so popular in red.**

**And the aside about lifegain riders, which is the most transferable idea in this section:**

> **There's a tendency to look at something like "gain 2 life" as simply a small bonus tacked onto a
> card. In reality it's much, much, much more than that.**

> **Games of Magic are decided on thin margins, and these little advantages are what add up to determine
> a game. Without the 2 life, Surge of Righteousness would be a mediocre card. With the 2 life it's
> really fantastic.**

**Self-Inflicted Wound.** Same logic applies to **the 2 points of life loss it inflicts.**

> **There's no shortage of good black removal in Standard, but there is a shortage of good black removal
> that costs less than three mana.** Since the most dangerous weenies are white and green, it'll be a
> valuable sideboard tool, **if not a maindeck consideration** — against **Warden of the First Tree**,
> **Rakshasa Deathdealer**, **Seeker of the Way**, **Soulfire Grandmaster**, **Sylvan Caryatid**.

> **What makes me most excited is its ability to answer a monstrous Fleecemane Lion, a card that gives
> slow, removal-heavy decks nightmares.**

**Encase in Ice.** **Special because blue has very few effective removal options.** In a deck without
access to black removal it's likely to be very important. **And it stops a creature without sending it to
the graveyard, invaluable against Ashcloud Phoenix and Flamewake Phoenix.**

**Display of Dominance.** **Green's way of putting the kibosh on black-and-blue controlling decks.** The
most common use is saving creatures from removal, **but the real value is its flexibility.**

> **Green Devotion, as a deck with tons of creatures and few ways to destroy permanents, is very weak
> against Ashiok, Nightmare Weaver. Display of Dominance gives the deck a card that's generally useful
> but destroys Ashiok whenever you need it to.**

Other targets: **Whip of Erebos** and **Kiora, the Crashing Wave**.

**On Limited:** he expects them to be **high picks in Booster Draft, and perhaps maindeck
considerations.**

## Notes for use

- **Duke's contrarian position is the point of the installment**, and his argument is the right one: a
  sideboard plan isn't a script, it's the thing that makes your 75 cards internally consistent. Without
  one, deckbuilding and in-match decisions are made independently and can contradict each other.
- **The count-in / count-out discipline is the single most concrete technique here.** For each expected
  matchup, count what you'd cut. That number tells you how many sideboard slots that matchup deserves. It
  prevents the exact failure Duke describes — sideboard cards you can't actually fit in.
- **"Sideboard for the matchup, not for the card" is the most common real-world error.** Losing to a
  specific card creates an emotional pull toward the specific answer. The Erase story is the perfect
  illustration: a perfect answer to one card, and a bad card against the deck.
- **The sidestep move is underrated.** When the opponent has invested in answers to your card, cutting the
  card turns their answers into dead draws. That's the mirror image of "blanking cards" from installment
  03 and the transform-sideboard bait from installment 32.
- **The lifegain-rider argument generalizes far past this cycle:** small riders on efficient cards are not
  bonuses, they're a meaningful fraction of the card's value, because games are decided on thin margins.
  Compare installment 29's argument about flexible cards and installment 07's about Valorous Stance.
- **Reading opponent tendencies is flagged as advanced by Duke himself**, and it should be — it's the
  point where sideboarding stops being about decks and starts being about people.
- **Cross-reference:** installment 39 (The Sideboard) is the construction half and contains the elephant
  method this depends on; installment 32 for Limited; installment 42 (The Metagame) for choosing which
  matchups to plan against.
