# Line-Up Theory

**Reid Duke — Level One, March 23, 2015**

---

## The paintbrushes

Duke's father spent thirty years as a painter and carpenter, and always taught him: **"There's a right
tool for every job."**

> An expert at what he does, I believe he could've painted the Sistine Chapel's ceiling with a Swiffer
> Wet-Jet Mop, **but such a thing would never happen with my dad.**

Instead he had painstakingly built an enormous collection of brushes — **giant rollers for walls and
ceilings, medium brushes for doors, and tiny pin-point brushes for corners and fine details.**

> If ever I'd get lazy helping my father paint and fail to find the right brush when I changed tasks,
> **he'd immediately pause the project and insist that nothing more be done unless it was going to be
> done right.**

**The transfer, and the stakes:**

> As a child, full of impatience and false confidence, I found my father's paintbrush system annoying
> and unnecessary. **Today, I'm incredibly thankful for the values it instilled in me.**

> If I made a mistake while painting it might mean an extra trip to Lowe's for a new brush or some more
> paint. **In Magic, you get one chance and there's no turning back.**

> **If I waste my Hero's Downfall on my opponent's Siege Rhino instead of using Murderous Cut, then I
> can later lose to their Elspeth, Sun's Champion and be out of the tournament.**

> **Take great care to line up your answers correctly against your opponent's threats. Make it as
> difficult as possible for your opponent to do the same against you.**

## Patience

**Line-up theory informs long-term planning.** In a lightning-fast game against red aggro you just do
whatever you can to survive in the moment. **But the longer games drag out, the more important it
becomes to answer each threat in the best possible way, and to be prepared for what's coming later.**

> **Often this means being patient. It means not going for the quick win, or taking a bit of damage from
> your opponent's creature.**

**The example:** it's tempting to use **Bathe in Dragonfire** on their **Smoke Teller**, but first ask
whether there's a different way to answer it.

- **Perhaps save the Bathe in Dragonfire for a flier later?**
- **Perhaps just let Smoke Teller attack for a couple of turns to see if you draw a creature that can
  block it?**

> **You'll know the answer to these questions once you begin to think in terms of line-up theory.**

## Deck versus deck

> **In the most extreme and pure form of line-up theory, you consider your whole deck against your
> opponent's whole deck.**

**The matchup:** **Sultai Control (Jack Fogle)** versus **U/B Control (Jelger Wiegersma)**, both from
Standard.

**The difference between them:** both are control decks, **but the U/B deck is more dedicated to the
control game plan, opting for extra counterspells where the Sultai deck has Satyr Wayfinders and more
planeswalkers.**

**Duke's teammate Jelger Wiegersma explained why he was so confident in game one:**

> **"I think the key is to almost never fight their card drawing. They usually have about six threats
> and I have about twenty answers. They just deck themselves."**

*(To "deck yourself" means to run out of cards and lose the game.)*

**Why this is the most extreme form of the theory:**

> He looked at both decklists and **saw through the distractions to the factor at the very core of the
> matchup — that the Sultai deck only had a small handful of cards that could actually win the game.**
> If he could save his Disdainful Strokes, Dissolves, and Hero's Downfalls for only those cards, **his
> opponent would be helpless.**

**The tie to inevitability:**

> The matchup typically goes long. **If it goes long enough, and the U/B player employs line-up theory,
> they will always win, because the Sultai player simply doesn't have enough cards in their deck to
> overcome the U/B player's answers.**

### The counter-example: ten-year-old Reid

**Duke's illustration of what happens without line-up theory** is the best teaching device in the
installment, because **every individual decision is defensible.**

Imagine ten-year-old Reid going straight from using the wrong paintbrush to playing a control mirror:

1. The Sultai player misses a couple of land drops early, **so Reid Dissolves two Satyr Wayfinders.**
2. **Turn six: Disdainful Stroke on a Dig Through Time.**
3. **Turn eight: the same play again.**
4. **Several turns later, a Thoughtseize clears the way for a Garruk, Apex Predator**, and the Sultai
   player wins.

**The autopsy:**

> Because of the contents of both decks, and the Sultai player's slow start, **it should've been all but
> a foregone conclusion that the U/B player would win.**

> **Little-kid Reid made reasonable decisions at every point in the game.** All of the spells he
> countered seemed relatively important, and **he didn't make any obvious blunders.**

> **However, because he failed to look at the big picture, he opened a door for something to go wrong,
> and it cost him the game.**

## Hand versus hand

**Now from the other side.** The Sultai player has a challenge: **the U/B player has inevitability, and
looking at the two decklists it seems clear the U/B deck should win** — unless the Sultai player makes
something special happen.

### First, watch whether your opponent is doing it

**The Sultai player still benefits from understanding the whole-deck line-up.** They should **watch
whether the opponent is employing it.** Against ten-year-old Reid **it might have been clear very
quickly that he wasn't being careful with his permission and wasn't playing for the long game.**

> **Once a number of permission spells are gone — either wasted on frivolous spells or milled with a
> couple of lucky activations of Ashiok, Nightmare Weaver — it is possible for inevitability to shift
> from the U/B player to the Sultai player.**

**But you don't want to count on it**, since it depends on a fluke of the draw or an opponent's mistake.

### The technique itself

> **I recommend employing hand vs. hand line-up theory.** In doing so you're **neither playing for the
> ultra-long-term, nor rushing things.** Instead you're **considering the resources each player has
> immediately available and trying to find an advantage by lining them up properly.**

**The worked Thoughtseize decision.** You're on the draw, turn two, both players on two lands, and you
cast Thoughtseize.

**What you know, layer by layer:**

1. **From the whole-deck line-up:** you do *not* have inevitability, **so it's in your interest to force
   the action.**
2. **From the hand-vs-hand line-up:** you have **Hero's Downfall** to answer their **Ashiok, Nightmare
   Weaver**, and **Disdainful Stroke** to answer their **Jace's Ingenuity**.

**Therefore:**

> **You'll want to take away either Dissolve or Hero's Downfall, since those are the answer cards
> allowing your opponent to line up properly against your Ashiok.**

**Which one depends on a read:**

- **If you think they'll tap out for Ashiok on turn three** → take **Hero's Downfall**.
- **Otherwise** → take **Dissolve**.

**And then the subtle follow-up, which is the real lesson:**

> Instead of running out your Ashiok right away, **consider holding it for a while.** If you wait, **you
> can draw another Thoughtseize and line it up against Hero's Downfall, creating an opening for your
> Ashiok.** However, **if you trade Ashiok for Hero's Downfall right away, you've given up the
> opportunity to trade a Thoughtseize for the Hero's Downfall should you draw one.**

> **You're not guaranteed to win this game, but this is the line of play that lines up the cards in your
> hand against the cards in your opponent's hand as well as possible. It gives you the best chance of
> winning a game in an otherwise unfavorable matchup.**

### Doing it without perfect information

**Thoughtseize makes clear examples because you see their hand. But you should run the same thought
process even when you don't.**

**The example:** you're on Abzan against red-white, and they cast **Goblin Rabblemaster**. **You'll kill
it with either Bile Blight or Ultimate Price — which?**

**The questions that decide it:**

- **Do you think your opponent has Hordeling Outburst?**
- **Do you think they have Stormbreath Dragon?**
- **Does your hand have a way to beat one or the other?**
- **Is your deck particularly well suited to beating one or the other?**

> **The answer depends on what tools you and your opponent each have at your disposal, and that's really
> what's at the heart of line-up theory.**

## Line-up theory in Limited

**In Constructed** you should basically always be thinking hand vs. hand. **Whole-deck line-up is
tremendously helpful but only applicable to relatively extreme matchups.**

**In Limited both matter**, with an extra challenge: **you often know very little about your opponent's
deck.** You don't always know who has inevitability or what must-answer cards they hold. **That said,
Limited games frequently go long, especially Sealed.**

### The old saying, and its modern correction

> There was an old saying from the early days of Limited that **you should "only use a removal spell on
> a creature that's going to kill you."**

**Duke's update:** today Limited games are a little faster and tempo is a bigger concern. **He doesn't
recommend taking it as gospel**, but says there's a good lesson in the extreme version.

### The cautionary sequence

**Bathe in Dragonfire is an extremely good Limited card, particularly because it lines up well against a
variety of threats.** Then this all-too-common sequence:

1. **Johnny is on the play but doesn't play a creature on turn one or two.**
2. **Jenny plays Smoke Teller on turn two.**
3. **Johnny casts Bathe in Dragonfire on the Smoke Teller.**
4. **Jenny plays Jeskai Windscout.**
5. **Johnny plays Salt Road Patrol, but eventually loses to Jenny's fliers.**

**The diagnosis:**

> **Salt Road Patrol is an absolutely perfect tool for answering Smoke Teller, but Bathe in Dragonfire
> was Johnny's only tool for answering a flying creature.**

### Sealed and bomb rares

> **In Sealed Deck you should typically expect your opponent to have one or more bomb rares.** Although
> you don't know what it will be, **you should maximize your chance of being prepared for it when the
> time comes.**

> **There might be other ways to beat your opponent's Summit Prowler, but you're going to desperately
> need your Reach of Shadows for when they play Atarka, World Render.**

**And the game-by-game adjustment:**

- **Game 1:** use your best judgment, **but err on the side of patience and preparedness.**
- **Game 2:** after seeing a card like Atarka, **you might begin to employ whole-deck line-up theory and
  say "I'm saving my Reach of Shadows for Atarka and I refuse to use it on any other creature."**

**Duke closes back on his father:** beating a card like Atarka requires a very special tool, **and you
don't want to be caught without it.**

## Notes for use

- **The two scales are the structure:** whole-deck vs. whole-deck (strategic, applies to extreme
  matchups and to game 2 onward) and hand vs. hand (tactical, applies always). Most players run neither
  deliberately.
- **The ten-year-old Reid example is the most instructive failure in the entire course.** Every
  individual play was reasonable. The loss came from never asking the whole-deck question. That's a
  different class of error from a misplay, and it's invisible in the moment — which is exactly why it
  needs a named theory to catch.
- **The counting move is what makes whole-deck line-up actionable:** count their actual win conditions,
  count your actual answers. Jelger's "six threats, twenty answers" is a two-second calculation that
  determines an entire match plan.
- **The hold-Ashiok lesson generalizes:** deploying a threat into a known answer spends your threat *and*
  forfeits the chance to strip the answer later. Patience preserves optionality, which is the same
  argument Duke makes about symmetric effects in installment 14 and about chump blocking in installment
  18.
- **The Bathe in Dragonfire sequence is the Limited version of the same error**, and the tell is
  specific: before spending a removal spell, ask whether a *creature* in your deck could have handled
  that threat instead. Removal is scarcer than blockers.
- **Cross-reference:** this is the direct application of installment 15 (Threats and Answers) — line-up
  theory is how you avoid the "wrong answer" that Dave Price's law warns about. It depends on
  inevitability (installment 16) to know whether patience is affordable, and on role assignment
  (installment 17) to know whether to force the action.
