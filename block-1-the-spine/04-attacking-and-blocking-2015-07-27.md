# Attacking and Blocking

**Reid Duke — Level One, July 27, 2015**

---

## The framing

Duke thanks readers for their patience through weeks of background and fundamentals. His line: you
don't go see *The Terminator* for the love story, and you probably didn't learn Magic because you
like building mana bases. This is the action scene, Level One's version of shoot-outs, car chases,
and explosions.

The disanalogy he draws: some people argue cheesy action movies are a waste of time, that fights and
chases lack substance. **They'd be fools to say the same about Magic.** Mastering fundamentals
matters, but if you can't execute when the combat phase arrives it's all for nothing. **At its core,
Magic is a game of beating the person sitting across the table from you.**

## Use your creatures

> **Your first lesson is that you should probably be both attacking and blocking much more than you
> are.**

**A common strategic error is being too passive during combat.** What is a creature for if not to
attack and block? There's always something to fear: losing your creature when you attack, the
opponent having a trick when you block, letting your life total get too low when you don't. **But you
have to be bold to win. The value of your creatures plummets if you're unwilling to put them into
combat.**

### The classic example

Your opponent attacks a **Cleric of the Forward Order** into your **Hitchclaw Recluse**. They could
have **Mighty Leap**, or be holding **Fiery Impulse** to finish your creature off.

**But the whole point of putting a defensive creature like Hitchclaw Recluse in your deck is to
block.** If you block you might lose it, but if you play the whole game without blocking you've lost
virtually all its value anyway. Unless you have a very specific reason not to, block.

> **Playing too passively is just as bad as playing recklessly.**

### The default rule

**When two similar creatures face off, you should usually attack if you can and usually block if you
can.**

**The blocking case.** Your opponent attacks **Dwynen's Elite** into your Dwynen's Elite. Block:
you're making an even trade and saving 2 damage. You could take it intending to attack back, but what
if they play a bigger creature? **Even in the best case, where you trade damage evenly, you're still
behind in the race.**

**The framing Duke offers:** creatures provide value every turn they attack or block.

- Neither attack nor block → **you've wasted a turn's worth of value.**
- Decline to block and attack back instead → **you've wasted half a turn's worth of value.**

He adds an important aside: **players don't always get the same number of turns in a game, so
mirroring your opponent's actions when you're on the draw will often be a losing battle.**

**The attacking case.** Why attack when the situation is reversed?

1. **They might not block.** They might fear **Might of the Masses**, or simply make a mistake. Even
   if they block nine times out of ten, that's free damage some of the time. **You'll win more games
   in the long run if you give yourself opportunities for free value.**
2. **More realistically, the Elites are destined to trade at some point.** Do it sooner and you leave
   fewer chances for things to go wrong. If you don't attack, they'll probably attack you and you'll
   probably block, but now you've opened the door: what if they now have Might of the Masses? What if
   they play a removal spell and take away your ability to block?

**The exception:** sometimes you have a special reason not to trade, like holding **Joraga
Invocation** and setting up a big turn. Absent that, **trade off creatures sooner rather than later
so your opponent can't take away your ability to do so.**

## Bluffing

Bluffing isn't a huge part of Magic. You can do well playing your cards in the obvious way and never
trying to pull the wool over anyone's eyes. **That said, why not do it if you can?**

**The traditional bluff:** attacking a smaller creature into a bigger one in the early turns. Attack
**Timberpack Wolf** into **Kytheon's Irregulars**. What do you do? I'd only make that attack if I had
**Titanic Growth**. Or would I?

**There's no easy solution, and that's the point.** Both players weigh risk against reward and size up
how bold the opponent might be.

**From the attacker's side:** how costly is losing the Wolf? How valuable are the 2 points? How
likely are you to block?

**From the defender's side:** Kytheon's Irregulars is a powerful card capable of giving a big
advantage in a long game. Doesn't that mean you shouldn't risk it? **But don't I know that, and
wouldn't that make me more likely to bluff?**

### Duke's one piece of hard-won knowledge

> **People bluff less often than you'd expect and people block less often than you'd expect.**

His own practice against an unknown opponent: he'd most often **not block** with Kytheon's
Irregulars, figuring the opponent probably had Titanic Growth. Whether he'd make the bluff attack
depends on the situation. **With a strong hand and a path to winning without risk, he wouldn't
bluff.** If he thought the game would come down to a close race that he could only win by sneaking in
extra damage, **he'd attack** — and would be surprised, though not shocked, if the opponent blocked.

### Semi-bluffing

It takes gall to attack a smaller creature into a bigger one, especially under tournament pressure.
Players are more willing to **semi-bluff**: you'd prefer they not block, but **it's not a catastrophe
if they do.**

Three shapes of it:

1. **Even trade you'd take anyway.** Attacking one Dwynen's Elite into another. You hope they take 2,
   but if they block, you traded creatures of equal power that would have traded eventually.
2. **Backup removal.** Attack **Timberpack Wolf** into **Patron of the Valiant**. If they block you
   shamefully bin your creature, but you finish the Patron with **Fiery Impulse**. Not thrilling, not
   a disaster.
3. **A trick you'd rather not spend.** You'd love to cast **Rhox Maulers** this turn, but if they
   block the Wolf with Patron you have **Titanic Growth** to save it and you'll cast the Maulers next
   turn instead.

**Duke's note on case 3, from the defender's side:** the fact that your opponent will have to spend
mana on a combat trick instead of anything else, like casting another creature, **should make you
more inclined to block.**

**The synthesis:** bluffing and semi-bluffing are more reasons to use your creatures aggressively.
**Every time you attack there's some chance they won't block. Every time you block there's some
chance they were bluffing.**

## The combat phase, step by step

Timing matters enormously with combat tricks, and using one at the wrong moment can be disastrous.
There's also tremendous value in concealing your intentions until the last possible moment. So Duke
walks the phase precisely.

### Priority

**Having priority means it's your turn to act, either to do something or do nothing.** Any time
something happens — spell, ability, attack, block, or moving from one step to another — each player
gets priority. If both do nothing, the turn moves forward and the process repeats.

The vast majority of the time you'll do nothing, to the point that the game zips along without anyone
mentioning priority. **It's simply good to know when you have opportunities to cast a spell, and how
things progress in the rare case they get complicated.**

His illustration: an opponent draws, plays a land, and says "Go." Technically their upkeep, combat
phase, and end step still happened, and **you could have cast a spell at any of them.** Even in the
simplest turn both players get priority many times.

**The active player** is the player whose turn it is. At the beginning of each step or phase, **the
active player always gets priority first.** If they do nothing, the nonactive player gets priority. If
the nonactive player does nothing, the turn moves on.

**Special case:** you always get priority after casting a spell or activating an ability, so even as
the nonactive player you get the first chance to respond to your own stuff.

### The steps

**Beginning of Combat.** The active player has exited the main phase and can no longer play lands or
cast anything but instants. **Last chance to cast something like Send to Sleep** to tap a creature and
stop it attacking.

**Declare Attackers.** The active player chooses attackers and taps them. **At this point there's no
turning back; nothing can cause a creature to "un-attack."** This is where the nonactive player might
flash in a creature like **Bounding Krasis** to ambush.

**Declare Blockers.** The nonactive player chooses blockers. Then the **active player has priority**,
and this is where they'd use a trick like Titanic Growth. Once the active player is done, the
nonactive player acts — their own tricks, or removal like **Swift Reckoning** (only with spell
mastery). Then back to the active player, until both do nothing.

**Combat Damage.** Damage is dealt, life totals adjust, dying creatures go to the graveyard. Then any
abilities triggering on damage or death happen.

**End of Combat.** One final chance to act. **You'd only use this in strange corner cases.**

### The major takeaway

There's no reason to walk all these steps every time; it would make the game slow and tedious. But be
aware of them, because they come up. **When combat is shaping up to be complicated, slow down and
make sure both players are clear on exactly what's going on and when.**

> **The active player must act first.** If you're the active player, you pass priority, and the
> nonactive player also does nothing, **it's too late to go back and cast your Titanic Growth.**

**Getting the last chance to move is an advantage for the blocking player**, who has full information
on what the opponent is up to before deciding.

## Combat tricks

Extremely useful, but easy to misuse. **Big rewards when things go well, big risks when they go
wrong.**

**The cardinal rule: try not to use combat tricks when your opponent has a lot of mana available.**

The disaster case: you cast **Dark Dabbling** and they **Unholy Hunger** the creature in response.
You've walked into a game-losing exchange. **They got a two-for-one** (your creature and your Dark
Dabbling for their Unholy Hunger) **and tempo advantage** from removing a creature, **while you spent
your mana accomplishing nothing.**

> Combat tricks are great when you can use them on your own terms, and extremely risky when you're
> forced to play into your opponent's hands.

**The consequence: don't plan to use combat tricks when you block**, because your opponent will have
all their mana open. Titanic Growth is great when your opponent taps out for a blocker and you can
attack into it without fear. **It's not great when you cast it on your blocker and cross your fingers
that they lack removal or a trick of their own.**

When both players have open mana and possible tricks, move through combat precisely. **Remember the
active player must move first, which advantages the nonactive player, who gets all the information
before acting.**

### The second risk: no creature to use it on

**Duke always advocates a high creature count.** Nobody's ever complained about drawing too many
creatures, but there's a real risk of drawing too few. **With no creatures, or only a few your
opponent can kill, all your combat tricks rot in your hand.**

Related: draw a good hand with a *single* trick and you can find the perfect window. **Draw an awkward
hand with too many tricks and you'll be forced to use them in imperfect situations**, playing into
your opponent's hands.

### The deckbuilding conclusion

**The more combat tricks you put in your deck, the more often you'll be compelled to use them at bad
times.** Duke notes the tricks in Magic Origins are particularly strong, and some aggressive
green-white draft decks might be happy with as many as four. **Under most circumstances, use caution
playing more than two combat tricks in a Limited deck.**

## The close

Attack and block aggressively; that's what creatures are there for. Know how to use your own tricks
safely and effectively, and **force your opponents to play theirs right into the hands of your Swift
Reckoning or Unholy Hunger.** Mastering attacking and blocking makes every one of your creatures and
spells more valuable than your opponent's.

## Notes for use

- **The single most actionable line in the installment:** people bluff less often than you'd expect
  and block less often than you'd expect. Both errors point the same direction — toward passivity —
  which is why Duke opens by telling you to attack and block more.
- **The "value per turn" accounting** (no attack and no block wastes a full turn of a creature's
  value, attacking instead of blocking wastes half) is the cleanest mental model here, and it's a
  tempo argument in disguise. Read alongside installment 05 and Eric Taylor's *it's about TIME* in
  Block 2.
- **The priority walkthrough is the part Arena players most need.** The client resolves priority
  silently, so the ordering — attackers declared and locked, then blockers, then active player acts
  first — is exactly the knowledge that gets people caught in paper. This is also what Block 4's
  Cranial Insertion exists to answer.
- **"Don't use tricks on defense"** generalizes past combat: any time you must act with the opponent
  holding open mana, you are playing on their terms. That's the same structural point Flores makes
  about tapping out in `block-2-core-theory-paired/01-whos-the-beatdown-1999-04-13.md`.
- **The two-trick cap in Limited** is a concrete deckbuilding constraint, and Duke's reasoning
  (excess tricks force bad timing) is a nice example of a card's value depending on how many copies
  of its *type* you run.
