# The Basics of Card Advantage

**Reid Duke — Level One, July 13, 2015**

---

## The cost of playing a spell

Mana is one fundamental resource: you amass it and cast a spell to get the effect you want. Simple,
but it isn't the only thing that happens when you cast a spell.

**Duke's opening puzzle.** You need to block a flier. Two options:

1. Cast **Hitchclaw Recluse**.
2. Cast **Bellows Lizard** and enchant it with **Mantle of Webs**.

Either way you spend three mana and end up with a four-toughness creature with reach. The Bellows
Lizard version even has an extra ability and lets you pump for two mana. **Looking only at mana, you
would never choose the Hitchclaw Recluse.**

But casting Hitchclaw Recluse is far more efficient, and Bellows Lizard and Mantle of Webs are both
rather weak cards. In terms of mana the two options cost the same. **In terms of Magic's other
fundamental resource, they're vastly different.**

The Bellows Lizard line costs **two cards** out of your hand. The Recluse costs **one**.

**Why that matters:** you start with seven cards and draw one per turn. That's a strict limit on what
you have to work with. If both players spend three mana and produce an effective defensive creature,
but you're left with one more card in hand, you'll be positioned to pull ahead in any number of ways.

## Card advantage, defined

Players who don't treat cards as a resource are likely to be frivolous, squander their spells, and
quickly run out of things to do. They'll be at the mercy of the top of their libraries, **a
frightening and unreliable way to play.** A player who treats cards as valuable will squeeze every
bit of value out of each spell.

> **Card advantage is, very likely, the single most important concept in competitive Magic. A
> tremendous proportion of games are decided, in one way or another, by card advantage.**

The term is nearly as old as the game. Duke credits **Eric Taylor**, one of the founding fathers of
Magic writing and theory, with the definition:

> "Card advantage is any process by which a player obtains effectively more cards than his [or her]
> opponent."

If that seems nebulous, it's because card advantage takes a tremendous variety of forms.

## Form 1: drawing extra cards

Here it's simple counting. **Don't forget that it costs you a card to play a spell out of your hand.**

- **Weave Fate** draws two, costs one to cast. **Nets one card.**
- **Jayemdae Tome** draws one per activation but requires an initial one-card investment. Activate it
  four times and it **nets three cards.**
- **A spell whose only effect is to draw one card provides no card advantage.** Cast **Dark
  Dabbling** purely to see a fresh card and you draw one, spend one, net zero.
- **Read the Bones** is similar to Weave Fate but costs 2 life and scries 2. Life loss and scrying
  both matter, but they're hard to compare directly. **In counting card advantage, Read the Bones
  nets one card**, and the other consequences are considered separately.

## Form 2: permanents count too

Card advantage is about more than the number of cards in hand. **Adding a permanent to the board
should typically be counted toward card advantage.**

**Tower Geist** adds a card to your hand *and* a creature to the battlefield. Like Weave Fate, that's
a net gain of one card. You end up with a 2/2 flier in play and the same number of cards in hand.

## Form 3: taking away your opponent's cards

Advantage is by definition relative to your opponent, so favorable interactions with their cards
count.

- **Mind Rot:** you're down the one card it cost to cast. Your opponent is down two. Both players are
  technically down cards, but **card advantage is relative** — your opponent is down one more than
  you, so you gained one.
- **Attacking permanents is the same as attacking cards in hand.** Cast **Languish**, three of their
  creatures die and only one of yours does: you lost two cards (Languish plus your creature), they
  lost three. **Net gain of one.**

## Virtual card advantage

Counting this way is simple, helpful, and a good habit. **It often doesn't tell the full story.**

**The problem case.** In a strict technical sense **Dragon Fodder** adds two creatures and is
therefore two-for-one card advantage. And in the exact case where two goblin tokens block two Bellows
Lizards, it did trade for two cards.

But the simplistic count treats **Dragon Fodder** and **Priest of the Blood Rite** as the same card.
Both put two creatures on the battlefield. How do you account for the fact that your opponent need
only play a 2/2 before those goblin tokens can no longer profitably attack? Or that a 5/5 flying
demon might win the game on its own?

**In practice, games reach a point where some cards can affect the outcome and some cannot.** At that
point it becomes misleading to keep counting cards like Dragon Fodder as card advantage. **Virtual
card advantage** is harder to quantify but accounts for the fact that not every card is equal.

### Dead cards and blanking

Anyone who's suffered a mana flood knows games reach a point where more land doesn't help. Once you
have enough mana to do everything you want, extra lands become **dead cards** that no longer impact
the game.

**One way to gain virtual card advantage is to blank your opponent's cards**, turning them into dead
cards. Duke insists this isn't advanced: **if you've ever played a big creature to block, you've
blanked your opponent's attackers.**

- Put a **Pharika's Disciple** on the battlefield and their 1/1s and 2/2s can no longer profitably
  attack. It can blank essentially any number of small would-be attackers.
- Against **Mind Rot** or **Nightsnare**, you can cast everything right away and hold nothing,
  blanking any future discard spells they draw.
- **You can blank cards during deckbuilding.** Worried about **Smash to Smithereens**? Build with no
  artifacts, and you've turned it into a dead card.

### Reducing the risk of dead cards

The reverse also earns virtual card advantage: avoiding dead cards, or finding a use for cards that
would otherwise be dead.

Duke's illustration. A game drags fifteen or twenty turns. Neither player benefits from land beyond
the sixth or seventh. Johnny sits frustrated with every useless land he draws. Jenny uses **Sigiled
Starfish** to scry extra lands and low-impact cards to the bottom, making her draw steps more
effective and digging toward cards that matter.

**The Starfish has never technically given Jenny card advantage.** It only changed which cards ended
up in her hand. It gave her *virtual* card advantage by helping her avoid dead cards.

### Low-impact and high-impact cards

Often a quick glance tells you some cards have been outclassed. **Bellows Lizard** is very likely to
be outclassed and very unlikely to change a game's outcome. It's a **low-impact card**, which is why,
despite its affordable cost, it's often left on the sidelines.

**Dragon Fodder** technically provides a two-for-one. But if one player casts Dragon Fodder and the
other plays **Shivan Dragon**, who came out on top? Shivan Dragon is a **high-impact card**, the type
very likely to swing a game if unanswered.

**Emphasizing high-impact cards is another way to earn virtual card advantage.**

> It's not always the player who technically gains card advantage who wins. One player might be
> casting Weave Fates until the cows come home, but if they're only drawing into Bellows Lizards and
> Dragon Fodders, one high-impact card like Shivan Dragon on the other side can make it all look
> silly.

## The closing position

Understand the theory and be able to count card advantage in simple exchanges. But recognize the
times when the technical view isn't enough and virtual card advantage matters just as much.

Competitive Magic runs on thin margins, and small sources of card advantage — a two-for-one trade,
blanking an opponent's card — can easily be the difference between a loss and a win.

## Notes for use

- **The counting rule to internalize:** a spell costs you one card to cast, so subtract that before
  claiming advantage. Draw-one spells break even. This is the single most common beginner
  miscount.
- **The two-tier structure is the real content.** Tier one is arithmetic and always available. Tier
  two, virtual card advantage, is where actual games get decided, and Duke is explicit that the
  arithmetic can point the wrong way.
- **Cross-reference to Block 2.** Duke attributes the canonical definition to **Eric Taylor**, whose
  *it's about TIME* is `block-2-core-theory-paired/04-its-about-time-1997-08-11.md`. Taylor in turn
  credits **Brian Weissman** as the godfather of the concept, and Flores credits **Robert Hahn's
  "Schools of Magic"** with first naming it. Three separate attributions for the same idea, which
  tells you how early and how collectively it formed.
- **"Blanking cards during deckbuilding"** is the seed of sideboard theory and connects forward to
  installments 39 and 40, and to Flores's principle 8 in
  `block-2-core-theory-paired/02-eight-core-principles-of-whos-the-beatdown-2012-03-16.md`.
- **Low-impact versus high-impact** is Duke's plain-language version of what Flores calls the
  Philosophy of Fire problem: whether a card that trades down still did its job.
