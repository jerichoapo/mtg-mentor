# Playing in a Grand Prix: Part I

**Reid Duke — Level One, May 18, 2015**

---

## The opening

> **Twelve years later, I still remember my first Magic Grand Prix just like it was yesterday. In fact,
> I'm quite sure the memory will stick with me until the day I die.**

> **While other kids my age waited all year for their vacation to Disney World, or a chance to watch
> their favorite sports team, once I'd played in my first big Magic tournament, the greatest joy I
> could've possibly imagined was simply playing in another one.**

> **Attending a Grand Prix has the potential to be a truly excellent experience. However, big tournaments
> can be hectic, stressful, and emotionally charged.**

---

## Setting goals

**The three reasons people enter big tournaments:**

1. **To perform well** — prize money, or the challenge of playing your best.
2. **To learn and grow as a player**, possibly to perform well in future tournaments.
3. **To have fun.**

> **None of these motivations are better or worse than the others. Personally, the reason I attend
> tournaments is pretty much an equal part of all three.**

**And why they're not independent:**

> **The three are closely related. Achieving all of them depends, first and foremost, on having a healthy
> and positive attitude. In my experience, it's much easier to play well when you're having fun. Getting
> overly frustrated will usually ruin your chance of taking anything of value from the experience.**

### Why result-based goals are a trap

> **Your goals shouldn't be completely rooted in your final ranking. Magic is a challenging game with
> many random elements; no one in the world can do well in every tournament they enter.**

**Two specific risks of a hard-line goal like "make Day Two":**

1. **You might become stressed and disappointed if you fall short**, which means **missing out on the
   other good things your tournament experience has to offer.**
2. **You make it harder to exceed the goal.** At 7-1 you've already met it — **however, you're doing very
   well and might be in striking distance of a great finish. If anything, you should increase your
   efforts at this point instead of being satisfied with what you've already done.**

### Process goals

> **The most helpful goals are the ones you can work on regardless of how you're doing in the
> tournament.**

His examples:

- **"My goal is not to get distracted when I'm playing."**
- **"My goal is to be very careful with my mulligan decisions."**
- **"My goal is to forget as few of my triggers as possible."**

### Benchmarks, for goal-oriented people

> **Some people, myself included, are very goal-oriented and need targets to perform their best. If
> you're like me, I recommend using benchmarks.**

- **"My goal is to win every match I play, but I'll be proud of myself if I go 6-3 or better."**
- **"If I don't win at least half my matches, I'll consider switching decks for next time."**

### Duke's own goals for GP Las Vegas

> 1. **I'll be proud of myself if I can win at least nine matches** (not counting byes).
> 2. **I want to make the best use of my sideboard that I possibly can.**
> 3. **I want to stay focused and not get frustrated if I go on a losing streak.**
> 4. **I want to play out every round, even if I'm out of contention for a prize.**

*(Note: one result benchmark, three process goals.)*

## Being at your best

**The problem he observes:**

> **I see players practice so hard for a tournament. They care so much and want to do well so badly. And
> yet some of these players, when the tournament gets close, destroy their hard work by not taking good
> care of themselves.**

> Maybe they stay out too late and don't get enough sleep. **Maybe they show up late to their round and
> receive a game loss penalty.** In one way or another, **they squander their chances for the wrong
> reasons.**

> **For all the good that practicing does, it won't help you unless you show up on the day of the
> tournament ready to play your best.**

### Physical

> **Every human being needs sleep, food, and water. Get to bed early, sleep well, and leave time for
> breakfast before you start playing.**

**Individual needs matter too:** coffee in the morning, **a medication you need to bring or take before
leaving**, a particular song or ritual that gets you in the zone.

**Duke's own:**

> **I like to stretch a little in the morning, especially if I've come off an airplane or a long car ride
> the previous day. Otherwise I'll feel uncomfortable sitting for so many hours straight and that can
> negatively impact how well I play.**

**And the multi-day framing:**

> **In a perfect world you should turn your eye toward feeling good at the tournament a couple of days in
> advance. I have one health-conscious friend who claims that the amount of sleep he gets on Wednesday
> and Thursday nights has more to do with his tournament performance on Saturday than how much he sleeps
> the night before.**

> **These are long days, so if a small improvement in your diet, or a small amount of cardiovascular
> exercise, can increase your physical endurance, these things can make a difference.**

### Mental

> **Anything you can do on Friday night that will make Saturday morning less hectic is a good idea.**

**The checklist:** **double-check that you're registered.** If it's Constructed, **have your deck built
and your bag packed.** If coordinating travel, **make sure everyone is on the same page.**

> **You already have enough on your plate playing the tournament. You don't need extra stress like
> rushing to find a card you need or waiting on a friend who thought the tournament started an hour later
> than it did.**

**On travel companions — unusually direct advice:**

> **Don't choose travel companions who won't allow you to be at your best. If a friend is doing something
> that bothers you, bring up the issue with them. If they're still doing it, consider not traveling with
> that person next time.**

> **You don't have to stop being their friend, but if you're routinely showing up at tournaments overtired
> or stressed out, you should most definitely make some kind of change.**

**On compartmentalizing:**

> **Any problems you've been having at home, work, or school — set them on the shelf when you leave for
> the tournament and pick them back up when you get home. If you've decided to take the weekend off to
> play Magic, then that's exactly what you should do.**

### Cut off practice

> **Playing a few games in the hotel room the night before is fine as long as you're having fun. Long
> hours of play dragging on toward midnight is usually a bad idea. It can cause burnout and frustration,
> and you'd rather go in fresh.**

**And the specific danger for Constructed:**

> **You might wind up second-guessing yourself and changing cards in your deck or switching strategies
> entirely. In my experience, this is typically more harmful than helpful.**

> **I like to play a lot Monday through Thursday before a tournament, and then take Friday as a light
> day.**

## Dealing with your opponents

> **You'll truly face all kinds of opponents, especially at a Grand Prix.** Any age, gender, level of
> experience, from any part of the world, **sometimes not even speaking the same language as you. The only
> thing that's certain is that they'll love Magic, just like you do.**

> **Don't let the thick air of competition make it hard to breathe.** Sure, you're trying to beat each
> other, **but the simple fact that you're sitting across from someone and playing Magic is a pretty good
> reason to be friends — at least in my book.**

> **There's definitely no reason to ever feel intimidated by an opponent, or to have a serious conflict
> with them.**

**And the practical handling of a bad one:**

> **If they're nice to you — which the vast, vast majority of your opponents will be — you can be nice to
> them. If they're mean, annoying, or overly competitive, you can simply tune out anything that bothers
> you and just play Magic. It's easy; I've done it this way for over a decade.**

### Communication

> **Playing against a stranger in a tournament really isn't much different from playing with a friend at
> home. The most important thing is just to communicate well, and make sure both players are very clear
> about what's going on at all times.**

**He points back to installment 25's "proper way to cast a spell":**

> **Take that level of precision to everything you do in the game, and insist that your opponent does the
> same.**

- **Announce everything clearly.**
- **If you're unsure exactly what your opponent is doing, ask for clarification.**
- **If a turn is shaping up to be complicated, take it slowly, one step at a time.**

### Life totals — the extra emphasis

> **You and your opponent should always be crystal clear on both players' life totals.**

- **Keep track of both life totals with pen and paper.**
- **Verbally confirm any time there's a change.**
- **If you see your opponent has an incorrect life total written down, you are required, by the rules, to
  clear up any confusion.**

> **The last thing you want is to make a decision thinking you're at 8 life when you're really at 6.
> Worse yet, you don't want a life total discrepancy to go by for a few turns — until it might be too
> late to correct it.**

## Playing by the rules

> **Remember that you are playing in a tournament, so the rules will be enforced strictly and precisely.
> Thankfully, being friendly and playing precisely are not mutually exclusive, so long as everyone is on
> the same page.**

**On take-backs:**

> **It goes without saying that there are no take-backs in tournaments. If you make a mistake and your
> opponent holds you to it, they aren't being a jerk, they're just playing by the rules. Never fault
> someone for doing exactly what the rules say.**

> **If you don't want to be a cutthroat player, you certainly don't have to be. However, you do need to be
> prepared, when you enter a tournament, to play seriously and precisely.**

### Cheating

**Duke's balanced framing:**

> **Cheating does happen in Magic tournaments, and it would be naive to think otherwise. Thankfully, it's
> not something you should live in fear of, and I feel confident saying it won't ruin your tournament
> experience.**

> **Most players want to play an honest game anyway, and those that don't are kept in check by the
> vigilance of judges and other players.**

**The two protective habits:**

1. **Shuffle your opponent's deck.** **Any time Player A shuffles their own deck — before the game, or
   after a Windswept Heath — Player B is allowed to shuffle it as well. I encourage you to shuffle your
   opponents' decks.**
2. **Clarify anything that seems off.** **If something seems fishy, don't give your opponent the benefit
   of the doubt — clarify the situation.**

**And the important distinction:**

> **In either case, it's important to remember that you're not accusing your opponent of cheating.
> Accusing someone of cheating can be taken very seriously. You're just taking the normal measures that
> are expected of tournament players.**

## Notes for use

- **The goal-setting section is the most transferable content**, and it's a genuine insight: result goals
  both punish you for variance and cap your ambition once met. Process goals — don't get distracted, be
  careful with mulligans, don't miss triggers — are workable in every round regardless of record. Duke's
  own list is one benchmark plus three process goals, which is a good ratio to copy.
- **"Practice won't help unless you show up ready" is the whole self-care argument in one line**, and the
  Wednesday-and-Thursday sleep observation is the version most people haven't heard.
- **The Friday-night rule is specific and worth adopting:** stop practicing, don't touch your decklist,
  make Saturday morning boring. Late-night second-guessing of a deck is a well-known way to arrive with a
  worse list than you tested.
- **The travel-companion advice is unusually blunt for a strategy column** and correct — the people you
  travel with materially affect your sleep and stress, and therefore your results.
- **Shuffling your opponent's deck is framed exactly right:** it's a routine expectation, not an
  accusation. Making it your default removes the awkwardness of doing it only when suspicious.
- **The life-total instruction is the most concrete rules point:** track both, confirm changes verbally,
  and you are *required* to correct a discrepancy you notice — including one in your favour.
- **Cross-reference:** installment 25 (Going Through the Motions) is the precision standard this invokes;
  installment 44 covers judges and in-tournament logistics; installment 27's warnings about frustration
  and over-respecting opponents apply directly; installment 45 closes the course on the learning theme.
