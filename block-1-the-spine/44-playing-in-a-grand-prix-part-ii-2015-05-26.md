# Playing in a Grand Prix: Part II

**Reid Duke — Level One, May 26, 2015**

---

# The role of judges

> **Once the tournament begins, judges are in charge of running things. In essence, their job is to help
> the players have a positive experience.**

**Three functions:** **making sure the tournament runs smoothly, answering players' questions both inside
and outside of gameplay, and enforcing policies designed to guide the tournament toward its most fair
possible result.**

**The three documents judges work from:** the **Comprehensive Rules of Magic**, the **Tournament Floor
Rules**, and the **Infraction Procedure Guide** — around three hundred pages.

> **The fact that judges are there to help us means that we players don't have to know all of this.**

## Judges are your friends

> **They want to help you. They want to answer your rules questions and help you learn the game. They
> want to protect you against cheating or being the victim of unfortunate misunderstandings. Finally,
> they want you to have fun and come back to play again.**

**The barrier he identifies:**

> **New tournament players are sometimes slow to ask judges for help. Perhaps they haven't gotten over the
> hall monitor being mean to them in elementary school — I really have no idea.**

> **Calling a judge during a match isn't "tattling," nor does it mean you're accusing your opponent of
> cheating. Judges are a wonderful resource, and it's a big mistake to not take advantage of what they
> have to offer.**

## What judges can help with

1. **Finding things.** Where to register, where to find pairings, where the bathroom is.
2. **Rules questions**, during a game or between rounds. **Duke's habit: any time I encounter a question
   at home, I make a note to ask a judge about it at my next Grand Prix.**
3. **Bathroom breaks.** **A judge will give you permission to go, and will even give you a time extension
   for your match.**
4. **Resolving problems in your match** — a disagreement, misunderstanding, or miscommunication.
   *"I attacked for 3 with my Tarmogoyf last turn, but now I realize it should have been a 4/5."*
5. **Illegal actions by either player.** Someone drew seven after a mulligan; someone played two lands in
   a turn; someone cast a spell without proper mana; someone flipped a card while shuffling the
   opponent's deck.

> **These things usually aren't big deals, but a judge needs to know about them. They'll take the proper
> measures to help you finish the game as fairly as possible, and decide whether a Warning or a stiffer
> penalty needs to be issued.**

## Warnings

> **If you attend a Grand Prix you'll see judges issue Warnings. You might receive one yourself. This is
> because Warnings are common, and they're no big deal.**

> **A Warning is basically an official statement of a judge saying, "Please try not to do this again."
> However, it does not come with a penalty attached.**

**Why they're tracked, which is the useful explanation:**

> **Everybody makes mistakes while playing Magic — everybody. Everybody has accidentally tapped the wrong
> lands for a spell, or accidentally knocked a card off the top of their library.**

> **However, if a player is tapping the wrong lands five or six times in every match they play, it's
> possible they're doing it intentionally to gain an advantage. Tracking Warnings helps judges identify
> and investigate players like this.**

**The escalation:**

> **If you receive a Warning, don't sweat it. If you receive two, try to tighten up your play, but don't
> let it upset you. If you receive three in the same day for the same infraction, you might get a
> penalty. If you have a long-term pattern of getting far too many, you might get a penalty — but this
> ought to be easy to avoid.**

## When to call a judge

> **If you're deciding whether or not to call a judge, just go ahead and call one.**

### Don't fix problems yourself

> Let's say your opponent puts a creature in the graveyard when it wasn't supposed to die, and nobody
> notices until the next turn. **If you were playing at home you might just put it back and continue.
> You should not do this in a tournament. Always let judges handle situations like this.**

### Don't take your opponent's word

**Duke's three examples, with the correct answers:**

| Situation | Their claim | **The actual rule** |
|---|---|---|
| **A.** You cast a spell on their face-down creature and aren't sure if you may look at it | — | **You are allowed to look at it** |
| **B.** You mulligan, then lay out seven cards face-down | *"You're forced to mulligan to five"* | **If you haven't looked at them, you probably won't have to** |
| **C.** You tap five lands, then change your mind before revealing anything | — | **You will be allowed to untap your lands** |

> **Never take your opponent's word on something that seems unclear. Your opponent does not have your
> best interest in mind. They might lie to you. Even more likely, they might just be mistaken. In either
> case, there's no reason to let it impact your tournament.**

## How to call a judge

**Step 1 — tell your opponent first.** *"I have a rules question, so I'm going to call a judge,"* or
*"I noticed you only had one white mana when you cast End Hostilities, so I'm going to call a judge."*

**Step 2 — raise your hand and yell "Judge!"** **Yell pretty loudly and keep your hand raised, because
tournaments are often crowded and noisy.**

**Step 3 — explain exactly what happened**, with **no details left out, and none of your own speculations
added.**

> **The right way:** *"John tapped these five lands and cast End Hostilities. Then he put his creatures in
> the graveyard. I picked up my creatures, but before I put them in the graveyard I noticed that John only
> had one white mana, so I put them down again and called for a judge."*

> **The wrong way:** *"My opponent tried to cheat me! He knew the only way he could win was playing End
> Hostilities, so he tried to do it illegally without me knowing!"*

**Step 4 — the ruling.** It may be immediate, or take time if there's disagreement, if the judge
investigates, or confers with another judge. **It may include an in-game fix (rewinding to before the
spell), a penalty (a Warning for improperly casting), and a time extension.**

**Step 5 — appeal, if needed.** **Let the first judge finish speaking, then say "I'd like to appeal that
ruling." The head judge will weigh in, and their decision will be final.**

## Be nice to judges

> **Judges are people, and specifically they're people who volunteer an almost inconceivable amount of
> their time to the game we love. Without judges, we couldn't have tournaments the way we have them
> now.**

> **They can also make mistakes. Just like players, judges range widely in experience. This is part of
> why there is an appeal system. Don't be afraid to appeal a ruling, but always do so respectfully.**

> **Judging can sometimes be a thankless job. Any time there's a dispute, the judge has to make a decision
> that will likely make one or both players unhappy. If a ruling doesn't go your way, roll with the
> punches, don't take it personally, and certainly don't take it out on the judge.**

# The clock

**The structure as of 2015:**

> **You get 50 minutes to play a best two-out-of-three match.** You cannot begin before the clock starts,
> and cannot ignore it when it reaches zero.

**At time:** any time extension you received is played out, **and then you play five additional turns.**

> **If it's your turn when time is called, then when you say "go," your opponent's turn is turn one, your
> next turn is turn two, and so on. If the game doesn't end before the end of the fifth turn, the game is
> a draw.**

**Match resolution:** **if game two is a draw, whichever player won game one wins the match. If your match
ends with game one or game three being a draw, the entire match is a draw.**

**Points:** **3 for a win, 1 for a draw, 0 for a loss.**

## Why draws are worse than they look

**The arithmetic:**

> **When there's a winner and a loser, three total match points are awarded. When the match is a draw,
> only two are. You'd rather win one match and lose one match than draw two matches in a row.**

**And the structural reason it's worse still:**

> **Grand Prix are very large and very top-heavy. Many thousands enter, but only a few hundred make Day
> Two, and only a fraction of those win prizes. You're looking to hit a home run, not simply to stay in
> the middle of the pack.**

> **Certain cut-offs, like the one to qualify for Day Two, are based on your number of wins — you need
> seven. So for those purposes, a draw is the same as a loss.**

> **From experience, I can tell you that it feels lousy to miss Day Two with a record of 6-2-1.**

## Playing at a fair pace

> **Fifty minutes is a pretty long time, so it's not as though you're playing Spit or some other
> lightning-speed game. However, you should make every effort to finish your matches on time.**

**Your obligations:** **play at a brisk pace, don't take more than a few minutes to shuffle and sideboard,
and make sure your opponent does the same.**

**The rules Duke reminds you are actually written down:**

> **You must play at a fair pace at all times, you can't take too long on any one decision, and you cannot
> slow down your pace of play because you want the game to be a draw.**

> **The guideline is that you shouldn't take much longer than three minutes to sideboard and shuffle
> between games**, although this is not a hard-and-fast rule.

**And the point that it applies even when time isn't tight:**

> **Even if it's early in the match, or it looks like you'll finish in plenty of time, both players still
> need to play at a fair pace. If the rules say you get a certain amount of time to make a decision, then
> taking more than that gives you an unfair advantage.**

> **Don't rush your opponents, but don't let them take more time than the rules allow, and don't let them
> put you at risk of running out of time.**

# Playing out all of your rounds

**The structure:** **Day One is nine rounds of Swiss.** Your record carries into Day Two, **so 9-0 or 8-1
is fantastic, but once you lose three rounds you won't be making Day Two regardless.**

> **Nonetheless, I encourage you to stay in the tournament and play out all the rounds no matter what
> your record is** — unless you'd be missing a side event you want, or **unless you're feeling sick or are
> otherwise miserable continuing to play.**

**The argument:**

> **Tournament experience is tremendously valuable, and very hard to come by. Playing games at home, or
> at your local store, really cannot simulate the experience of playing in a Grand Prix.**

> **At a big tournament you're paired against new opponents in a highly competitive setting. Everyone
> brought their best deck and is trying their hardest to beat you. It's the best practice you can get.**

**The sunk-cost framing, used correctly:**

> **Even at 1-3 you should make the most of the day. You've already travelled to the event, paid the entry
> fee, built your deck, and blocked a day out of your calendar. Why let the chance to gain tournament
> experience go to waste?**

> **If you play out the rounds you'll inevitably learn more about the format, learn more about your deck,
> and you can continue to work toward any goals you set for yourself before the tournament.**

# Reflection

> **Always look at a big tournament as a learning experience. Whether you win or lose, there will always
> have been some things you did well and some things you can work on.**

**The tension he names:**

> **It can be hard to balance healthy reflection with your desire to do well in the tournament. If you
> make a big mistake in round six, you certainly do want to remember it and learn from it. However, you
> don't want to be thinking about it while you're playing rounds seven, eight, and nine.**

**The solution — schedule it:**

> **I recommend blocking out some time after the tournament for reflection. The car ride home on Sunday or
> Monday night is perfect.**

> **Just as good as personal reflection, you might be able to talk things over with friends. What did
> everyone learn, what were they proud of themselves for, what would they change for next time?**

**And the second benefit of scheduling it, which is the real insight:**

> **If you block off time after the tournament for reflection, it also means you're free to keep a clear
> head while you're playing. Focus on the task at hand, not on what happened last round or what record
> you want at the end of the day.**

> **If you make a mistake, brush it off and make the best of the situation. Revisit it only once
> everything is said and done.**

## Notes for use

- **⚠️ Tournament structure has changed since 2015.** Round counts, the Day Two cutoff, round length, and
  the Grand Prix system itself have all been revised. **Treat "50 minutes," "nine rounds," and "seven wins
  for Day Two" as historical.** The *principles* — draws are worse than they look, play briskly, call
  judges freely — all still hold.
- **The scheduled-reflection idea is the most valuable content here and generalizes well beyond Magic.**
  Deferring analysis to a fixed time after the event does double duty: you actually do the reflection, and
  you stop it from contaminating your play in later rounds. Most people do the reverse — ruminate during,
  forget after.
- **"Never take your opponent's word" is the practical rules advice**, and Duke's framing is right: they
  needn't be dishonest to be wrong. His three examples all have counterintuitive correct answers, which is
  exactly why asking matters.
- **The right-way/wrong-way judge call is a good template:** state observable facts in sequence, omit
  motive and accusation. That gets you a better ruling and avoids escalating a mistake into an
  accusation.
- **Warnings being tracked-but-harmless is worth understanding** before your first one, since receiving
  one feels much worse than it is. The tracking exists to catch patterns, not to punish single errors.
- **The draw arithmetic is genuinely underappreciated:** two draws are strictly worse than a win and a
  loss, and against a win-count cutoff a draw is simply a loss. That should make you play faster.
- **Cross-reference:** installment 43 is the first half (goals, preparation, opponents); installment 25
  (Going Through the Motions) is the precision standard that prevents most Warnings; installment 45
  (Becoming a Better Player) closes the course on the reflection theme.
