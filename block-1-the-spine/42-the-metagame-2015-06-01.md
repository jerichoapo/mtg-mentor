# The Metagame

**Reid Duke — Level One, June 1, 2015**

---

## What the metagame is

> **The metagame — the game outside the game — is all of the decisions, resources, and information that,
> while not explicitly part of the game, are nonetheless important to Magic.**

> The game we play is called Magic: The Gathering, governed by precise rules concerning deckbuilding,
> gameplay, and tens of thousands of cards. **The metagame is everything in Magic that's not defined by
> the rules.**

> **It's the deck archetypes that are popular, the new combo everyone's talking about, and the hot
> sideboard strategy for the Esper mirror. It's everything the players know, and everything the players
> know the other players know.**

**And the self-referential line:**

> **As you read, this article is becoming part of the metagame.**

### The war-game analogy

Duke imagines a game with three factions — **α, β, and ω**.

> **To master the game you'd need a deep understanding of all three. However, if all your friends always
> play β, then for your metagame it's in your best interest to practice only against β.**

- **If your friend Sandy always uses flying units, this is an important aspect of your metagame.**
- **If your friend Tommy has a poor internet connection and moves slowly during battles, this is an
  important aspect of your metagame.**
- **If you branch out and play new opponents, your metagame changes and you'll need to adjust your
  practice methods and strategy.**

*(Note the range: the metagame includes not only what decks people play but how individuals behave.)*

---

## What decks are you likely to face?

> **The most basic, and arguably most important, aspect of the Magic metagame is the relative popularity
> of different deck archetypes.**

> If you could show up to your ten-player FNM **knowing that three players will play Abzan, three Esper,
> two Mono-Red, and two Green Devotion, you could leverage that into an advantage by building your deck
> accordingly. To think ahead about what decks your opponents are likely to bring is called predicting
> the metagame.**

**The limits at scale:**

> **For a thousand-player Grand Prix it's impossible to predict what every individual will do. Even if
> you could, it would be impossible to predict which of those individuals you'll be paired against.**

> **However, it is absolutely possible to make a broad prediction like "between ten and twenty percent of
> players will be playing Esper Control." Educated predictions like this are tremendously helpful.**

---

## How to predict the metagame

### Frank Karsten's "winner's circle metagame"

**Duke credits Hall of Fame pro Frank Karsten with a method still used by many players and writers.**
Examine recent tournament results — for example, all Standard Grand Prix in the last two months — and
assign each archetype:

| Finish | Points |
|---|---|
| **1st** | **6** |
| **2nd** | **5** |
| **3rd–4th** | **4** |
| **5th–8th** | **3** |
| **9th–16th** | **2** |

> **Then average out the data and assign each archetype a percentage.** These numbers are meant to help
> predict what decks will be played and perform well the following weekend.

**Why it works:**

> **What decks people have played in the past few weeks is a great indicator of what they're going to
> play this weekend. Moreover, the decks people see doing well when they look at tournament results are
> also the ones they're likely to pick up for their next event.**

**And Duke's honest caveat about the numbers:**

> **Predicting a metagame is not an exact science. While the numbers can tell us a lot, in the end we're
> trying to guess at what a collection of individuals will do.**

> **There's no mathematical reason why a first-place finish at Grand Prix Toronto ought to be valued
> exactly twice as highly as a tenth-place finish at Grand Prix Shanghai played two weeks later.**

### The five factors

**1. What archetypes have been popular lately?**

> **For most things, what's happened in the past is the best indicator of what will happen in the future.
> Last week's metagame will have naturally accounted for most of the important factors. To ignore it
> would be a mistake.**

**2. What archetypes have been winning?**

> **Anyone undecided on what deck to play is very likely to pick up a deck that's had success recently.
> For some it won't be the exact deck that got first place last weekend, but for others it will.**

> **Put greater weight on better finishes and on more recent results.**

**3. What have the loudest voices been saying?**

**This is the most interesting factor because of how Duke frames it:**

> This could be a respected player in your region, but most often it means **the popular columnists for
> the popular strategy websites. When Patrick Chapin speaks, the world listens.**

> If he or a colleague writes "Mono-Red is great, everyone should play it," **that's well worth noting.
> Whether you and I actually think Mono-Red is great is hardly relevant. The fact that it was said at all
> will make Mono-Red a larger portion of the field the following weekend.**

**4. Is card availability a problem?**

> **Are some decks prohibitively difficult to obtain? The older the format, the more important this will
> be. The higher the level of competition, the less important it will be.**

> **At the World Championship every player will find a way to get the cards they want. For a low-stakes
> Legacy event on Magic Online or at a local store, budget decks might be major players.**

**5. Any other applicable trends?**

- **Midrange decks like Abzan tend to be slightly over-represented, because many people find them more
  fun to play than extreme strategies like Mono-Red.**
- **Fast strategies are slightly over-represented on Magic Online, because some players like to burn
  through lots of matches as quickly as possible.**

**His practical advice on doing this well:**

> **I find it helpful to discuss it with my friends before a tournament. Where my own predictions often
> fall wide of the mark, a compilation of several educated guesses usually does pretty well.**

> **I've found predicting the metagame to be a skill one can cultivate through practice and experience.**

## What to do with a predicted metagame

**The three aspects of Constructed tournament preparation:** **archetype choice, fine tuning, and
practice.**

### Archetype choice — the option Duke mostly declines

> Once in a while all factors line up and lead you to predict a very extreme metagame — **say a single
> archetype at thirty percent of the field.** It might then be best to **make a metagame deck choice that
> matches up well against the expected field and absolutely crushes the most popular archetype.**

> **Some players have had a lot of success with such a technique, but it's rarely an approach I choose
> personally. Particularly with all the uncertainties, I prefer to focus on the latter two aspects —
> mastering my deck of choice and having the best possible decklist for it.**

### Fine tuning — the option he always takes

> **What I will most definitely do is choose some of my cards with the predicted metagame in mind. If I'm
> playing Abzan and I predict a lot of Mono-Red, you'd better believe I'm going to pack three or four
> Drown in Sorrows in my sideboard.**

### Practice — and what to practice against

> **Your predicted metagame should help guide your practice sessions.**

**Three targets, not one:**

1. **The decks you expect to be most popular.**
2. **New breakout decks you haven't played against before.**
3. **Oddball decks that are different from what you're used to.**

**Why the third matters:**

> **Nobody knows what to do the first time they play against Jeskai Ascendancy Combo. It's an unpopular
> deck, and one you can typically ignore. However, if there's new information leading you to believe it
> might be heavily played at your next tournament, a little practice in the matchup would be tremendously
> helpful.**

## Information cascades

**Duke summarizes Patrick Chapin's "Information Cascades in Magic," which he calls seminal.**

> **Sometimes the metagame reaches a point where what players are doing is determined more by what other
> players are doing than it is by what are actually the best strategies.**

### The upward cascade

> Chapin begins with an archetype that history determined **was not a particularly good one. However, at
> the time it was extremely popular and lots of players brought it to tournaments.**

> **With enough popularity, even poor decks will break through to Top 8s and win tournaments. More
> players see the deck putting up good results and choose to pick it up. In this way a deck's popularity
> feeds on itself.**

### The downward cascade — Duke's own addition

> **The opposite can also be true. I believe great archetypes and strategies often "get lost" from the
> tournament scene. People brush them off simply because they haven't seen other players using them
> recently.**

> **The archetype might be great, but if it's not popular enough it might fail to have breakthrough
> finishes, and its lack of popularity will feed on itself.**

**The information asymmetry that causes it:**

> **It's easy to see with a quick look at tournament coverage which decks are making Top 8s. It's
> typically not easy to see which decks have strong or weak win rates, and which ones have untapped
> potential.**

### The Jane Doe scenario

**Duke's fictional but, he says, quite realistic example:**

> Jane Doe builds a mono-blue beatdown deck and takes **third at a Grand Prix. Her deck gets mentioned in
> coverage but is largely overshadowed by the winning deck, and by a second deck that put three copies in
> the Top 8.**

> **Jane Doe is not a famous player, and through sheer coincidence none of the most popular columnists
> choose to feature her deck. In the next two Grand Prix, a small dedicated group pilot mono-blue to good
> overall results, but none break through to a Top 8.**

> **Under these circumstances mono-blue could well be one of the best archetypes in the format, but a
> month after Jane's great finish it would most certainly be an unpopular deck.**

**The opportunity:**

> **Once in a while, finding one of these "lost decks" can be a great way to get an edge in a tournament.
> It's led me to success a handful of times in my career, especially in formats like Legacy, which are
> slow to change.**

### Chapin's point

> **The major point Patrick Chapin was trying to drive home is that there are times to listen to the
> community and to tournament results, and there are times to think for yourself and make your own
> decisions.**

> **The only thing that's certain is that you should always keep your eyes open and pay attention to what
> other players are doing.**

## Notes for use

- **The winner's circle scoring is a concrete, reproducible method** and still worth running: weight
  recent high finishes, average, convert to expected share. Duke's caveat about its arbitrariness is fair
  but doesn't undermine its usefulness as a prior.
- **Factor 3 — the loudest voices — is the sharpest insight in the installment**, and it's a
  reflexivity point: what matters is not whether the recommendation is *correct* but that it was *made*,
  because the recommendation itself changes the field. Metagame prediction is partly media analysis.
- **Information cascades cut both ways, and Duke's downward version is the actionable half.** Popularity
  is self-reinforcing in both directions, so good decks can disappear for lack of a famous pilot. The
  exploit is looking at win rates rather than Top 8 counts — data that is harder to get and therefore
  less priced in.
- **Note his personal preference:** he declines hard metagame calls in favor of mastering one deck and
  tuning it. That's consistent with installment 41's conclusion that piloting beats selection, and it's a
  variance argument — a metagame call is a bet on a prediction, mastery is not.
- **Practicing against oddball decks is the underrated advice.** The first time you face an unfamiliar
  deck you play it badly regardless of how good your deck is; a small amount of exposure removes most of
  that penalty.
