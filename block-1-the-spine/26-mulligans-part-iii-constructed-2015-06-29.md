# Mulligans Part III: Constructed

**Reid Duke — Level One, June 29, 2015**

---

## Why Constructed is harder

**All Limited decks share common characteristics.** Some are faster, some slower, some have bombs. **But
they're predictable** in that they virtually all **win by attacking with creatures, managing the board
with removal, and accumulating small advantages.**

> **This is not the case in Constructed.**

> **Constructed decks can be wildly different. They employ completely different game plans, and can
> range from blisteringly fast to glacially slow. Cards that are great against one Constructed deck
> might do virtually nothing against another.**

**Hence the mulligan question is much more complicated here.**

---

# Knowing your own deck

> **Mulligan decisions in Constructed are all about context — the way your deck matches up against your
> opponent's.**

**The first step is understanding your own deck:** What is your game plan? What tools do you need? What
are the costs of mulliganing, or of keeping a bad hand?

## Key cards

> **Sometimes the presence or absence of a particular card can change the value of your hand in an
> extreme way.**

### The extreme case: Modern Bogles

**The deck** puts auras like **Rancor** and **Daybreak Coronet** onto a hexproof creature like **Slippery
Bogle** or **Gladecover Scout**, beating down quickly **without ever being vulnerable to creature
removal.**

**It plays eight one-drop hexproof creatures and badly needs one in the opening hand.**

> **When playing Bogles against an unknown opponent, you should mulligan every seven- and six-card hand
> that doesn't have a Slippery Bogle or Gladecover Scout.**

**The mulligan hand** — Razorverge Thicket, Windswept Heath, Rancor, Ethereal Armor, Daybreak Coronet,
Unflinching Courage, Path to Exile — **has a good mix of lands and spells, but is lacking a key card.**

**And the inverse, which matters just as much:**

> **When the key card is present, you'll need to loosen your standards a little. You don't want to
> mulligan a functional hand with a Slippery Bogle only to see a six-card hand that doesn't have one.**

**Two keeps he gives:**

- Forest, Plains, Horizon Canopy, **Gladecover Scout ×3**, Rancor
- Razorverge Thicket, **Slippery Bogle**, Rancor, Ethereal Armor, Daybreak Coronet ×2, Unflinching
  Courage

> **Neither is a perfect hand; each will probably need a little help from the top of the library.
> However, the presence of the key card makes both worth keeping.**

### The moderate case: Standard Green Devotion

**A ramp deck** generating lots of mana to cast ultra-powerful spells **starting around turn four.** You
want a mana creature like **Elvish Mystic** or **Sylvan Caryatid** for a jump start.

> **You don't have to automatically mulligan a hand without these, but their presence or absence is a
> very important factor. If you get a borderline hand without one, you can feel fairly comfortable
> throwing it back.**

**The mulligan:** Forest ×4, Polukranos World Eater, Genesis Hydra, Hornet Queen. **A good mix of lands
and spells, but no acceleration and simply too slow.**

**The six-card keep:** Forest ×3, Nykthos Shrine to Nyx, Elvish Mystic, Sylvan Caryatid.

> **This six-card hand has no heavy-hitter cards. However, everything is in place for you to win if you
> draw well.** With something like a **Whisperwood Elemental** on top, **you can cast it on turn three.
> Because you have your mana acceleration, keep this rather than mulliganing to five.**

**His side note on card quantity:** Green Devotion, **as a deck with a high mana curve, really needs its
resources, and will struggle to win when it mulligans to five.**

## Cheap cards, coming out fast

**What the key cards of both decks have in common: they're cheap.**

> **For most decks, the key cards — the ones most important to have in the opening hand — are the cards
> you want to cast in the early turns.** Your more expensive cards can come off the top and be cast
> later, **but if you miss out on making your ideal turn-one or turn-two play, you can never rewind the
> game to that point.**

> **Constructed Magic is fast and unforgiving. Coming out too slowly is an easy way to lose a game.**
> Put a premium on having your best early plays in your opening hand. **Mulligan more aggressively if
> either your deck or your opponent's deck is very fast.**

### The two-hand comparison

**Duke's best teaching device here.** Both hands have **three lands and four spells**, both feature cheap
spells, and both can kill the opponent's first few creatures. **One is a mulligan; the other is
excellent.**

**Hand A — Mono-Red Aggro**
Mountain, Mountain, Mana Confluence, Wild Slash, Atarka's Command, Lightning Strike, Lightning Strike

> You're seeking to end the game quickly, and **your advantage comes from coming out fast and flooding
> the board with creatures before the opponent can mount a defense. Unfortunately, this hand has no
> creatures.**

> **You desperately want to play a creature — hopefully two or three — in the first three turns, but you
> don't have many draw steps before you miss that window. To keep this hand would mean not playing to
> the strengths of your deck.** → **Mulligan.**

**Hand B — Abzan Control**
Sandsteppe Citadel ×2, Windswept Heath, Thoughtseize, Bile Blight, Hero's Downfall, Hero's Downfall

> A defensive deck seeking to drag the game out and grind small advantages. **Your goal is to survive to
> the mid and late game, where your powerful spells and two-for-one advantages let you win a topdeck
> war.**

> **This hand is perfect for that plan.** You dismantle the opponent's initial offense, **and the nature
> of your deck means you can be patient and wait for the top of your library to deliver the rest.** →
> **Keep.**

**The principle:** the same seven cards are good or bad depending entirely on **how many draw steps your
game plan gives you to fix them.**

---

# Knowing your opponent's deck

> **Knowing your opponent's deck is just as important as knowing your own.** While you go into a
> tournament with a game plan, **you might have to subtly or greatly adjust it according to how your
> deck matches up.**

**The information asymmetry across a match:**

> **In a tournament you often won't know what deck your opponent is playing until after you make your
> mulligan decision in game one. However, you will always know after sideboarding. Your sideboarding is
> a very important factor in your mulligan decisions.**

## Key cards against your opponent's deck

**Post-board, your key cards can be sideboard cards.** If you're in Modern and you've boarded in three
**Back to Nature** against Bogles, or three **Ancient Grudge** against Affinity:

> **You don't necessarily have to mulligan until you find these cards, but consider that a decent
> six-card hand with one will be much better than a mediocre seven-card hand without.**

> **If you're on the fence, the presence of sideboard cards like these can be a great tiebreaker in
> favor of mulliganing.**

## Fast matchups

> **You need to be prepared for what your opponent is going to throw at you.** It can be fine to keep a
> slow hand in certain matchups, **but once you know your opponent is playing Mono-Red Aggro, you should
> mulligan until you find a hand that won't simply get run over.**

## Slow matchups

> **In other matchups you can feel confident the game won't be ended by an early rush. Here it's good to
> loosen your standards in order to avoid the disadvantages of starting down a card.**

**The illustration.** On Esper Control, holding:

Temple of Enlightenment, Temple of Deceit, Polluted Delta, Swamp, Island, Negate, Dig Through Time

> **A hand you're thrilled to keep against an opposing control deck like Abzan or Blue-Black Control.
> However, a hand you should mulligan against Mono-Red.**

> **Everything depends on context, and the pace of the matchup is one of the most important factors.**

---

## The preview: Molten Vortex

**Duke calls it the type of card he's most excited to preview** — an effect **both extremely powerful and
extremely unique. A game changer.**

**Its two ancestors:** **Stormbind** and **Seismic Assault**, both tremendously powerful in their times.

**How it compares:** **probably favorably.** It's **much more affordable, cheap to cast and half the
price of Stormbind to activate.** Since **Seismic Assault is free to activate, it has more potential in
a combination deck** that dumps a hand full of lands at once. **But Molten Vortex is the better card
used in any kind of "fair" capacity** — and Duke immediately concedes "fair" may not be the right word,
since there are plenty of overpowered uses.

**Where it could go:**

- **With Life from the Loam** in Legacy or Modern.
- **A Temur-colored Standard deck** with **Satyr Wayfinder** and **Treasure Cruise**, offering the card
  volume to fuel it. **With the Wayfinder and Den Protector engine you could dump the Vortex into the
  graveyard and pick it up late once you're ready.**
- **No special support at all.** Against weenie creatures, **you only need to shoot down two or three
  before it has had a huge, worthwhile effect.**
- **Low-curve decks with no other use for extra lands.** If nothing costs more than 1 or 2 but games run
  seven or eight turns, **it wouldn't be uncommon to have three or four extra lands in hand to
  discard.** He calls it **a tremendous amount of reach for a red-based aggro deck.**

### Why it belongs in a mulligan article — the tension

**Duke previews it here for two reasons that pull against each other**, which is what makes the section
worth reading:

**1. It's a key card.** If you've put it in your deck, **you've likely sculpted your game plan around it,
and your deck functions substantially better when you draw it.** So **you'll be more inclined to
mulligan a hand without it.**

**2. It demands a large card quantity.** It **lets you use resources that would otherwise be
unimportant, but you can't take full advantage if you're low on cards in hand.** So **going down a card
is extra costly.**

> **You'll have to strike the right balance.**

## The closing

> **Mulligan decisions are about weighing pros and cons, risks and rewards. What's the cost of going
> down a card? What's the risk of keeping a subpar opening hand?**

> **In this three-part series I've been able to offer lots of questions, but few concrete answers.
> However, improving at mulligan decisions is largely about cultivating your instincts and getting a
> feel for what works and what does not. Keep working at it.**

## Notes for use

- **The key-card rule cuts both ways, and the second direction is the one people miss.** A key card
  doesn't only justify mulliganing hands without it — **it justifies keeping otherwise-marginal hands
  that have it.** Duke states this explicitly and it's the more actionable half.
- **The two-hand comparison is the core lesson of the installment.** Identical land/spell ratios,
  opposite verdicts. The variable is **how many draw steps your plan affords you to fix the hand.** Fast
  decks must have it now; slow decks can find it later.
- **The Molten Vortex tension is a genuinely useful pattern to recognize:** a card that is both a key
  card *and* card-hungry pushes your mulligan standards in opposite directions. Any build-around that
  needs volume has this property, and it means neither "mulligan until you find it" nor "keep everything"
  is right.
- **Sideboard cards as mulligan tiebreakers** is a small, concrete, immediately usable idea, and it only
  applies from game two onward — which is why knowing your opponent's deck changes the decision more
  than anything about your own.
- **Cross-reference:** installment 19 gives the Two-to-Five-Lands baseline and the general framework;
  this installment is where you deviate from it. Installment 41 (Choosing Your Deck) and installment 42
  (The Metagame) supply the knowledge of opponents' decks this depends on. Line-up theory (installment
  23) is the in-game continuation of "knowing your opponent's deck."
- **Duke's honest closing** — lots of questions, few answers, instincts over rules — is the correct
  summary of all three mulligan installments.
