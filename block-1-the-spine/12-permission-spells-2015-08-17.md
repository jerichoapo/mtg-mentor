# Permission Spells

**Reid Duke — Level One, August 17, 2015**

> Among the most feared spells in all of Magic. Challenging to use properly, terrifying to play
> against.

---

## What a permission spell is

**Permission spells counter an opposing spell as it is being cast.**

**Why the name.** Duke's dramatization: Johnny, staring down six untapped Islands, says "I'll cast
Siege Rhino… is that okay?" And Jenny, pondering, says either "Yes, that's okay," or "No, I'll cast
Clash of Wills for five."

**The original permission spell was called Counterspell**, and "counterspells" also refers to the
category generally.

> **"Counterspells" = "Permission Spells" = "Counters" = "Permission"**

**Duke's admission about why they confuse people:** the cards are unique in what they do. At early
points in his own career he remembers trying to use Counterspell to destroy a permanent already in
play (not legal), and trying to use it to stop an opponent playing a land (also not legal).

## The vocabulary, precisely

Duke notes that memorizing technicalities is often tedious, **but in the case of permission it's very
helpful.** These terms are familiar; the exact definitions may not be.

**Stack.** Where spells and abilities wait to resolve. **When you cast a spell or activate an ability
it goes on the stack, and both players get a chance to respond. If they choose not to, it resolves as
normal.** Duke's reassurance: it can feel scary when you first learn about it, but it's really not so
bad.

**Spell.** **A spell only exists while it's being cast, while it's on the stack, and while it's
resolving.** You can use permission to remove a spell from the stack before it has had its effect.
**Instants and sorceries are spells as you're casting them. Creatures, planeswalkers, enchantments,
and artifacts are also spells as you're casting them, but not once they're on the battlefield.**

**Permanent.** Once on the battlefield they're permanents instead. **Any card or token on the
battlefield.**

**Land.** **Lands are permanents, but they are never spells.** Playing a land is a *special action*
and **does not use the stack** — it happens immediately and **cannot be countered or responded to.**

**Card.** In zones other than the stack and the battlefield, everything is merely a card. **Just as
you cannot Path to Exile a creature card in the graveyard, you can only use permission on a spell
currently on the stack.**

## Permission as early defense

**One of blue's traditional weaknesses is a shortage of removal**, so blue mages sometimes rely on
permission to avoid falling behind early.

**The strength: it answers anything.** **Lightning Strike** cannot kill **Courser of Kruphix**, and
**Ultimate Price** cannot kill **Fleecemane Lion**, **but Nullify stops either.** If you keep from
falling behind, the opponent may not get a creature onto the battlefield at all in the early game.

**The problem: it does nothing once you're behind.** Permission cannot remove a creature or
planeswalker from the battlefield, **so if one slips through you need another way to answer it.**

### Play/draw and matchup effects

> **Permission has slightly higher value on the play than on the draw**, since you're less likely to
> fall behind.

**Good against medium-speed decks like Abzan**, which sometimes won't play a creature until turn
three, by which time you're ready.

**Falls short against blisteringly fast decks like Mono-Red or White Weenie.** You can't counter a
turn-one creature, at least not in Standard, **so you start the game with a big problem.** Either you
take a ton of damage from it, **or you tap out to answer it with removal or a creature of your own.**

> **At this point your shields are down, and your opponent resolves another threat. You'll have to tap
> out to answer that one, and if you're not careful you find yourself in a vicious cycle where your
> permission spells are largely useless.**

**Hence: a mix of permission and removal is often your best bet.** There are matchups and situations
where leaning too heavily on permission is costly.

## Pushing a tempo advantage

**Duke's puzzle:** if I told you the final spell cast in a game of Magic was **Disdainful Stroke**,
what do you think happened?

**We tend to think of permission as defensive**, since its purpose is to stop what the opponent is
doing. **However, sometimes it's best used to stop them from stopping you.**

**One thing that could have happened:** an aggressive blue deck had a fast start with cheap creatures.
On turn five the opponent tapped out for **End Hostilities**, hoping to get back in, **and the blue
player countered it with Disdainful Stroke.** One last attack ended the game, **making Disdainful
Stroke the last spell played and very likely the winner's MVP.**

> **Permission is poor when you're behind, good when you're at parity, and downright deadly when
> you're ahead.**

**The archetype this describes:** blue-based aggressive decks are traditionally cold-blooded killers
of slow, controlling decks. **They get ahead early, then sit back on counterspells to prevent the
control deck from clawing back in.**

## Locking up the game

**Permission maintains its value throughout the game**, which is another of its great virtues. Beyond
early defense and pushing tempo, **it helps you lock up the late game. Permission can be important to
inevitability.**

**The nightmare it prevents.** You're on a late-game control deck focused on card advantage. You've
fended off the early rush and you're dominant, drawing countless extra cards off **Dig Through Time**
and **Dragonlord's Prerogative**. **Then you watch your opponent tap one land, two, three… eight,
nine — Crater's Claws for eight knocks you out of the game.** Just when everything was going so well.

> **This nightmare can be avoided with a modest amount of permission.** If blue is known for two
> things, it's permission and card drawing, **and the two make a deadly combination.** Once you've
> taken control, your card drawing sees a large portion of your library, and if there are even a few
> counters in there, **you can make sure the game doesn't slip through your fingers.**

## Permission in your sideboard

**Great sideboard options for aggro, control, and combo decks alike** — virtually anyone with access
to blue mana can consider some.

**Three reasons:**

**1. No liability against hyper-aggressive decks.** Counters in your *sideboard* won't hurt you
against decks that put you on the back foot immediately. **Too many in your maindeck would.**

**2. Perfect tailoring.** Like removal, after sideboarding you tune the suite to the person across from
you:

- **Disdainful Stroke** — great against ramp decks like Green Devotion.
- **Negate** — excellent against controlling decks like Esper Dragons.
- **Annul** — stellar against Blue-Red Artifacts and Constellation decks.

**3. Matchups slow down after sideboarding**, and you may need extra answers to what they bring in. His
example: you might normally consider **Ultimate Price** your best weapon against Green Devotion, **but
if they're siding in Nissa, Worldwaker, you may need to sprinkle in permission to be prepared.**

## Permission in Limited

**Everything true in Constructed is also true here.** The important difference: **decks in Limited,
both yours and your opponent's, are likely to be less focused.**

> **Balance is key. Make permission one extra tool at your disposal, but don't lean too heavily on
> it.** It's great to have a small number in your maindeck or sideboard, **but you'd want a special
> reason before playing more than two or three.**

**Their stock rises in Sealed compared to Draft.** Sealed is slower and more centered on late bombs.
**An ordinary common like Bone to Ash can be the perfect answer to a bomb like Kothophed, Soul
Hoarder.** Duke recommends counters as **perfect sideboard cards once you know whether your opponent
has a late-game bomb, and importantly, what it is.**

## Playing with permission

**Early game:** sometimes you must pull out all the stops to avoid falling behind on tempo. **Don't be
too proud to Dissolve a Foundry Street Denizen if that's what the situation calls for.**

**Later:** you sometimes have the luxury of being selective.

**The worked example.** You're on Blue-Black Control with a modest five counterspells, plus a lot of
removal and a few creatures, against Abzan. **Use the right tools for the right jobs:**

- **Anafenza, the Foremost resolves** — you can answer it fine with **Hero's Downfall**, **Murderous
  Cut**, or **Languish**.
- **A morph that could be Den Protector** — troublesome once it hits the battlefield, **so save your
  Dissolve for that.**

**And the crucial caveat, which is what elevates this from a rule to a skill:**

> **These questions are fluid and might change from one situation to the next.** At 20 life you might
> let a **Siege Rhino** resolve and kill it with removal. **At 5, maybe it's worth the Dissolve.** If
> you expect to tap out for a creature next turn, **maybe countering Anafenza is a fine call just to
> keep the board under control.**

## Playing against permission

**The exploitable weakness:**

> **Permission spells must be cast in a narrow window.** If your opponent passes the turn with mana
> open for Dissolve, **they've made a commitment of mana, and they must either use it or let it go to
> waste.**

**So:** if you suspect a held Dissolve, **cast your second-best spell**, or cast nothing at all if you
have that luxury.

**Force them to move first.** If you can get ahead on board, they can't keep sitting on counters. **If
they pass with three Islands untapped, just attack with your Rakshasa Deathdealer and pump it.** After
a few turns of this **you force them to make the first move**, perhaps opening a window for you.

**Sequence around the sweeper.** Most permission-heavy Standard decks also play **Languish** or **End
Hostilities**. **Plan for the turns when they tap out for those.**

> **You don't want to let your opponent Dissolve your Siege Rhino and then untap and Languish the rest
> of your creatures away.** Lead with one of your weaker creatures, **saving the Rhino, perhaps your
> most important spell, for the turn your opponent taps out.**

**The exception where they lose nothing by holding.** On a turn where they plan to cast an
instant-speed card-drawing spell like **Dig Through Time** or **Jace's Ingenuity**, holding mana costs
them nothing. **If you suspect this, it can be in your interest to play right into the counterspell**,
because at least you force them to wait a turn on their other spell. **It's all about making things as
inconvenient as possible.**

### The final warning, which matters most

> **My last piece of advice is not to be too passive. The worst thing you can do is let the fear of a
> counterspell cause you to lose the game when your opponent doesn't have one at all.**

**Each turn that passes with no action favors the late-game control deck**, so don't give them more
time than you have to.

> **When in doubt, err on the side of being aggressive. Your opponent won't always have it. If they
> do, oh well — you might not have been able to play around it even if you'd tried.**

## Notes for use

- **The one-line summary to keep:** permission is poor when you're behind, good at parity, and deadly
  when you're ahead. That single sentence reorganizes counterspells from a "control card" into a
  position-dependent card, which is the same move Flores makes about role assignment.
- **The vocabulary section is genuinely load-bearing for Arena players.** Lands never use the stack;
  a spell only exists on the stack; a permanent is not a spell. The client hides all of this, and
  these are exactly the distinctions that get people caught in paper. Pair with installment 04's
  priority walkthrough and Block 4's Cranial Insertion.
- **The "narrow window" exploit is the best practical content here.** Open mana is a *commitment*, and
  commitments can be taxed. Casting your second-best spell into held-up mana is the same idea Duke
  gives in installment 06 from the other direction.
- **The Siege Rhino sequencing lesson generalizes:** when the opponent holds both a counter and a
  sweeper, lead with the card you can afford to lose. That's sequencing under uncertainty, which
  installment 20 covers directly.
- **The closing warning is the most important sentence in the installment** and the one most often
  ignored. Playing around a counterspell that isn't there loses more games than walking into one that
  is.
- **Cross-reference:** installment 09 introduced permission inside the control archetype; this is the
  full treatment. Installment 16 covers inevitability, which this installment names as permission's
  late-game role.
