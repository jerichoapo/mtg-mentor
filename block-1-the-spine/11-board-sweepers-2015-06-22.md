# Board Sweepers

**Reid Duke — Level One, June 22, 2015**

---

## Why a card-category installment

Duke typically shies away from discussing individual cards in Level One. **But certain categories are
so important, so powerful, and so recognizable that even a fundamental strategy guide would be
incomplete without them.**

**Board sweepers are cards capable of destroying many creatures at once.** Also called **wraths**,
named after their oldest and most iconic ancestor.

**Wrath of God is simple in its wording, if not in its effect on the game**, but sweepers take many
forms, and the differences matter whether you're playing with them or against them:

| Variation | Example |
|---|---|
| **Symmetric** | Wrath of God |
| **Asymmetric** | In Garruk's Wake |
| **Conditional** | Seismic Rupture |
| **Destroys more than creatures** | Perilous Vault |

## Board sweepers are format defining

**Duke is careful with the term.** Format defining means more than being powerful, and more than
being commonly played across multiple decks.

> **It means that most, or all, decks in the format are warped around its existence.**

> **One of the first things you need to learn about a new format is what board sweepers are out
> there.** This impacts both how you build your deck and how you play.

### In Limited

**Some formats have few or none.** Great — play a normal game and focus on developing your board.

**Dragons of Tarkir had Seismic Rupture at uncommon**, meaning you won't face it every game or even
every draft, **but shouldn't be surprised by it either.** Being conditional, you can make small
adjustments to reduce vulnerability:

- **Green-red decks** can make great use of it if most of their creatures have toughness 3 or more.
- **Blue-red decks** can use it well with few creatures, or mostly fliers.
- **Other red decks** can simply sideboard it against opponents who are particularly vulnerable.

> **If your draft deck is utterly devastated by Seismic Rupture, this is a weakness you should be
> aware of.**

**Fate Reforged had Crux of Fate at rare.** Technically conditional, but in many cases it destroys
everything anyway — **it's hard to build resilience against it.** However, since it's a rare, **you
probably shouldn't worry about it in Limited until you see for certain that your opponent has it.**

### In Constructed

**Even more important, since anyone who wants to play them can.** Duke's checklist for the Standard of
the time:

- Most control decks play **one Ugin, the Spirit Dragon** and **one to three End Hostilities or Crux
  of Fate.**
- **Drown in Sorrow** is a common sideboard card.
- **Casting multiple copies of the same creature makes you vulnerable to Bile Blight.**

**In deckbuilding**, if the prevalent sweepers threaten you, brainstorm solutions. His examples: **some
mono-red decks turn to dash creatures to beat Drown in Sorrow**, and **nearly all Green Devotion decks
run Whisperwood Elemental as insurance against End Hostilities.**

**In play**, weigh the risk and reward of casting more creatures into a possible sweeper.

## When to put sweepers in your deck

### Limited

**Sweepers are powerful in both formats, but in Limited — where power levels are lower and every deck
runs a healthy number of creatures — they're typically bombs.**

> **You should always play with a card like Crux of Fate, no matter how many creatures you're playing
> or how aggressive your deck is.**

**The reasoning is about control of the effect:**

- **If it looks like you're going to cast it, you can save a few creatures in your hand.**
- **If you're going to win without it, you can choose not to cast it at all.**

**Lower-power sweepers like Seismic Rupture are more in question.** Strong maindeck if they fit your
deck and the format is right — **the faster the format and the more weenie creatures, the better.**
**They will virtually always be excellent sideboard cards.**

### Constructed: control decks

**Sweepers are at their best in control decks.** Control values card advantage and wants long games,
and sweepers serve both. **Perhaps most importantly, control decks typically play few creatures of
their own, so an effect that reads as symmetric is hardly symmetric at all.**

### Constructed: creature decks

**Sometimes circumstances are right, but the costs are much higher.**

> **Board sweepers ask you to center your game plan around them. They're not particularly helpful when
> you're trying to play a normal game and fight a fair fight.**

**The worked Abzan-mirror example**, which is the best thing in this installment:

You're on the play. **Turn four, no creatures on the battlefield, and your hand has Siege Rhino and
Crux of Fate.** What do you do?

- **Do nothing** → you're not using Siege Rhino to its full potential.
- **Cast a creature** → you might not use Crux of Fate to its full potential.

**Say you cast Siege Rhino, as you probably should.** Opponent plays their own Siege Rhino. Now what?
**Casting Crux would be completely symmetric and accomplish little**, so suppose you do nothing.

**Opponent casts Anafenza, the Foremost.** Now you can trade two-for-two, Crux plus your Rhino for
their Rhino plus Anafenza. **A fine trade, but unexciting, and your removal spell has been overpriced
and awkward.**

**You could hold out hoping for a third creature. But what if they don't cast one? What if instead
they kill your Siege Rhino and attack for more damage than you were counting on?**

> I find scenarios like this to be very common. **Crux of Fate isn't a bad card in an Abzan mirror —
> you can almost always find a use for it. However, it's also somewhat unreliable.**

**The counterfactual makes the point.** Replace Crux of Fate with **Hero's Downfall** and your life
becomes much easier: **cast your Rhino, Downfall their Rhino, and start beating down.**

> **In short, spot removal tends to fit the plan of creature decks better than board sweepers do.**
> But if there's a matchup (Green Devotion) or a particular card (Wingmate Roc) for which you really
> need a sweeper, the option exists.

## Maindeck or sideboard? The central irony

**Sweepers are easier to put in your sideboard than your maindeck**, being great against creature decks
and very poor against control.

> **Ironically, though, board sweepers are much more powerful as maindeck cards than as sideboard
> cards.**

> **An astounding number of game ones come down to: "Player A will win if they have a board sweeper.
> Player B will win if they do not."**

**Two reasons this is true:**

**1. The creature deck usually can't play around it before sideboarding.** A deck designed to put
creatures into play and attack **is likely excellent at executing that plan and not much else.**

**2. The control or midrange deck usually lacks enough spot removal to beat the creature deck before
sideboarding.** To dismantle Green Devotion or Mono-Red in game one with spot removal alone, **a deck
would need so many removal spells there'd be no room for anything else — and far too many dead cards
against decks that don't win via creatures.**

### What changes after sideboarding

**The creature deck** knows it must be prepared, so it **adjusts its game plan and diversifies its
threats** using planeswalkers, dash creatures, or whatever it has. **Sometimes it brings direct answers
like Thoughtseize or Negate.**

**The control/midrange deck** sideboards in all the removal and defensive measures it has. **Now the
plan is to kill every creature on sight, as quickly as possible.** There's no opportunity for the
creature deck to build a huge army. **The sweepers might still be effective, but they're no longer the
primary game plan.**

**The conclusion:**

> **If you've decided to play unconditional sweepers (End Hostilities, Crux of Fate), they'll be at
> their most potent in the maindeck.** Conditional sweepers like **Drown in Sorrow** are still very
> good as sideboard cards, since they're so effective in the particular matchups where you want them.

## The preview: Languish

**Languish straddles the line between conditional and unconditional**, and at **four mana it's
relevantly cheaper** than End Hostilities and Crux of Fate. **Duke thought those two characteristics
together might make it format defining.**

**What it does:** wipes all small creatures, **even through regeneration and indestructible**, but
leaves creatures with **5 or more toughness** alive.

**Why that's convenient for black creature decks:** **Siege Rhino; Tasigur, the Golden Fang; Dragonlord
Silumgar;** and **Silumgar, the Drifting Death** all survive it. **You can easily design your own deck
to be resilient, taking maximum advantage of the symmetric effect.**

**The speed/scale combination:** at four mana it's **fast enough to save you from red weenie decks and
Goblin Rabblemaster**, while **its effect is large enough to remain powerful into the late game**,
answering **Thunderbreak Regent** and **Dragonlord Ojutai**.

**Duke's forecast:** he didn't expect it to replace Drown in Sorrow and Crux of Fate, but to have a
place beside them. **With so many sweepers available, you choose the one that best suits your needs**,
and building to be resilient against the variety available will be a challenge with great rewards.

## Notes for use

- **The definition of "format defining" is worth keeping**: not merely powerful or widely played, but
  a card that warps how every deck in the format is built. That's a much higher bar than the phrase
  usually gets used for.
- **The maindeck irony is the load-bearing insight.** A sweeper is at maximum power exactly when the
  opponent hasn't yet had the chance to prepare for it, which is game one — and that's the game where
  you're least sure you want it. This is the cleanest example in the course of a card whose value
  changes across the three games of a match.
- **The Abzan mirror walkthrough is a general lesson about awkward cards.** A sweeper in a creature
  deck creates a decision loop where every option undercuts one card in your hand. The tell for that
  problem is not "is this card good" but "does holding it make my other cards worse."
- **Cross-reference to installment 09**, where Duke notes control decks make great use of symmetric
  effects precisely because they run few creatures. This installment is the fuller version of that
  claim, and installment 14 (Symmetric Effects) generalizes it.
- **Cross-reference to Block 2.** The sweeper is the archetypal card that decides *inevitability* —
  Zvi's question of who wins a long game — and it's also the card that most often forces a role
  reassignment mid-match, since a creature deck that must play around a wrath is no longer purely the
  beatdown.
- **Reading caution:** the final third is a Magic Origins preview from 2015. The Languish analysis is
  a good worked example of evaluating a new sweeper, but the format commentary is long dead.
