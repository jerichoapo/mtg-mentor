# Tempo

**Reid Duke — Level One, July 20, 2015**

---

## The opening

Duke's aside: one of his great joys aside from Magic is music, though he learned young that his love
of it would be relegated to listening rather than making. The concept he never got was tempo, how
fast or slow to play. He didn't want to pay attention to his sheet music, his teacher, or his
bandmates — he wanted to do his own thing at his own pace. Eventually even he couldn't listen to the
disharmonious clatter, so he gave up music and went back to Magic.

He doesn't like making the same mistake twice, so in Magic he has deep respect for tempo.

## What tempo is

> **Like mana and card advantage, tempo is a resource. It differs in that it cannot easily be
> "counted" like the other two can.**

**In its most basic form, tempo is board presence.** It derives from how your creatures, lands,
planeswalkers, artifacts, and enchantments match up against your opponent's, and the consequences
that follow. **It's called tempo because the way the two players jockey for the resource dictates the
pace of the game.**

**Its relationships:**

- **Very closely related to mana.**
- **Often, though not always, related to life total.**
- **Frequently traded against card advantage in both directions.**

**Trading tempo for cards:** take a turn to cast **Weave Fate** and you gain card advantage, but
you've spent a turn without improving your board, so you may have given up tempo.

**Trading tempo for cards, the other way:** get so far ahead on board that your opponent is forced to
**chump block** — block and lose a creature — just to preserve their life total.

## Using your mana effectively

Mana curve matters **because of tempo**. A deck of all five-mana spells has nothing to do for four
turns, so **your mana on turns one through four is wasted.** If your opponent uses their mana more
effectively, you fall behind on board, on tempo, and in the game.

> **If you find yourself very often ending the turn without using all your mana, this should be a red
> flag** that there's an inefficiency in either how you built your deck or how you're playing.

### The worked decision

Your opponent plays a **Cleric of the Forward Order**. You play your second land, and your only
available play is **Reave Soul** on the Cleric. Should you?

**It's complicated.** Sometimes other factors outweigh tempo — maybe you want to save Reave Soul for
a flier later. **However, casting Reave Soul is the play that gains the most tempo:** you spend your
mana efficiently and avoid falling behind on board.

**Now add information.** Your hand also has **Read the Bones**, a three-mana card you hope to cast
next turn. If you don't cast Reave Soul now, you either wait much later to use it or delay Read the
Bones. **The longer it takes to cast your spells and deploy your threats, the more tempo you lose.**
And after Read the Bones you'll want to start casting what you drew. **This may be your one good
window to cast Reave Soul.**

## Your lands

**Tempo is about developing your board, and playing lands is part of developing your board.** Each
land provides one more mana every turn for the rest of the game.

Two consequences:

1. **This is why progressive turns become more powerful, with wilder tempo swings as the game goes
   on.**
2. **This is why missing a land drop is so devastating.** You fall behind on tempo immediately *and*
   your ability to recover tempo on future turns is damaged.

### Lands that enter tapped

A land like the one you fetch with **Evolving Wilds** costs you one mana on the turn you play it, a
potential loss of tempo. **The flip side** is much more versatility than basic lands, since you
search for whichever color you're missing.

**The rule of thumb:** on any turn you didn't have something pressing to spend mana on, playing a
land like Evolving Wilds is a good long-term investment. Whether to avoid tapped lands or include
them **is a question of your exact strategy and how much you can afford a small early tempo loss.**

### Mana creatures

**Leaf Gilder** functions like a land once in play, offering extra mana every turn it survives and
potentially letting you pull ahead on tempo as the game goes. **The catch is the initial investment
of mana.** Some decks are willing to make it, others aren't.

## Creatures and initiative

At any moment a player is playing one of two ways:

- **Proactive:** deploying threats, attacking, progressing their own game plan.
- **Reactive:** defending, answering the opponent's threats.

**It's good to be proactive whenever possible.** The proactive player takes **the initiative**,
meaning they set the pace and force the opponent to react.

**The advantages of initiative:**

1. **If you're attacking very aggressively it's often difficult for your opponent to attack you
   back.** You don't have to mount a defense, worry about your life total, or protect your
   planeswalkers. The best defense is a good offense.
2. **Forcing your opponent to block puts them in a frightening position.** Removing a key blocker
   often means a big tempo swing and a lot of damage. And even when the creature survives, **the
   attacking player usually has all their mana available** (it's their turn) **while the blocking
   player might not**, having just tapped out to play the blocker. So combat tricks like **Titanic
   Growth** and **Mighty Leap** can be used to full potential.

**The asymmetry in investment.** For a player who wants to attack, creatures require an initial
investment, since they can't attack the turn they arrive. **But for a player concerned with
blocking** — who is jockeying for tempo exactly as their opponent is — **a creature creates a tempo
swing immediately.** Enters-the-battlefield triggers and haste are excellent in tempo terms.

## Removal spells, and "removal spells"

Removal can be a great way to gain tempo: take out a blocker to keep attacking, or take out an
attacker and defend yourself with little risk.

**Unholy Hunger** is simple, elegant, no-questions-asked removal. If your opponent spends their turn
playing a creature and you spend yours killing it, **you've probably broken even on tempo** — and
also broken even on card advantage, a one-for-one trade.

**Its one flaw is that it's a little pricy**, which sets up the article's central example.

### The cheap-creatures strategy

**A perfect example of a tempo-based strategy: deploy a lot of cheap creatures and try to win before
your opponent can answer them all or cast more powerful spells.** Unholy Hunger costs five mana, and
against this strategy the best it can do is kill a creature that cost two or three.

**The key insight:** if you fill your deck with cheap creatures, your cards will likely be less
powerful and unable to stand up in a fair fight. **Fortunately, you don't have to engage in a fair
fight.**

**Scenario A — you're ahead, they have the removal.** You deploy four or five creatures in the first
five turns. On turn five they start fighting back with Unholy Hunger on one of them. **Turn five may
have produced no net tempo change, but since you were so far ahead beforehand, you preserved your
tempo advantage.**

> **Once you're ahead on tempo, every turn the game state does not change, you glean an advantage in
> another form** — in this case massive damage from attacking.

**Scenario B — you're ahead *and* you have the removal.** You're ahead on board from cheap early
creatures. Your opponent desperately casts **Sentinel of the Eternal Watch**, hoping to block and
stabilize both the board and their life total. **You cast Unholy Hunger on the Sentinel and attack
with everything.**

This is preserving, or pushing, a tempo advantage: **making a play that realizes a concrete advantage
because you have the upper hand in tempo.** When your opponent misses a beat, knowing how to
capitalize is key.

> **Removal spells are great for pushing a tempo advantage because, unlike creatures, there's no
> initial investment. Their impact on the board is immediate.**

### Bounce spells and the card-advantage tradeoff

Unholy Hunger is a one-for-one, no change in card advantage. **A bounce spell like Disperse will
actually leave you with card disadvantage much of the time:** you spent a card, and their creature,
though off the battlefield, is still a resource they can use long-run.

**Nevertheless the two will sometimes have an identical impact.** At two mana, Disperse can be a
great tool if your plan is focused on tempo at the expense of card advantage.

**In the four-creatures-versus-fresh-Sentinel spot, Unholy Hunger and Disperse are likely very
similar — either one wins the game.** The Sentinel is in their hand rather than the graveyard, but
you're likely winning immediately by attacking. If not, they have to invest six mana again, probably
a full turn.

> **Traditional measures of card advantage go out the window when one player loses the game with five
> or six cards left in hand.**

### The three conditions that make bounce work

Duke is precise that these were **perfect circumstances** for a bounce spell, for three specific
reasons:

1. **You're already ahead on tempo** and using Disperse to push the advantage, realizing it as life
   total.
2. **You're bouncing a six-mana creature**, forcing them to spend far more mana replaying it than you
   spent on Disperse. You might even cast another creature this turn to extend further.
3. **Your opponent has a hand full of other cards and is bottlenecked on mana** — more things to
   spend mana on than mana available.

## When tempo doesn't matter

> **What makes tempo such a challenging concept is that it can be hard to know how its value changes
> from one situation to the next.**

**Plenty of times neither player can push a tempo advantage:**

- **A creature stall**, where neither player can profitably attack.
- **A late game where both players have plenty of mana to spare.**

**The illustration:** you Disperse their Sentinel but can't attack. On their turn they have plenty of
mana and nothing else to do but replay it. **For a split second you gained tempo, but you couldn't
push it. The end result is the identical game state, except you've lost Disperse from your hand.**

**In cases like these, tempo should take a back seat and you should focus on card advantage instead.**

## The close

The value of tempo changes from one game to the next and even one turn to the next. **Knowing when
you can capitalize on tempo offers a great advantage, but identifying when to focus on other aspects
of the game instead is just as important.**

## Notes for use

- **The definition to keep:** tempo is board presence, and it's the resource you can't count. That
  uncountability is why it took the community years longer to formalize than card advantage.
- **The most quotable mechanism:** once you're ahead on tempo, every turn the game state doesn't
  change converts into advantage in another form. That's the engine behind every aggro deck ever
  built, and it's the same claim Flores makes in the Philosophy of Fire using life total as the
  currency.
- **Duke's three conditions for a bounce spell** are the best practical test in this installment.
  Bounce is bad by default and good under those three conditions specifically. Applying them stops
  most Vapor Snag misuse.
- **The closing move is the mature one:** knowing when tempo *stops* mattering (creature stalls, late
  games with spare mana) is what separates this from a purely aggressive reading. Compare Eric
  Taylor's argument in `block-2-core-theory-paired/04-its-about-time-1997-08-11.md` that decks tilt
  toward one resource or the other, and that Memory Lapse is actively bad in the deck built for the
  long game.
- **Read next:** installment 06, which is the explicit tempo-versus-card-advantage installment, and
  installment 17 on role assignment, where "who can push tempo" becomes "who is the beatdown."
