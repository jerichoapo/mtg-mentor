# Control Decks

**Reid Duke — Level One, October 6, 2014**

---

## The polar opposite of aggro

**Where aggro decks strive for speed, control decks are geared for the long game.** Where aggro wins
by executing its own game plan, **control focuses on shutting down the opponent and wins later, at its
own convenience.**

> **We call them control decks because their goal is not to kill the opponent, but merely to control
> the game. Their top priority is to stop the opponent from executing his or her game plan.**

Duke notes this can be amazingly effective and particularly appeals to players who enjoy long, often
complex games.

## Card advantage in control decks

**Every deck must be built with tempo in mind**, but the relationship differs:

| | Aggro | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Tempo goal | **Get ahead** | **Keep pace** |
| Wins via | **Tempo** | **Card advantage** |
| Tempo concern | Primary resource | **Only as it relates to defending itself** |

**Control decks merely need to avoid falling so far behind on tempo that they lose because of it.**

**Their edge comes through card advantage.** If they survive the early turns and make games go long,
**most opponents run out of resources and peter out**, while the control deck **becomes more and more
powerful once its expensive spells and card advantage come online.**

**The preferred form is the simplest and most direct: spells that draw extra cards.**

### The turn-eleven illustration

Duke makes the case with arithmetic. A game reaches turn ten or eleven. Your aggro opponent is more or
less spent — you've answered the early rush and **they're now drawing one card per turn off the top,
which will often be a useless land.**

You've cast several card-drawing spells, so **you've drawn about twenty-five cards to their eighteen.**
That likely means more mana, more cards in hand, and more relevant creatures and spells to fight with.
**Beating their measly one draw step per turn is academic.**

> If you have enough of these card-drawing spells, **one will draw you into another into another and
> you'll never have to worry about mana flood.** Your opponent will simply be buried in card advantage
> until eventually you find some powerful creature or planeswalker to put them out of their misery.

## Inevitability

Card advantage might come via creatures, spells, planeswalkers, or anything else. **But it must be
there, and in high enough concentration, or the deck will not function properly.**

> **Control decks are built on the premise of inevitability. The deck must be powerful enough in the
> late game that its pilot can be confident of a win so long as they can defend themselves and drag
> the game out indefinitely.**

**Achieving inevitability is always the first step and the top priority when building a control
deck.** If you defend successfully against the early rush only to suffer mana flood and lose anyway,
**all your efforts were wasted.**

**Duke's working definition, pending the fuller treatment in installment 16:**

> **You have inevitability if your deck is powerful enough that you'll win a very high portion of
> games that go long.**

**Ensure it by building with plenty of card advantage and powerful cards.**

## Example: White-Blue Control

A very classic example featuring the most recognizable features of the **archetype** — Duke's term for
a recurring strategy with numerous possible variations.

### Mana in control decks

**The first thing to notice is the high land count: 26.**

Card-drawing spells and powerful cards reduce the risk of mana flood, which is what lets the deck
afford the high count. **But why control decks need a high concentration of mana is a separate
point:**

**Aggro decks would often be happy never to draw another land beyond the third or fourth. Control
decks typically need to hit every land drop into turn six, turn seven, and sometimes beyond.**

Since they're focused on the late game, **control decks may not have many cheap plays and cannot
operate if they miss land drops.** They lean on powerful plays like **End Hostilities** and **Elspeth,
Sun's Champion** to catch up on tempo, **so it's paramount they have the mana when the time comes.**

### Creatures in control decks

**Newer players might be shocked to see only two creatures**, against 28 or more in the aggro decks,
and Duke's own recommended minimum of 14 in a 40-card Sealed deck. **But a small creature count is not
unusual for control**, which focuses on defense and card advantage and only wins once firmly in
control. **A small number of potent, reliable win conditions is the most effective way to execute
that plan.**

**Two advantages of a low creature count:**

**1. Virtual card advantage by blanking their removal.** Imagine playing against this deck holding
**Murderous Cut** and **Devouring Light**. What can you do with them?

- Spend them on an **Elspeth** token — hardly a winning battle, **and an exchange that leaves the
  control player ahead on card advantage.**
- Use them to tap **Prognostic Sphinx** for one turn.

**Either way, the removal is stuck in hand until the game is all but decided. You never get to use it
effectively.**

**2. Great use of symmetric effects.** With few creatures of your own, **End Hostilities** is
tremendously powerful and central to the plan. **Fated Retribution** and **Elspeth's −3** are also
useful tools.

## Permission spells

Introduced here for the first time in the course. Duke's gloss on the name: *"Please, Reid, may I
pleeease cast my spell?"*

**Also called counterspells**, because of their wording and in honor of their most iconic ancestor.
Duke notes **Counterspell itself would be too powerful by today's standards and hasn't been printed
for many years.**

**Why they're great defensive tools: they stop virtually any threat before it becomes a problem.**

His comparison is precise. **Murderous Cut** cannot:

- prevent a creature like **Siege Rhino** from triggering its enters-the-battlefield ability
- kill a planeswalker
- stop an instant or sorcery

**Dissolve answers all of these.**

**Why they suit control specifically:** they contribute to inevitability. **Once you're in the late
game and things are going to plan, a Dissolve in hand means there's very little your opponent can hope
for to claw back in.**

### The catch

> **Permission spells cannot help you come back from behind.** Once a creature or planeswalker is on
> the battlefield, you need another way to deal with it.

That makes them tricky to use well:

- **Sometimes you must Nullify on turn two on absolutely anything they play**, just to avoid falling
  behind — in a game where tempo is a big concern.
- **Other times you must be very patient** and save Dissolve for a devastating threat you can't answer
  any other way.

> **Having a mix of permission spells and removal spells is ideal**, since that gives flexibility to
> handle any situation.

## Building a control deck

**Duke's build order:**

**1. Inevitability first.** Chock the deck full of enough card advantage and powerful cards that you
feel very confident winning the late game. **If you fail at this, the strategy simply won't work.**

**2. Early defense second.** All you want is to survive, develop your mana, and generate card
advantage. **Do whatever you can to dismantle your opponent's aggression** — permission, removal,
blocking creatures, any other tool. **No one way is better than another so long as it lets you survive
long enough to start overpowering opponents late.**

### Control decks come in many forms

**Blue is the most common control color**, and the color of permission, **but not all control decks are
blue.** Some dominate with planeswalkers. Some achieve inevitability by recurring cards from the
graveyard. Some assemble powerful card combinations their opponents can't overcome.

> **Whatever form they take, the heart of all control decks is the same: card advantage, inevitability,
> and defense.**

## The tournament warning

**A control deck can be an excellent tournament choice**, and because of their complexity and the long
games, **there are great rewards to practicing ahead of time and truly mastering one.**

**But the special challenge:**

> **You have to be prepared for anything your opponents can throw at you. Know what strategies you're
> likely to run into and have a plan for utterly dismantling them.**

## Notes for use

- **Read this against installment 08 as a matched pair.** The two archetype installments are built on
  the same axes, and the table at the top of this file is the compression of both.
- **The build order is the actionable part:** inevitability first, defense second. Most failed control
  decks are built in the reverse order, stuffed with answers and then short of a reason to win.
- **The "blanked removal" argument is the strongest form of virtual card advantage in the course.**
  A low creature count doesn't only dodge removal, it strands it in the opponent's hand, which is a
  card-advantage gain that never shows up in a naive count. Cross-reference installment 03.
- **Duke's working definition of inevitability** is looser than Zvi's. Duke: you win a very high
  portion of long games. **Zvi, quoted in Flores:** you have inevitability if and only if from the
  current position you will win a long game, and you have it *in a matchup* if you have it on turn
  one. See `block-2-core-theory-paired/02-eight-core-principles-of-whos-the-beatdown-2012-03-16.md`,
  principle 4. Duke's fuller treatment is installment 16.
- **The permission-spell catch is the same structural point Flores makes about role assignment:** a
  card that only preserves a position is useless once the position is already lost. That's why "who's
  the beatdown" has to be answered before you decide whether to hold up mana.
