# The Basics of Mana

**Reid Duke — Level One, July 6, 2015**

*Editorial note on the original: this was part of a run where Duke revisited key concepts updated for
Magic Origins, reintroduced for the next wave of players.*

---

## Why mana comes first

Nearly every game of Magic begins the same way: a player plays a land. Mana is one of the fundamental
resources, and without it you simply can't play the game.

But its importance starts before gameplay. **Mana is also one of the key elements of deckbuilding.**
In the same way you can't cast a spell without the proper mana in a game, you can only put creatures
and spells in your deck if your mana base can support them.

## The mana base

A deck's mana base is its lands plus any supplemental ways to produce mana, along the lines of Leaf
Gilder or Nissa's Pilgrimage. It can be simple, say seventeen Plains in a mono-white Limited deck, or
complex: multiple colors, lands with unusual abilities, and indirect mana from artifacts, creatures,
or spells.

Duke's caveat, stated up front: **there are no hard and fast rules.** Any guideline he offers might
need adjusting or scrapping under certain circumstances. What he can give is a helpful starting
point.

## How many lands to play

**The traditional benchmark: lands should make up a touch over 40% of a deck.**

- **40-card deck → about 17–18 lands**
- **60-card deck → about 24–25 lands**

This is tried and true and has worked for many players for many years. You'll rarely be making a big
mistake sticking to it. Several factors justify small adjustments.

### Non-land mana sources don't count one-for-one

The presence of creatures like Leaf Gilder affects how many lands you need, but **the correlation
isn't one-to-one**, for three reasons:

1. You cannot cast it unless you already have lands.
2. It's less reliable than a land, because it's easier for your opponent to kill.
3. You normally include such a card because you want to jump ahead on mana, but you still want to
   play a land every turn, at least for the first few turns.

So they shouldn't be counted directly as lands. But two copies of Leaf Gilder can be a fine reason to
play seventeen lands instead of eighteen in a Limited deck.

### How expensive is your deck?

An equally important question. If all your spells cost four or less, you need fewer lands than a
player running multiple cards at seven or eight.

## Colored mana

Harder than the count. There's not much to it in one color, but at three or more things get hairy.

**The core tradeoff, which Duke frames as a general pattern in Magic: power versus consistency.** The
more colors you play, the wider the range of cards you can access, so your deck is capable of more
powerful draws when everything runs smoothly. But more colors also makes it likelier you'll be stuck
without the right colors to cast a spell, lowering consistency.

The question that decides the balance: **what tools do you have access to?**

### With only basic lands

**Two colors is virtually always the right balance.** One color is a perfectly fine choice, but if
there's an appealing card in a second color you can feel safe adding it. With two colors you'll cast
both colors of spells in most games — not all, but what most players consider an acceptably high
percentage. **Beyond two, you'll begin to notice problems.**

### With better tools

Duke's analogy: **a deckbuilder is like a carpenter. The better the tools, the more complex the
projects.** Nonbasic lands and non-land mana sources can be powerful and let you play more colors.

Given a bevy of cards like Meteorite, Evolving Wilds, Caves of Koilos, or Shivan Reef, you can start
to feel comfortable with a **three-color deck**. Playing more than three colors is a rather advanced
technique, and he recommends against it until you're very comfortable with two- and three-color
decks.

### Splashing

Even with multiple colors, it's often in your interest to center the deck on one or two and be less
reliant on the rest.

His example: a sealed deck primarily black and red, but you want two copies of Possessed Skaab. It
might be fine to play only three or four sources of blue. You'll often find blue mana by the time
you're ready to cast it, and even if you don't, **having one card stuck in hand for a while isn't a
complete catastrophe.** This is **splashing**.

Building a deck equally reliant on all three colors is possible but difficult. **The more things that
can go wrong, the less often things go exactly right.**

### The colored-source thresholds

Duke's starting-point numbers for reliably having a given color in your opening hand:

| Deck size | Minimum sources | Safe |
|---|---|---|
| 40-card | 9–10 | 11 |
| 60-card | 15–16 | 17–18 |

## The mana curve

The mana question isn't finished once you've picked lands. It also determines what spells you play
and the overall structure of the deck.

**A deck's mana curve is the balance it strikes between cheap and expensive cards.** A "high mana
curve" has more expensive cards than usual; a "low mana curve" is mostly cheap cards.

**Why the extremes fail.** All five-mana cards would mean nothing to do for the first four turns. All
weak one-mana spells would mean you're never making the best possible play on any turn after the
first. **There's a lot of value in having at least a couple of cards at each mana cost.**

**No master formula.** The right answer depends on context: what cards you have access to, your
strategy for winning, and what your opponents are likely to be doing at each point in the game.

**Where it shows clearest: fast creature decks.** If your goal is to unload damage quickly you really
want to come out of the gates on turn one or two. Some Constructed decks play twelve, fourteen, or
more one-mana creatures.

**But it's a question of strategy.** If you aren't winning with a quick creature rush, playing dozens
of one- and two-mana creatures waters down the deck and reduces the number of powerful spells you can
play. You should have some early plays, but there's no mystical number. Ask how fast your opponents
are likely to be: do you have to start defending on turn one, or is it sometimes okay to play nothing
until turn three?

## Mana "hosed" and mana "flood"

Every new player learns these early. **Mana hosed** is slang for not drawing as many lands as you
want; **mana flood** means drawing far too many. These happen to any player, with any deck, at any
level of competition. It's simply part of the game.

Duke's reframe: although any particular game where you get mana hosed is aggravating and unfun,
**variance in lands drawn is actually something that enriches Magic** and helps make it the game it
is.

### Mitigating flood: mana sinks

Look for **mana sinks**, cards that aren't necessarily expensive themselves but can use extra mana in
late-game scenarios.

He notes it's completely normal to draw eight, nine, or more lands over a long game. With a mana sink
in play you might still prefer to draw spells, but when you inevitably start drawing more lands than
you need, at least you can put them to use.

### Mitigating hosed

Look for cheap cards that can answer or trade with more expensive cards.

### The real advice

**Recognize that drawing the wrong number of lands is a normal part of the game, and try not to be
frustrated by it.**

**The common pitfall:** a new player overcompensates by adding or removing lands after every mana
hosing or flood. If after a couple of dozen games you notice a pattern of things working out poorly,
learn from it and adapt. **The point is not to let short-term frustration overcome rational
decision-making.**

## Worked example: a sealed deck

Duke walks a Magic Origins sealed pool, mostly **red and green**, splashing **black** for a removal
spell (Cruel Revival) and a strong creature (Blazing Hellhound).

**Step 1 — how many lands?** A typical sealed deck plays seventeen or eighteen. This one looks
typical, so no reason to deviate. Three factors push toward eighteen:

- a handful of expensive cards at five and six mana
- a mana sink in Volcanic Rambler
- three colors

Three factors push back toward seventeen:

- a **Leaf Gilder** and a **Meteorite**, each counting as a fraction of a land
- two **Elvish Visionary**, a cheap card that draws another card and can help find lands if stuck early

**Verdict: seventeen.**

**Step 2 — the black splash.** Black is only a splash, so play enough that you'll usually draw one in
a normal game, without going far beyond. Three sources might work, four would be ideal. The deck has
**Meteorite**, **Evolving Wilds**, and **Llanowar Wastes** to help. Adding **one Swamp**, to go with
the Evolving Wilds, makes four sources of black. Perfect.

**Step 3 — the red/green split.** The rest is evenly split, so aim for close to an even split of
Forests and Mountains. But most of the cheap plays are green, and the deck really wants to cast
Elvish Visionary early to come out smoothly.

Counting Llanowar Wastes as green and Evolving Wilds as both, **seven Forests and seven Mountains
gives nine total green and eight total red.** He deliberately **does not count Meteorite**, because
the deck needs both red and green before turn five. Had he played an eighteenth land, it would have
been another Forest.

## The closing claim

If your deck has a good mana base and a balanced mana curve, you've avoided one of Magic's most
dangerous pitfalls. Make your mana serve you rather than feeling helpless against awkward draws, and
you're well on your way.

## Notes for use

- **The 40% rule and the colored-source thresholds are the two numbers to memorize.** Everything else
  in this installment is judgment.
- **Duke's colored-source numbers are the beginner-grade version of Frank Karsten's tables.** Karsten
  computes them properly with hypergeometric probability and separates play from draw. Read
  `block-3-manabase-reference/02-how-many-lands-do-you-need-2017-05-30.md` for the rigorous version; Duke's
  9–10-or-11 and 15–16-or-17 are useful rules of thumb that Karsten's work refines.
- **Note the tension with Turbo Xerox** (Block 3). Duke's 40% guideline is the conventional wisdom
  Alan Comer's approach deliberately breaks, by arguing that cheap card-selection spells substitute
  for lands. Duke gestures at this with Elvish Visionary but doesn't push it. The two readings are
  compatible: Duke is describing the default, Comer the exception and why it works.
- **The "don't over-adjust after one bad game" advice** is the most transferable line here, and it's
  the same discipline Karsten's regression on 110 winning decklists is designed to enforce.
