# Symmetric Effects

**Reid Duke — Level One, September 1, 2014**

---

## The problem

**Gaining an advantage means gaining an edge relative to your opponent.** A game of Magic is a struggle
to set yourself apart from, and ahead of, the other player.

**With that in mind, there's an entire category of cards that seem nearly impossible to use
effectively.**

> **A symmetric effect impacts both players the same way, so it's difficult to glean an advantage from
> one under ordinary circumstances.**

**And yet some of the most powerful cards in Magic feature symmetric effects.**

> **If you have the creativity and the vision to find a use for them, you can wield weapons other
> players cannot.**

Duke lays out four methods.

---

## Method 1: make better use of the effect than your opponent can

**The insight underneath everything else in the installment:**

> **No event can ever really impact two people in exactly the same way, because no two people ever
> exist under exactly the same conditions.**

His analogies: the summer sun feels less oppressive standing in the shade of a tree than to a friend
six feet away in the open. Or **the effects of a brutal winter on an ill-equipped invading army
compared to the same winter on the locals, who have the clothing, the shelter, and the know-how to
cope.**

> **The key is to put yourself in a position to take better advantage of a symmetric effect than your
> opponent can.**

### Dictate of Karametra

**It makes all lands produce additional mana.** Play it and you're giving your opponent extra mana;
there's no way around it.

**What you can do is make sure you use the extra mana better.** If you decided first to put it in your
deck and second to cast it in this particular game, **it should mean you're going to untap and spend a
lot of mana on something very powerful.** Cast **Genesis Hydra** for X=10, or make **Polukranos, World
Eater** monstrous for enough to wipe out their creatures. **It doesn't matter exactly what, so long as
it's substantially more powerful than whatever your opponent will do.**

**And Duke points out the advantage is structural, not lucky:**

> **You're the one who put Dictate of Karametra in your deck, not your opponent. You're the one who
> planned your strategy around it.** It might well be that your opponent is playing a basic creature
> deck with not much to do with the extra mana. **The impact of the symmetric effect was, in reality,
> not very symmetric at all.**

### Eidolon of the Great Revel

**Over a long game it's not surprising if the Eidolon deals 6 or 8 damage to both players. But there's
no need to panic.**

> Well, that's not entirely true — **someone ought to be panicking, but hopefully not you.**

**The requirement:** make sure you're pressuring their life total more aggressively than they're
pressuring yours.

> **It's fine if your own Eidolon causes you to end the game at 12 life instead of 20; so long as your
> opponent winds up at 0, it won't matter in the slightest.**

## Method 2: dodge the effect entirely

**Duke's illustration is Y2K.** As January 1, 2000 approached, everyone panicked about whether computer
systems would fail, businesses lose money, banks fail, missiles go off. **He never worried — because he
was ten years old.** No investments to depreciate, no job to lose, no money. **He was pretty sure he'd
still be able to climb his favorite tree no matter what happened to all those crazy computers.**

> **Sometimes you can take advantage of a symmetric effect by, quite simply, having nothing to lose.**

**Two examples:**

**Renounce the Guilds.** **It's perfectly legal to cast it with no multicolored permanents of your
own.** Your opponent sacrifices a multicolored permanent and you simply go about your business. **Put
it in a mono-white deck, or any deck without many multicolored permanents.**

**Whispering Madness.** Both players discard their hands and draw new cards. **But you choose when to
cast it.** Play out the rest of your hand first and **make it the final card you cast** — you discard
nothing and **draw cards equal to however many your opponent was still holding.**

## Method 3: create the best window

**The key to the Whispering Madness case generalizes:**

> **You, as the player with the symmetric effect, have control of your own spell. You choose when, and
> even if, you cast it.** Given that symmetric effects are often powerful and game-changing, **this
> flexibility is extremely valuable.**

### Gaze of Granite

**Spend enough mana and it blows up the world.** Dodging it would be quite difficult, since nearly
every deck plays noncreature permanents. **But being in control of such a powerful weapon makes it very
appealing.**

**The simplest factor, and one of the most important:**

> **You don't have to cast it unless you want to.** If you happen to be winning on board, press the
> advantage and forget Gaze of Granite exists. **So long as you're in control of it, a symmetric effect
> need never hurt you.**

**And when you do need it**, once it becomes clear you'll have to cast it as your get-out-of-jail-free
card, **start saving creatures in your hand to cast afterward.** That way you kill more of their
creatures and fewer of yours — **card advantage** — and you're positioned to recover faster.

> **You control the symmetric effect, so you have the greater ability to plan your strategy around it.**

## Method 4: lock up the game once you're ahead

### Sire of Insanity

**A card you're likely to cast if and only if you're winning on board.**

- **Ahead:** your opponent discards their hand and loses any hope of clawing back.
- **Behind:** casting it effectively **freezes the game state**, making it very difficult to turn
  things around.

> **Symmetric effects impact players differently depending on their starting conditions.** When you're
> winning, or when you have no cards in hand anyway, **having both players discard is a blessing.** When
> you're losing and need spells to fight back, **it's a disaster.**

### Ember Swallower

**When it becomes monstrous, both players sacrifice three lands.** The player with more and better
creatures loves it; the player behind hates it. **It's very hard to come back when you no longer have
enough mana to cast your spells.**

**The elegant part Duke highlights:** both Sire of Insanity and Ember Swallower **are giant creatures
and contribute greatly to "being ahead on the board" in and of themselves.** When you're making Ember
Swallower monstrous, **you're very likely about to have the most powerful creature on the battlefield,
so the symmetric land loss benefits you hugely.**

## The summary

> Once you understand symmetric effects, they become some of the best tools at your disposal. **You can
> build your deck to make the best possible use of them. You can plan your strategy around them. You
> can cast them when they benefit you and save them when they don't.**

## The preview: End Hostilities (Khans of Tarkir)

**Duke's read:** you can tell from the first three words it's a big one. **"Destroy all creatures" is a
game-breaking effect worth playing at virtually any mana cost.** End Hostilities is efficiently costed
at five, **and adds "and all permanents attached to creatures"** — Equipment, bestow Auras, and other
attached Auras. (Those would normally die with the creature, but it can come up **if the creature is
indestructible or gets regenerated.**)

**What it offers:** potential card advantage, an answer to the opponent's best cards, **and a safe,
clean board state as soon as you hit five mana. Perfect for any deck powerful enough to win late.**

**In Limited:** a bomb. **Every card similar to End Hostilities ever printed has been a Limited
powerhouse.**

**In Standard:** potentially format defining, with **Supreme Verdict** rotating out and leaving a hole.
**Two advantages over Supreme Verdict:**

1. **No blue mana required.** Playable in mono-white, or in Mardu (RWB) or Abzan (WBG). **Even in a W/U
   deck it's easier on your mana and more reliable to cast.**
2. **It destroys permanents attached to creatures.** Particularly important against Theros-block
   bestow. Duke: *I can't count the number of times I've needed Supreme Verdict to get me out of
   trouble, only to find that a bestowed Boon Satyr comes into play and finishes me off. Never again.*

### Duke then runs his own four methods on the card

**Making better use of it than your opponent.** If you put it in your deck and know it, **plan your
strategy around it.** Be prepared to play a slightly longer game, since it stops any early creature
rush, **and build to recover quickly and dominate starting around turn six.**

**Dodging it.** **You don't need a creatureless deck**, but you might play slightly fewer creatures.
**Don't put too many onto the table in the first four turns**, or you'll destroy your own cards along
with theirs. **Aiming for a turn-five End Hostilities followed by a powerful six-mana creature sounds
like a winning strategy.**

**Creating the window.** **You're in control, and that flexibility means everything.** With creatures
in play you can keep attacking and save it in case things turn. **Cast it right away on turn five or
wait patiently for them to commit more creatures. It's all up to you.**

**Locking up the game.** Duke broadens the definition of "ahead" here, which is the most useful part:

> In previous examples I referred to the player ahead on board as the one with more and better
> creatures. **But there are plenty of other ways to be ahead.** It might mean having one or more
> planeswalkers in play. It might mean a powerful artifact or enchantment. **Or it might simply mean
> having a bunch of lands in play and a deck that's very powerful in the late game.** In any of these
> cases, End Hostilities can lock up the game for you.

## Notes for use

- **The governing insight is the best sentence in the course so far:** no event impacts two people the
  same way, because no two people are in the same conditions. Symmetry on the card is not symmetry in
  the game. Every technique here follows from that.
- **The four methods are a genuinely reusable checklist** for evaluating any symmetric card: can I use
  the effect better, can I dodge it, can I control the timing, and does it lock in a lead I already
  have? Run all four before dismissing a symmetric card as unplayable.
- **Method 3 is the one people underuse.** The right to *not cast* a card is a real form of value, and
  it means a symmetric effect in your deck is strictly optional while the same effect in the
  opponent's deck is a threat. That asymmetry is invisible on the card face.
- **The creature-count corollary is the practical deckbuilding output:** if you're playing a sweeper,
  you're also committing to deploying fewer creatures before it. Installment 11 (Board Sweepers) is
  the full treatment; this installment is where the principle is derived.
- **Cross-reference:** installment 09 notes control decks make great use of symmetric effects because
  they run few creatures — that's Method 2 (dodging) applied at the archetype level. Installment 11
  covers the sweeper case at length. Installment 10's discussion of Ari Lax boarding in End Hostilities
  despite playing creatures is Method 1.
