# Play or Draw?

**Reid Duke — Level One, March 16, 2015**

---

## The answer, up front

**The same question begins every game of Magic**, so every player ought to be able to give an educated
answer.

**The mechanic:** the player who goes first skips their first draw step; the player who goes second does
not.

**Duke gives away the conclusion immediately:**

> **To play first is to make the popular choice. If you choose to play first 100% of the time, you'll
> rarely be making a mistake. In the corner cases where you might be making a mistake, you're making a
> very, very small one. So I'll save us some time and headache: you want to play first.**

**But he tells you to keep reading**, because it matters to understand the reasons, **and even more to
understand how being on the play or the draw changes the dynamics of the rest of the game.**

## The advantages of being on the play

> **The advantages of going first are often quite large, resulting in a much higher than 50/50 chance to
> win the game if all other factors are equal.**

**1. Tempo.** Being on the play offers a **substantial tempo advantage that's typically well worth
skipping your first draw step.**

**2. Initiative.** The player going first is **much more able to take the initiative, taking the game
where they want it to go and forcing the opponent to react.** The player going second **needs to do
something quite special to take the initiative back.**

**3. An extra turn, often.** Duke's argument here is sharper than the usual version:

> **There's nothing in Magic to guarantee both players get the same number of turns. Quite the
> opposite.** The fact that the player going first has a substantial advantage **more often than not
> means the game will end on their turn, in their favor.**

> Looking at a game where one player gets six turns and the other only five, **it's easy to see the
> advantage in being on the play.**

### The advantage scales with format speed

> **The advantages of going first are magnified based on the speed and power level of the format.** As a
> game goes long they dissipate, **but if the game ends quickly they're rather massive.**

**The ratio argument:** as the total number of turns played gets smaller, **one extra turn matters
more.** The ratio of four turns to three is larger than eleven to ten.

> **Being on the play or draw is sometimes fairly unimportant in Limited, but can mean life or death in
> fast formats like Modern and Legacy.**

## The advantages of being on the draw

> **Relatively speaking, the advantages are smaller and more subtle. Always remember, you're drawing
> first, not necessarily drawing extra.**

**What it does buy:** more total resources to work with on each of your turns. **Two consequences
deserve special attention:**

**1. It's easier to make your land drops.** More cards simply means more chances to have a land.

**2. It wins topdeck races by half a turn.** When the game boils down to waiting for one player to draw
a certain card — **a creature standoff where each player waits to draw something to break the stall** —
**you'll draw what you need a half turn sooner on the draw than on the play.**

### When the draw suits you

**If your deck is prone to those issues, you take some advantage from drawing:**

- **You need to hit a lot of land drops but don't have much card advantage.**
- **You need to draw a certain card or combination before your deck gets moving.**

> **In other words, if you have a slow deck, being on the draw might be helpful.**

**And it's also better with a bad hand:**

> **If you mulligan to four, you prefer to be on the draw. If you keep a one-land hand, you prefer to be
> on the draw.** If your draw is slow and you need to hit lots of land drops before doing anything
> relevant, **you prefer to be on the draw.**

**The catch, which Duke names immediately:**

> **You don't know what each player's hand will look like until after you've chosen.**

**Where this matters by format:** in formats where decks are **faster and more consistent**, the
draw's advantages aren't likely to matter. **In Sealed Deck, where a shaky mana base and low power level
make players slower out of the gates, they could prove helpful.**

## Quantity of mana on the play and the draw

**The interesting sub-question.** On the draw you have more resources and hit land drops more easily.
**On the play you take your turns and play your lands first, perhaps getting one more total turn.**

**Duke's fabricated illustration**, oversimplifying the goal to "spend as much total mana as possible":

**Johnny, on the play:**

| Turn | Action | Total mana spent |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Plays a Mountain, taps it | **1** |
| 2 | Plays a Mountain, taps both | **3** |
| 3 | Plays a Mountain, taps all three | **6** |
| 4 | **Misses his land drop**, taps three | **9** |
| 5 | Same | **12** |

**Jenny, on the draw:**

| Turn | Action | Total mana spent |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Plays an Island | **1** |
| 2 | Plays an Island | **3** |
| 3 | Plays an Island | **6** |
| 4 | Plays an Island | **10** |

> **Except for a brief moment on Jenny's fourth turn, at every single point in the game Johnny has spent
> an equal or greater total amount of mana.**

**The conclusion:** if the game is relatively short, **the mana advantage goes to Johnny.** If it drags
on, **Jenny might catch up**, partly because drawing first gives her a better chance of hitting more
land drops.

**The variables Duke lists as complicating this:** how long the game lasts, the number of lands in each
deck, respective mulligan tendencies, and each player's ability to successfully use every available mana
every turn.

> **The question is far too complex for a blanket statement.** However, **it is fairly clear that the
> player who goes first will have the advantage in terms of mana quantity at most points throughout the
> early game.** If the game goes long, the player on the draw has a chance, but not a guarantee, of
> catching up.

**His Khans-specific note**, which shows the reasoning applied: morph, outlast, and delve, plus plenty
of other activated abilities and lands that enter tapped, **make decks hungry for a large quantity of
mana.**

> **Even slow decks that play for the late game — the ones that would normally take more advantage from
> drawing first — can make great use of an extra turn's worth of mana.** You do want the best chance of
> hitting your land drops, **but I've generally found that giving up the chance to play first is too
> high a cost.**

## A zero-sum game

**This is the argument that actually settles the question, and it's the most valuable section.**

> **A zero-sum game, also called a strictly competitive game, is played between two opponents and
> results in one winner who takes everything and one loser who gets nothing. Tournament Magic is a
> zero-sum game.**

> **What's good for one player is bad for their opponent.** If your chance to win increases by ten
> percentage points, your opponent's decreases by ten. **A positive effect on one side implies a
> negative effect on the other, and the two must always sum to zero.**

**Why this isn't obvious in practice:**

> This might seem rather obvious, but it's not always intuitive in the midst of a game. **When you're
> focused on playing your own game, it's easy to forget that causing something bad for your opponent can
> be even better than following through with your own plan.**

**The conclusion it forces:**

> **Even if you're playing a slow deck that might take some advantage from being on the draw, you should
> still usually choose to play first. If your opponent has a fast deck, you need to take the play away
> from your opponent.**

**And the diagnostic:**

> **If Johnny chooses to play first in game two, and Jenny chooses to draw first in game three, then one
> of the two players has almost certainly made a mistake.** It's impossible for both players to benefit
> equally from Johnny going first.

## Choosing to draw

> **This is the real reason it's correct to play first in such an overwhelming majority of cases. Being
> on the play has the potential to yield quite a large advantage under the right circumstances, but
> being on the draw can only ever yield a relatively small advantage.**

> **Therefore, if either player has the potential to benefit from being on the play, both that player
> and the opponent should choose to play first.** And if you're unsure, **it's far, far safer to choose
> to play first.**

### The four conditions

**All of the following must be true for drawing to be correct:**

1. **The chances of either player achieving a substantial long-term advantage via an early-stage tempo
   advantage are low.**
2. **One or both players need a lot of resources to operate** — many land drops for expensive spells, or
   demanding colored-mana requirements.
3. **Total quantity of mana is not a limiting factor in either player's game plan.** Duke flags the
   distinction explicitly: **drawing first helps you make land drops, but playing first gives you an
   extra turn with which to spend your mana.**
4. **The game is likely to go long.**

**How often that happens:**

> **Regularly in Sealed Deck** (though not always, and perhaps not even a majority of the time),
> **occasionally in Booster Draft**, and **almost never in Constructed.** I have chosen to draw first in
> Constructed before, **but only under the most extreme and unlikely conditions.**

## The close

**If you were unsure, play first.** If you were already choosing to play first, Duke hopes he's
articulated why.

> **If you're playing a fast deck, enjoy the tempo advantage of getting ahead right away. If you're
> playing a slow deck, take that opportunity away from your opponent and make them play the game you
> want to play.**

**But as you master Limited, keep your eyes open for the times drawing is right.**

> **The advantage you stand to gain might be small, but the small advantages are what add up in Magic.**

## Notes for use

- **The practical answer is one word: play.** The value of this installment is not the answer but the
  reasoning, which turns up repeatedly elsewhere.
- **The zero-sum argument is the transferable idea and the best thing in the installment.** Denying your
  opponent an advantage can beat taking one yourself. That reframing applies far beyond the die roll —
  it's the logic behind hate-drafting, behind sideboarding to attack rather than to shore up, and behind
  Flores's principle 6 about seizing the role your opponent wants.
- **The asymmetry argument is what makes "always play" safe:** the upside of the play is large and the
  upside of the draw is small, so under uncertainty you take the option with the bigger tail. That's a
  decision-theory point, not a Magic point.
- **The four conditions are a genuine checklist**, and worth noting how demanding they are — all four,
  simultaneously. That's why the exception is rare.
- **Cross-reference:** "break serve" from installment 06 and installment 17 is the same phenomenon seen
  from the draw side. The tempo argument depends on installment 05. The mana-quantity table is a
  concrete version of Eric Taylor's claim in
  `block-2-core-theory-paired/04-its-about-time-1997-08-11.md` that mana and time are interchangeable.
- **Duke's warning about the ratio effect** (one extra turn matters more in short games) is why this
  decision is nearly irrelevant in slow Limited formats and close to decisive in Legacy.
