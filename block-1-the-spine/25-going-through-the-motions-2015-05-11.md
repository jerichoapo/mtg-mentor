# Going Through the Motions

**Reid Duke — Level One, May 11, 2015**

> **Everything matters in Magic. Everything.**

---

## The tragic tale

Duke opens with a transcript:

> **Player A:** "I'll cast Silumgar's Command to bounce your Island and give your Dromoka Warrior
> -3/-3."
>
> **Player B:** "I'll cast Center Soul to save my Dromoka Warrior."
>
> **Player A:** "What color are you choosing with Center Soul?"
>
> **Player B:** "It doesn't matter, I guess I'll choose blue."
>
> **Player A:** "Okay, Dromoka Warrior now has protection from blue. A creature with protection from
> blue can't be enchanted by blue Auras, **so the Jeskai Runemark that had been attached to your Dromoka
> Warrior falls off.**"

**Duke was Player B.**

> I lost the game in a painful and embarrassing fashion because of that mistake. **Nonetheless, looking
> back, I'm glad it happened the way it did, because it taught me a valuable lesson. It taught me that
> every decision I make matters.**

**The precise version of the lesson:**

> **Or, to put it more precisely, you never know which of your decisions is going to matter. Therefore
> you have to behave as though each and every one of them is important.**

> I had a choice between protection from blue and protection from black. **I had to decide, and yet I
> didn't treat it as an important decision. Because of that I made it incorrectly and it cost me the
> game.**

**And the point that the Runemark was only one of several ways it could have mattered:**

- **What if the opponent was planning to attack with a black creature later that turn?** Naming black
  would have let him block safely.
- **What if the opponent could follow up with a sorcery-speed black removal spell like Rite of the
  Serpent?** Naming black would have taken that option away.

> **By treating my decision as unimportant, I'm ignoring all of these possibilities, which might be
> important to the outcome of the game.**

## The proper way to cast a spell

**Duke makes the rules point explicitly, and it's stronger than "be careful":**

> **I broke the rules when I originally cast the Center Soul. By not choosing a color, I skipped a step
> — a decision — in the resolution of my spell. This is not the proper way to cast a spell and it's
> illegal to do it this way.**

> This is not to say that what I did makes me a cheater or a villain, or that I deserve to be burned at
> the stake. **However, anyone with the goal of being a tournament player should get in the habit of
> casting their spells exactly as the rules dictate, and should insist that their opponents do the
> same.**

### The four steps

1. **Announce your modes and any alternate or additional costs.**
   - Casting a Command: *"I'll cast Silumgar's Command to bounce your Island and give your Dromoka
     Warrior -3/-3."*
   - Revealing a Dragon for **Foul-Tongue Invocation**: **put the two cards on the table
     simultaneously, or otherwise have your opponent acknowledge what you're revealing.**
   - Casting **Crater's Claws**: **announce how much mana you're spending on X.**
2. **Announce your targets.**
3. **Distribute any effects that must be distributed.** *"I'll cast Pyrotechnics to deal 1 damage to
   Dromoka Warrior, 2 damage to Herald of Dromoka, and 1 damage to you."*
4. **Pay the cost.** Usually tapping lands. **If you already had mana in your mana pool from another
   source, announce how much you're using and how much remains.** Pay any additional non-mana costs, as
   with **Collateral Damage**.

### The rule that governs all four

> **The order doesn't matter. The only important thing is that you do them in their entirety.**

> **You cannot take some of the steps of casting a spell without taking them all. You cannot take some
> of the steps, pause to see your opponent's reaction, and then take the rest.** You must cast your
> spell completely and properly **before your opponent is allowed to respond and before the game can
> continue.**

**Duke's own footnote on his example:** with **Center Soul** you actually **choose your color when the
spell resolves, not when you cast it.**

### Why you must insist your opponent does it too

> **If you react — either with an in-game response or simply with your body language — to your
> opponent's spell before they've paid all costs and made all decisions, you're giving them an
> advantage. You're allowing them to have more information for their decisions than the rules would
> otherwise allow.**

> Sometimes this won't matter. **But you never really know when it might matter. So behave as though it
> matters.**

### The worked example of it going wrong

**Player A is at 3 life.**

1. **Player B says "Pyrotechnics"** and reveals the card.
2. **Player A casts Ojutai's Command** to gain 4 life and draw a card.
3. **Player B says, "Okay, well I wanted to deal 3 damage to you and 1 to your Dromoka Warrior."** And
   **now decides which lands to tap, with the additional information that Player A is casting Ojutai's
   Command in response.**

**Duke's verdict assigns fault to both players:**

> **Player B cast their spell improperly, and Player A allowed the game to continue instead of insisting
> on going through the motions.** These two things combined to **give Player B an unfair advantage, and
> could potentially influence the outcome of the game.**

## It's not over until it's over

**This connects to the previous installment's advice against conceding and against worrying about
slowrolling.**

> **The game isn't over until one player officially loses or concedes. Until then, your decisions matter.
> You should make them precisely and carefully.**

**As Player B, casting the potentially lethal Pyrotechnics**, first consider everything that might go
wrong:

- **Could your opponent counter it?**
- **Could they gain life? How much?**
- **What happens if you don't win on the spot? How will you deal the last few points?**
- **Is there a safer way to go for the win?**

**Having decided to cast it, be precise about every decision inside the cast:**

- **Should you do it before or after you attack?**
- **How should you distribute the damage, in case something goes wrong?**
- **What lands should you tap for it?**

**As Player A, facing the lethal Pyrotechnics:**

> **First make sure your opponent has cast their spell properly. Have they distributed the damage? Have
> they tapped their lands? Only after you've confirmed this should you react, or even acknowledge that
> the spell has been cast.**

**If you have Ojutai's Command**, you'll survive — **but make sure you do so in the most favorable
way.** You know you're gaining 4 life, **but what second mode? What lands do you tap?**

> **It might seem irrelevant, but what if you draw a white instant only to find that you foolishly
> tapped all of your Plains?**

**If you don't have it**, you might be in trouble, **but don't just concede in a huff.** Survey the
board. **Do you have Anticipate in hand? Is there anything you might draw into?** The answer might be
no, **but it doesn't cost you anything to take a final look.**

## Every decision matters

**Duke's illustration of how deep this goes, on a turn-one land drop:**

> **Should I play my Island or my Polluted Delta on the first turn?** I have Silumgar's Scorn in hand,
> so I want access to two blue mana as quickly as possible. **But what if I draw Bile Blight? Do I have
> enough information to know what land I'll want to get with Polluted Delta? What changes if my opponent
> Thoughtseizes my Silumgar's Scorn? Which would be better to have in play if my opponent Thoughtseizes
> my Silumgar's Scorn and then I draw Temple of Deceit on turn two?**

> **It's unlikely that this decision will influence the outcome of the game, but it's not impossible.
> Since you don't know for sure, it's in your best interest to treat the decision with respect.**

### Which lands to tap

**The concrete habit, and the best takeaway in the installment:**

> With lots of lands in play and a cheap spell to cast — **if it's Hero's Downfall, tap your Swamps and a
> Radiant Fountain, and leave your Temple of Deceits and Dismal Backwaters untapped.**

**Two separate benefits:**

1. **Information concealment.** **Maybe you don't even have a blue spell in hand, but your opponent
   doesn't know that.** Treating the decision as unimportant **unintentionally gives away information.**
2. **Preserving real options.** **Once in a while you might be able to Anticipate into Dig Through Time,
   and Dig Through Time into another Dig Through Time** — a possibility you give up **if you needlessly
   tapped too many blue sources.**

### Sequencing

> **How to sequence your plays is another category of decisions that seems small but can often be
> important.**

> Proper sequencing is largely about managing information: **giving yourself the most information for
> your decisions, and concealing as much as possible from your opponent when they make theirs.**

> Once in a while it helps you make a better decision or leads your opponent into a worse one. **Either
> can change a loss into a win, so proper sequencing is certainly worth your effort.**

## Don't drive yourself nuts

**The necessary counterweight, and Duke gives it real weight:**

> Treat every decision, even the small ones, with respect. **That said, don't go overboard. Magic is a
> game and ought to be fun.** Even with the competitive backdrop of a tournament, **you still want to
> have fun and your opponents still want to have fun.** Moreover, **in tournaments you have additional
> pressure to play quickly so you don't run out of time.**

**Three specific warnings:**

- **Don't agonize over small decisions so much that it ruins the game for you.**
- **Don't burn yourself out. You don't want your brain fried by turn four of the first game of the
  tournament.**
- **Don't slow your pace so much that you can't finish your matches.**

**The resolution — habits, not deliberation:**

> **Each choice you face, take a quick second to make sure you're not missing anything, make the best
> decision you reasonably can, and go with it.**

> **More importantly, try to develop habits that allow you to make your small decisions slightly better
> and slightly faster** — like tapping your basic lands and keeping your dual lands untapped. **There
> will be times when you need to deviate from your habits, but with experience you'll come to identify
> and understand those times a little better.**

## Notes for use

- **The thesis is precise and worth stating exactly:** not "every decision matters" but **"you never know
  which decision will matter, so behave as though each one does."** That's a claim about uncertainty,
  not about importance, and it's what makes the discipline rational rather than obsessive.
- **The rules content is real and often skipped.** A spell must be cast completely — modes, targets,
  distribution, costs — before the opponent may respond. Partial announcement to fish for a reaction is
  illegal, not merely rude. **This is the installment most relevant to an Arena player moving to
  paper**, because the client enforces all of it silently and invisibly.
- **Insisting your opponent goes through the motions is the half people neglect.** Reacting early, even
  with body language, hands them information the rules don't entitle them to.
- **"Tap basics, keep duals untapped" is the single best habit in the course** — free, mechanical, and
  it both conceals information and preserves options. Duke's framing of habits as the way to get small
  decisions right *quickly* is the practical resolution to the time-pressure problem.
- **The closing section is unusually honest** about the cost of the discipline: mental fatigue and clock
  pressure are real, and the answer is habit formation rather than more deliberation per decision.
- **Cross-reference:** installment 20 (Sequencing) is the systematic treatment of the information half.
  Installment 24 supplies the don't-concede and slowroll arguments this builds on. Installment 01's
  "focus" is the underlying skill. For the rules interactions themselves — protection, layers, priority —
  Block 4's Cranial Insertion is the reference.
