# Mulligans Part II: Limited

**Reid Duke — Level One, June 15, 2015**

---

## Where this picks up

**Part one simplified the question and gave the baseline:**

> **Keep your hand if you have between two and five lands. Mulligan if you have zero, one, six, or seven
> lands.**

**This installment covers when to deviate from it, using Limited examples.**

---

# The two questions that always apply

> **Whatever deck you're playing, Limited or Constructed, there are two questions that should always
> factor into your mulligan decisions.**

## Question 1: How well does your deck mulligan?

> **Another way to say this is, simply, how much does it cost you to go down a card? For some decks the
> cost will be tremendous; others might be able to shrug it off.**

### Where card quantity matters most

**Card quantity — not to be confused with card quality — is the gross number of resources you have
access to.**

**High mana curves.**

> **If you want to cast Dragonlord Atarka, you're going to need to draw her, you're going to need to draw
> seven sources of mana, and you're going to have to draw everything you need to fight and survive in the
> interim. If you mulligan to five, the numbers simply won't add up — you won't have enough cards.**

**Critical-mass decks.** Duke builds a deliberately fictional deck: **twenty Mountains and forty Wild
Slashes**, winning by pointing ten Wild Slashes at the opponent. (**Decks dedicated entirely to burning
the opponent are very real.**)

**The arithmetic is the clearest demonstration of the concept in the course:**

> Drawing three Mountains and ten Wild Slashes, **you need thirteen cards to win. You'll accomplish this
> by turn six on the draw with no mulligan — not bad.**

> **However, for each mulligan you take, you'll need one extra draw step to get the number of cards you
> need.** Mulligan to five and draw five Mountains instead of three, **and now you're not winning until
> turn ten on the draw, or turn eleven on the play.**

### Where it matters least

**Low curves, or single cards with abnormally high impact.**

**Adding one-drops to the burn deck.** Put **four Zurgo Bellstriker** and **four Firedrinker Satyr** in.
**Cast one on turn one and it likely attacks uncontested three times — 6 damage, worth three Wild
Slashes.**

> **Suddenly the presence or absence of a one-drop becomes an important consideration that might
> sometimes outweigh card quantity.**

- **Unexciting hand without a creature** (four Mountains, three Wild Slashes) → **feel more comfortable
  mulliganing.**
- **Six-card hand with a one-drop** → **its impact can make up for your card-quantity disadvantage.**

**The Modern version:**

> Mulliganing to six, five, or four is incredibly costly. **But if you stick a Dark Confidant or
> Bitterblossom on turn two, the card advantage will undo your initial disadvantage. A five-card hand
> with a Dark Confidant can often be better than a mediocre seven-card hand.**

## Question 2: How much confidence do you have?

> **The second question is how necessary it is to take chances.**

**In Constructed** — mostly about the matchup.

> If the matchup is very favorable — say you're against Mono-Red and boarded in four **Drown in Sorrow**
> and six lifegain cards — **you should be relatively less inclined to keep a risky hand. Your chances are
> high and will probably remain high even if you mulligan to six.**

**In Limited** — mostly about the quality of your deck.

- **A fantastic deck with lots of premium removal and four or five bombs** → **you have an advantage over
  most opponents. Less need to keep a risky hand.**
- **A deck you're unhappy with, low power relative to your opponents** → **slightly more inclined to take
  a chance**, such as keeping a two-land hand that needs to draw more lands.

**When your opponent mulligans first:**

> **If you're on the draw and your opponent mulligans before you, you should be less inclined to keep a
> risky hand** — although **perhaps more inclined to keep a hand that's slow but safe.**

> **Your opponent can't have their best draw, so you don't need perfection in order to win, just
> something functional.**

### The skill-gap factor

> **If you feel the skill gap between you and your opponent is large, you can factor that in. It may seem
> heartless to judge someone else's playing abilities, but in the end, Magic is a game about beating the
> player across from you. It's a mistake to ignore the clues about what your opponent may or may not be
> capable of.**

- **Opponent is new, or made a long list of mistakes in game one** → **maybe don't keep a risky hand in
  game two. You know you can outplay them in a fair game.**
- **Opponent is a great player with a great deck** → **you might need something special in order to win.**

### The bias warning

> **Issues of ego and self-confidence can sometimes cloud your thinking on this question, no matter who
> you are.**

> **Something I see too often is that after a string of bad luck, a player will be more likely to keep
> bad hands instead of mulliganing. Either consciously or subconsciously, they begin to feel like they —
> or their deck — can't do any better.**

> **Keep a level head, and don't let the rush of emotions you can feel during a tournament make the
> decisions for you.**

# Deviating from Two to Five Lands in Limited

> **Mulligan decisions in Limited are more clear-cut. The games are slower and mulligans are very costly.
> For the most part, you ought to be keeping most hands that are functional.**

## Mana base: the three categories

> **When the mana base in your deck is shaky, you have to be more careful about mulliganing. With bad
> mana, there's no guarantee you'll be able to cast your spells if you go to six.**

| Category | Example | Mulligan effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Good mana** | Mostly mono-white splashing two red: **12 Plains, 4 Mountains, 1 Wind-Scarred Crag** | **A little more freedom** |
| **Normal (leaning good)** | Blue-Black: **9 Islands, 9 Swamps** | **Use the normal guidelines** |
| **Normal (leaning bad)** | U/B/r splashing Dragonlord Kolaghan and Roast: **8 Islands, 7 Swamps, 3 Mountains** | **Use the normal guidelines** |
| **Bad mana** | W/G/U in equal thirds: **6 Plains, 5 Forests, 5 Islands, 1 Evolving Wilds** | **Hard to mulligan a hand with all your colors** |

**On the bad-mana case:**

> **Frequent readers know it's important to avoid decks like this. Once in a while, though, a draft might
> go very poorly and you'll be forced to make the best of a bad situation.** With decks like these,
> **it's hard to mulligan a hand that has all your colors, since you're too likely to get a
> non-functional six-card hand.**

## One-landers and six-landers

> **You can strongly consider keeping one-landers and six-landers once you've already mulliganed. It's
> hard to get a good hand in Limited when you're down to five, so sometimes you'd rather just chance a
> risky six-card hand.**

### Keeping a six-land seven

**Only under two circumstances:**

1. **Your deck has very bad mana, but you have all of your colors.**
2. **The one spell in your hand is something very, very special.**

**What qualifies as special:**

> Your spell **needs an abnormally high impact on the game. It should reliably trade for two or more of
> your opponent's cards. It must also be cheap enough that you feel confident casting it before it's too
> late.**

**His examples:** **Crux of Fate**, **Mastery of the Unseen**, **Silumgar Assassin**.

### Keeping a one-land seven

> **You should rarely keep one-land hands in Limited.**

**The false logic he names:**

> Players are prone to **"if I draw my second land, I'll be fine."** The problem is that **the mana curve
> of a Limited deck typically stretches up to six or seven. It's rare that you'll be able to operate at
> full capacity with only two or three lands. Plus, you'll probably have to draw all of your colors
> before you can cast your spells.**

**The three requirements to keep one:**

1. **You'll almost certainly need to be on the draw.**
2. **You'll need more than one card that costs one or two mana.**
3. **You'll have to be able to do something special with the hand, to justify the risk.**

**His two worked examples:**

**A mulligan on both play and draw.** Even drawing the second land promptly (**about 50% on the first
draw step, about 75% within two**), **you have two average two-drops. The best they're likely to do is
trade with your opponent's creatures, and then you're still stuck. You need to draw three lands — two of
them Mountains — before you can do much.**

> **The risk is high, and the reward is low.**

**A keep on the draw.** Here **you already have both colors of mana and more early spells.** Once you hit
the second land, **Jeskai Sage and Anticipate help you get momentum and draw more lands. You have
card-advantage potential if you can trade off or exploit the Jeskai Sage, and you have a bomb to take
over the game if you survive a little while.**

> **With this hand, the rewards might be great enough to risk keeping on the draw.**

*(The difference between the two: the keepable hand has colors sorted, cheap action, a way to find more
lands, and a payoff worth surviving to.)*

## Mulliganing hands with two to five lands

> **In Limited I advise keeping most hands that are functional, and most hands with between two and five
> lands can be considered functional. However, there absolutely are circumstances where you should
> mulligan even though your hand has both lands and spells.**

**The simplest case — non-functional two-landers:** **two Mountains with all green cards costing 4 or
more.** An easy mulligan; **the risk is high and the reward is low.**

### Fast decks

> **A less-obvious issue arises when you're playing a fast deck.** Some decks with lots of cheap
> creatures are built to have an early advantage. **However, when they cannot press that advantage, they
> find themselves at a disadvantage in the late game due to their lack of powerful spells.**

> **In such a case, keeping a slow hand can be problematic, and you should be willing to mulligan
> slightly more.**

> On an aggressive White-Red deck, **if you keep, you're not playing to the strengths of your deck.
> You'll be behind by the time you reach the late game, and drawing a bunch of Dromoka Warriors and War
> Flares will not make it easy to claw back.**

### Facing fast decks

**Your standards also rise when the opponent is fast.**

> Your deck is slow and your opponent ran you over on turn five in game one. **Although slow, this hand
> might be fine for a normal game. However, against a very aggressive deck you're likely to be in panic
> mode by the time you reach turn four.**

> **Even if you're not dead, you won't have the luxury to hold up mana for Contradict, and you'll
> probably be forced into unfavorable blocks by the time you start casting creatures.**

**Plus a color tiebreaker:**

> **Notice also that all your lands are Islands. It's fairly likely you'll draw a Swamp by turn four, but
> the chance that you won't can be a tiebreaker in favor of mulliganing a borderline hand. Furthermore,
> you're taking away your chance to get lucky and draw a defensive black card that costs 2 or 3 mana.**

### When you need to draw both lands and spells

**The general principle, and the most useful diagnostic in the installment:**

> **For the above hand to become good, you need to draw a Swamp, you need to draw more spells —
> preferably cheap ones — and you need to do it in a narrow time frame. This is a common problem, and
> it's a good reason to mulligan a hand with between two and five lands.**

**Example 1.** A hand with a good mix of lands and spells that's still a mulligan:

> **The first problem is that you need to draw one or more Forests to cast your green spells. The second
> problem is that you need to draw multiple creatures before you can use these spells to their full
> effect. It's very hard to do both of those things at the same time.**

> **Sure, there are some sequences of draws that can turn this into a good hand, but there's also too
> much that can go wrong. You can get color-hosed, you can get mana flooded, or you can simply have a
> slow, unexciting hand.**

**Example 2.** A Red-Black deck with a small blue splash:

> **This is a case where you already have too many lands, and yet you need to draw more before you can
> cast your spells. What can you really hope for? If you could teleport a Swamp and an Island to the top
> of your deck, you could cast the spells in your hand — but then you have seven lands and only two
> spells.**

## Notes for use

- **The Wild Slash arithmetic is the best illustration of mulligan cost anywhere in the course.** Each
  mulligan costs you a full turn in a critical-mass deck. That converts an abstract "going down a card is
  bad" into a countable number, and it's why burn and ramp decks mulligan worse than anything else.
- **The two questions are the framework:** how badly does *my deck* need cards, and how badly do *I* need
  to gamble. The second is situational and includes the matchup, whether the opponent mulliganed, and
  the skill gap.
- **The "need both lands and spells in a narrow window" test is the most portable diagnostic here.** If a
  hand requires two different kinds of draw on a deadline, mulligan it, even with a fine land/spell
  ratio. Most bad keeps fail exactly this test.
- **The tilt warning is important and under-discussed:** after bad luck, players keep *worse* hands, not
  better ones, because they stop believing a better hand is available. That's the specific bias to watch
  for in yourself.
- **Note the honesty about assessing opponents.** Duke acknowledges it feels heartless and does it
  anyway, because ignoring evidence about who you're playing is a mistake. Compare installment 27, which
  warns against the opposite error — over-respecting a strong opponent's *draws*, as distinct from their
  *play*.

  half; installment 30 for the Sealed context (mulligan a six-land six-carder if you have your colors);
  installment 21 for the play/draw interaction.
